# Descartes, Meditationes de prima philosophia (AT VII): A Commentary

References are to AT VII by page and line ("AT 25,12"). The main text is the Meditations with the dedicatory letter, the preface and the synopsis; the seven sets of Objections and Replies and the geometrical exposition appended to the Second Replies are folded into the units they bear on, cited by set and AT page. Neither the 1641 nor the 1642 edition breaks the letter or the preface into paragraphs, and both mark the Meditations' paragraphs only by blank intervals; the units below follow Adam–Tannery's paragraphing for the Meditations and divide the two unparagraphed pieces by argumentative step.

Five misreadings recur in the work's reception and are guarded against throughout. The doubt of the First Meditation is a method of inquiry, not a skeptical position and not a rule for conduct (Fifth Replies, AT 350–351; Seventh Objections with Notes, AT 460). The first certainty of the Second Meditation is not drawn by syllogism from a suppressed major premise; it is grasped by a simple intuition of the mind (Second Replies, AT 140), and the Meditations never use the formula "I think, therefore I am." The Second Meditation does not assert that mind and body are really distinct: it determines what I know myself to be and leaves what I am in reality open until the Sixth (AT 8; Second Replies, AT 129; Fifth Replies, AT 357). Thought names every act of which I am immediately aware, sensing and imagining included insofar as they are seemings, not intellection alone (AT 29; Second Replies, Definition I, AT 160). And God's veracity secures the remembered conclusions of demonstrations, not the clear perception present to attention (Second Replies, AT 140, 146); on this distinction rests Descartes's answer to the charge of circularity.

## To the Most Wise and Distinguished Dean and Doctors of the Sacred Faculty of Theology of Paris

(AT 1,1–6) Descartes turns the dedication into an argument. His reason for offering the work is just, and the Faculty will find its own reason for defending it equally just once it understands his purpose, so stating that purpose is the only commendation the work needs. The letter is a bid for licensing. The first edition's title page announced an approbation of the doctors that the Faculty never gave, and the second edition dropped the claim; the bid shapes everything that follows, since the work is presented as serving aims the Church has already declared its own.

(AT 1,7–2,30) God and the soul are the chief questions that philosophy rather than theology must demonstrate. Believers may hold both by faith, but unbelievers can be brought to no religion and hardly any moral virtue until both are proved by natural reason: in this life vice is often better paid than virtue, and few would prefer the right to the useful if they neither feared God nor expected another life. The believer's reasoning—God exists because Scripture teaches it, and Scripture is to be believed because it comes from God—is legitimate for him, since faith is God's gift and the grace that moves us to believe the rest can move us to believe that God exists. Offered to an unbeliever, the same reasoning would look circular. Descartes then turns the theologians and Scripture against fideism. The doctors teach that God's existence can be proved by natural reason; Wisdom 13 holds the pagans inexcusable because, having been able to judge the world, they should more easily have found its Lord; Romans 1 calls them inexcusable too and adds that what can be known of God is manifest in them. Descartes reads that phrase as a licence for his method: whatever can be known of God can be shown by reasons drawn from nothing but our own mind. Knowledge of God is said to be easier and more certain than knowledge of created things, a ranking the Meditations will make good by placing self-knowledge and knowledge of God above knowledge of bodies. Circularity also first appears here, as the defect from which Descartes claims to free natural theology; Mersenne (Second Objections, AT 124–125) and Arnauld (Fourth Objections, AT 214) will turn the same charge against the Meditations themselves.

(AT 2,31–3,8) On the soul, Descartes invokes the Fifth Lateran Council (session 8, 1513, under Leo X). It condemned those who held that human reason shows the soul to perish with the body while faith alone holds the contrary, and it charged Christian philosophers to dissolve their arguments and prove the truth as far as they could. The target is the double-truth position of the Averroists and Alexandrists, soon to be associated above all with Pomponazzi. Citing the Council's mandate casts the Meditations as obedience to an ecclesiastical command. The claim must be read with the Synopsis, which concedes that the work does not prove immortality but only that the soul's destruction does not follow from the body's (AT 13,23–14,17). The first edition's title promised a demonstration of the soul's immortality; the second edition replaced it with the soul's distinction from the body, and when the Second Objectors complain that nothing is said of immortality, Descartes points them back to the Synopsis (Second Replies, AT 153,1–3).

(AT 3,9–28) Most of the impious, Descartes continues, refuse belief on the ground that no one has yet demonstrated God's existence or the mind's distinctness from the body. He denies the premise. Nearly all the arguments great men have offered on these questions have demonstrative force once properly understood, and he doubts that any can be found which someone has not already found. What philosophy lacks is a single careful selection of the best arguments, set out so clearly that all will agree they are demonstrations. Finally, those who know the method he has cultivated for resolving difficulties in the sciences—not new, since nothing is older than truth—have pressed him to apply it here. The disclaimer of originality is diplomatic but not empty. The proofs of the Third and Fifth Meditations have scholastic and Anselmian ancestors, as Caterus insists by setting them beside Thomas Aquinas (First Objections, AT 94–99). Descartes's real claim to novelty lies in the manner of exposition: the Second Replies will identify it with analysis, the route by which the reader rediscovers the truth as if he had found it himself (AT 155,23–156,5).

(AT 4,1–31) Everything Descartes could do is in this treatise. He has not collected every argument for the same conclusions, which is worth doing only where none is certain enough, but has pursued only the first and chief ones, and he dares to offer them as most certain and evident demonstrations; he adds that no better route lies open to human wit, excusing the boast by the necessity of the cause and the glory of God. Certainty does not guarantee accessibility. Archimedes, Apollonius and Pappus wrote proofs that everyone accepts as evident, because each step is easy taken alone and each coheres with what precedes, yet few understand them because they are long and demand an attentive reader. Descartes holds that his own proofs equal or surpass geometry in certainty, but they too are long and interdependent, and above all they require a mind free of prejudice and able to withdraw itself from the senses; few people are apt for metaphysics, as few are apt for geometry. The comparison carries a thesis about evidence: certainty belongs to the chain of reasons, not to its reception, so that the failure of many readers to be persuaded counts nothing against the proofs. The precondition named here is the task of the First Meditation, and the Second Replies call it the main labour of metaphysics, whose first notions, though as evident as the geometer's, conflict with the prejudices of the senses (AT 157,6–16).

(AT 5,1–9) Geometry and philosophy differ in a further respect. In geometry everyone assumes that nothing is written without a certain demonstration, so the unskilled err more often by approving falsehoods in order to seem to understand than by refuting truths. In philosophy, where everything is thought arguable on either side, few look for the truth, and many hunt a reputation for ability by attacking the best arguments. Descartes forecasts the reception the Meditations will meet: where disputability is presumed, the objector is rewarded. He returns to the point in the Preface (AT 9,28–10,5) and against Gassendi, whom he accuses of putting rhetoric in the place of reasons (Fifth Replies, AT 350,4–8).

(AT 5,10–6,20) Hence the request. Whatever Descartes's reasons are worth, they concern philosophy and will achieve little without the Faculty's protection, since its authority in matters of faith is second only to the councils and its judgment in philosophy is thought unrivalled. He asks three things: that the Faculty correct the work, which he does not claim to be free of error; that what is missing or insufficiently explained be supplied, by the doctors or by himself once advised; and that, when the reasons proving God's existence and the mind's distinctness from the body have been brought to full clarity, the Faculty publicly declare them demonstrations. Then all errors on these questions would soon disappear. Truth would bring the able and learned to subscribe, and authority would bring the atheists, who are smatterers rather than people of real ability, to drop their opposition and perhaps even to defend reasons that all capable minds hold to be demonstrations, lest they seem not to understand them. The wish to seem intelligent, which in philosophy breeds objection, is thus to be harnessed to assent once authority has fixed what intelligence agrees to. Descartes asks for authority not as a premise of the proofs but as a social condition of their acceptance.

## Preface to the Reader

(AT 7,1–13) The questions of God and the human mind were touched on in the Discourse on Method (1637), not to treat them fully but to sample them and learn from readers' judgments how they should later be handled. They seemed important enough to deserve more than one treatment, and the route Descartes follows is so little trodden and so remote from common use that he judged it unwise to teach it at length in a French book open to all, lest weaker minds think they ought to attempt it. Writing in Latin is a choice of audience. The route begins with universal doubt, which is dangerous to minds that cannot carry it through; Descartes concedes as much to Arnauld, comparing the doubt to fire and iron, which are kept from children but not abolished, since without them nothing firm can be established in philosophy (Fourth Replies, AT 247,8–23).

(AT 7,14–19) The Discourse had asked readers to report anything in it worth criticizing. On these questions only two objections worth noting came in, and Descartes answers them before giving his fuller account. The two he selects mark the two joints of the Meditations that will draw the heaviest fire in 1641: the passage from what I know of my essence to what my essence is, and the passage from an idea to the existence of what it represents.

(AT 7,20–8,15) The first objection runs: from the fact that the mind, turned upon itself, perceives itself to be nothing but a thinking thing, it does not follow that its nature or essence consists only in thinking, so that "only" excludes everything else that might belong to the soul's nature. Descartes replies that the Discourse meant to exclude other things not in the order of the truth of the matter (ordo ad ipsam rei veritatem), which it was not then treating, but only in the order of his perception (ordo ad meam perceptionem): he knew nothing to belong to his essence except that he was a thinking thing, a thing having in itself the faculty of thinking. He will show later how it follows from his knowing nothing else to belong to his essence that nothing else does belong to it. The distinction of orders governs the Second Meditation, which stays within the order of perception. The promise names the crux of the work, and Arnauld seizes on it: the Second Meditation, he says, leaves the question where the Discourse left it, and the proof must wait for the Sixth, where it depends on a clear knowledge of God that the Second does not yet possess (Fourth Objections, AT 198,9–199,18). Descartes answers with the notion of a complete thing: I need not know everything in the mind, only enough to understand it as a complete thing without body, and God's power to bring about separately whatever I clearly understand separately guarantees the real distinction (Fourth Replies, AT 219–227).

(AT 8,16–28) The second objection runs: from my having an idea of a thing more perfect than myself, it does not follow that the idea is more perfect than I am, still less that what it represents exists. Descartes finds an equivocation in "idea." Taken materially, as an operation of the intellect, an idea cannot be more perfect than I am. Taken objectively, as the thing represented by that operation, it can be more perfect by reason of its essence, even though it is not supposed to exist outside the intellect. How existence follows from such an idea will be shown at length. The distinction carries the causal proof of the Third Meditation, which requires that what an idea represents—its objective reality—demand a cause containing at least as much reality. Two objectors force refinements. Caterus treats objective being as an extrinsic denomination, the mere ending of the intellect's act in an object, which needs no cause; Descartes answers that objective being is the thing itself as it exists in the intellect, an imperfect mode of being that is nonetheless not nothing (First Objections, AT 92; First Replies, AT 102–103). Arnauld's question about materially false ideas makes him distinguish the idea as an operation from the idea as giving matter for error (Fourth Replies, AT 231–235).

(AT 8,29–9,18) Descartes has also seen two long writings that attacked not his reasons but his conclusions, with arguments borrowed from the atheists' commonplaces. Such arguments carry no weight with anyone who understands his reasons, and because many people are swayed more by opinions heard first than by a refutation heard later, he will not answer them here, lest he have to report them first. He says only that all the atheists' usual arguments against God's existence depend either on ascribing human affects to God or on claiming for our minds the power and wisdom to determine and comprehend what God can and should do; if we remember that our minds are finite and God infinite and incomprehensible, these arguments raise no difficulty. The incomprehensibility of the infinite becomes a condition of the idea of God in the Third Meditation, where the infinite is understood without being comprehended (AT 46,18–21); the First Replies develop the point by distinguishing understanding from comprehending and the infinite from the indefinite (AT 112,17–113,8). The ban on presuming to know God's purposes becomes the Fourth Meditation's exclusion of final causes from physics (AT 55,20–26), which Gassendi contests and Descartes defends by distinguishing what we may conjecture piously in ethics from what we may claim to know in physics (Fifth Replies, AT 375).

(AT 9,19–10,5) Having once tested public judgment, Descartes takes up the same questions again and with them the beginnings of all first philosophy, expecting no popular applause and few readers. He urges no one to read the work except those able and willing to meditate seriously with him and to withdraw the mind from the senses and from all prejudices, and he knows such readers are rare. Those who do not try to grasp the series and connection of his reasons, but quibble over single clauses, will gain little; they may find much to cavil at, but little that presses or deserves an answer. "The beginnings of all first philosophy" widens the scope beyond the two topics of the title: Descartes told Mersenne privately that the six Meditations contain all the foundations of his physics (letter of 28 January 1641, AT III 298). The demand to follow the series of reasons turns the Epistle's geometric analogy into a rule of reading. Descartes charges both Gassendi, who reports his reasons truncated or not at all (Fifth Replies, AT 353,10–19), and Bourdin, who strings together his words in a distorted order (Seventh Objections with Notes, AT 459,15–24), with breaking it.

(AT 10,6–23) Because Descartes cannot promise even good readers satisfaction at first sight, or foresee every difficulty, he will first set out in the Meditations the very thoughts by which he believes he reached certain and evident knowledge of the truth, to see whether the reasons that persuaded him will persuade others. He will then reply to the objections of men of outstanding ability and learning to whom the Meditations were sent before printing; their objections were many and varied enough that he hopes little of importance will occur to anyone that they have not touched. He asks readers not to judge the Meditations until they have read all the objections and their solutions. The Meditations present the order of discovery—what the Second Replies call analysis, as opposed to the synthetic order of definitions, postulates and axioms (AT 155,21–156,16)—and the Objections and Replies belong to the work rather than to an appendix. The Second Replies explain the choice of title in the same terms: Descartes wrote meditations rather than disputations or theorems to signal that he had business only with readers willing to consider the matter attentively with him (AT 157,17–22).

## Synopsis of the Six Following Meditations

(AT 12,1–9) The First Meditation sets out the reasons for which we can doubt all things, especially material things, so long as we have no foundations for the sciences other than those we had before. The usefulness of so great a doubt is not obvious at first, but it is very great: it frees us from all prejudices, opens the easiest way to lead the mind away from the senses, and makes it impossible for us ever again to doubt what we later find to be true. The clause "so long as we have no other foundations" makes the doubt conditional and temporary; it touches beliefs held on their old grounds, not beliefs as such. The third benefit states the strategy: a truth is certified by surviving the strongest doubt that can be raised against it. Descartes appeals to this when Hobbes accepts the doubts but complains that Plato and others had already raised them (Third Objections I, AT 171,4–16). He rehearsed them, he replies, as merely plausible reasons, partly to prepare readers' minds for considering intellectual things apart from bodily ones, partly to answer them later, and partly to show how firm the later truths are, since these metaphysical doubts cannot shake them; he could no more omit them than a medical writer could omit the description of a disease he means to teach how to cure (Third Replies, AT 171,18–172,10).

(AT 12,10–14,17) In the Second Meditation the mind, using its own freedom, supposes that none of the things whose existence it can doubt in the least exists, and notices that it cannot then fail to exist itself. This lets it distinguish easily what belongs to itself, that is, to the intellectual nature, from what belongs to body. Readers expecting arguments for immortality there are warned that Descartes tried to write nothing he could not demonstrate exactly, and so followed the geometers' order, setting out everything on which a proposition depends before concluding anything about it. The first requisite for knowing immortality is a concept of the soul as clear as possible and wholly distinct from every concept of body, which the Second Meditation supplies. Also required are the knowledge that whatever we clearly and distinctly understand is true just as we understand it, which could not be proved before the Fourth Meditation; a distinct concept of corporeal nature, formed partly in the Second and partly in the Fifth and Sixth; and the conclusion, drawn in the Sixth, that things clearly and distinctly conceived as different substances, as mind and body are, really are substances distinct from one another. The Sixth confirms this by divisibility: we understand every body as divisible and every mind as indivisible, so their natures are not only different but in a way opposite. Descartes went no further, for two reasons. These results suffice to show that the mind's destruction does not follow from the body's corruption, which is enough to give mortals hope of another life. And the premises from which immortality itself could be concluded depend on an account of the whole of physics: that all substances, things that must be created by God in order to exist, are by nature incorruptible and cease to be only if God withdraws his concurrence; that body taken in general is a substance and never perishes; but that the human body, as distinct from other bodies, consists only of a certain configuration of members and similar accidents, while the human mind is not made up of accidents but is a pure substance, which remains the same mind when it understands, wills and senses different things. The human body becomes another body as soon as the shape of some of its parts changes. So the body easily perishes, and the mind is by nature immortal. The "freedom" with which the mind makes its suppositions anticipates the Fourth Meditation, which places judgment in the will: suspension of assent is already the will's act. The order of reasons explains why the real distinction cannot appear in the Second Meditation, as Descartes repeats against the Second Objectors (Second Replies, AT 155,11–20) and Hobbes (Third Replies, AT 175,1–5). Immortality, however, stays at the level of hope. When the Second Objectors argue that God might have made the soul to last only as long as the body, Descartes concedes that he cannot refute this, since human reason cannot determine what depends on God's free will; natural reason shows only that nothing in the dissolution of a body, a mere change of shape, gives any ground to think a substance perishes (Second Replies, AT 153,4–25). The account of the human body as individuated only by configuration has a cost Arnauld presses: it seems to return to the Platonic view of a human being as a soul using a body (Fourth Objections, AT 203), which the Sixth Meditation's doctrine of union is meant to block.

(AT 14,18–15,2) The Third Meditation sets out, fully enough as Descartes thinks, his chief argument for God's existence. Because he wanted to lead readers' minds as far as possible from the senses, he used no comparisons drawn from bodily things there, so some obscurities may remain, which he hopes the replies will remove. One is how the idea of a supremely perfect being, which is in us, has so much objective reality that it can only come from a supremely perfect cause. The replies illustrate this by the idea of a very ingenious machine in the mind of an artisan: as the objective artifice of that idea must have a cause, namely the artisan's knowledge or that of someone from whom he received it, so the idea of God in us must have God himself as its cause (First Replies, AT 103,25–104,24). Objective reality is not nothing, since it needs a cause as much as the machine does. And the explanation can be borrowed but not indefinitely: knowledge passed from teacher to pupil only postpones the question of where the content first came from. The self-denying ordinance against bodily comparisons shows the Meditations' rhetoric serving their epistemology: an analogy from the senses would reinforce the very habit the Third Meditation must break.

(AT 15,3–12) The Fourth Meditation proves that everything we clearly and distinctly perceive is true and explains what falsity consists in; both must be known to secure what precedes and to understand what follows. Descartes adds in brackets that the Meditation does not treat sin, the error committed in pursuing good and evil, but only error in judging between true and false, and that it does not consider matters of faith or of the conduct of life, but only speculative truths known by the natural light. The bracket was added at Arnauld's suggestion, to forestall theological misunderstanding of a theory that locates error in the will (Fourth Objections, AT 215,15–216,9); Descartes points to it when he thanks Arnauld (Fourth Replies, AT 247,24–248,10). "To secure what precedes" does not mean that the Fourth Meditation makes the certainty of the "I am" depend on God: it establishes the general rule that clear and distinct perception is true, which the earlier Meditations used only as present evidence. The objection that a Turk would then sin by embracing Christianity, which he does not clearly perceive, is answered by the same restriction: faith rests on grace, not on clear perception of its content (Second Objections, AT 126,21–127; Second Replies, AT 147–149).

(AT 15,13–19) The Fifth Meditation explains corporeal nature in general and demonstrates God's existence by a new argument, in which difficulties may again arise that the replies will resolve; finally it shows how the certainty even of geometrical demonstrations depends on the knowledge of God. The "new argument" is the proof from the essence of a supremely perfect being, which Caterus measures against Thomas's rejection of the Anselmian argument (First Objections, AT 98–100) and Gassendi attacks by denying that existence is a perfection (Fifth Objections, AT 322–326). The dependence of geometry on God is the passage that gives rise to the charge of circularity. Descartes limits it in the Second Replies to the knowledge of conclusions recalled when we no longer attend to the reasons from which we deduced them; first principles grasped by present intuition are not what the logicians call scientific knowledge and need no such guarantee (AT 140,12–18; 146,14–28).

(AT 15,20–16,10) The Sixth Meditation distinguishes understanding from imagining and describes the marks of this distinction; proves that the mind is really distinct from the body and shows that it is nonetheless so closely joined to it that it forms one thing with it; reviews all the errors that usually arise from the senses and the means of avoiding them; and finally gives all the reasons from which the existence of material things can be concluded. Descartes does not think these reasons very useful for proving what they prove—that there is a world, that human beings have bodies, and the like, which no one of sound mind ever seriously doubted—but considering them shows that they are less firm and less clear than the reasons by which we arrive at knowledge of our mind and of God, so that the latter are the most certain and evident of all that human wit can know. Proving this alone was his aim in the Meditations, and he does not list the other questions treated in passing. The Meditations thus argue for a ranking of certainties, not for the existence of the external world. This answers in advance Gassendi's charge that no one could be persuaded Descartes really doubted bodies (Fifth Objections, AT 258,10–13): he did not doubt them in life, and the distinction between the conduct of life and the search for truth, which Descartes says he inculcated in many places, makes the doubt serious without making it practical (Fifth Replies, AT 350,22–351,11). The phrase "forms one thing with it" (unum quid componat) announces the union of mind and body, the doctrine that keeps the real distinction from turning the human being into two things.

## Meditations on First Philosophy, in Which the Existence of God and the Distinction of the Soul from the Body Are Demonstrated

### First Meditation: What Can Be Called into Doubt

(AT 17–18,3) Some years ago Descartes noticed how many falsehoods he had accepted as true in youth and how doubtful everything he had since built on them was. He concluded that everything must once in his life be overturned to its foundations and begun again from the first, if he wanted to establish anything firm and lasting in the sciences. The task seemed enormous, and he waited for an age so mature that none fitter for learning would follow; he has delayed so long that further deliberation would now be culpable. Today, having freed his mind from all cares and secured leisure, alone, he will devote himself seriously and freely to the general overthrow of his opinions. The project is foundational from its first sentence: the overthrow serves "something firm and lasting in the sciences," not the suspension of belief for its own sake. "Once in life" (semel in vita) marks the doubt as a single undertaking, not a standing attitude, which is what Descartes will insist on against Gassendi and Bourdin. The fiction of a particular day—"today," and in the Second Meditation "yesterday's meditation" (AT 23,22)—makes each Meditation an exercise in real time. The Second Replies ask readers to spend not the brief time needed to read the First Meditation but several months, or at least weeks, on what it treats before going on (AT 130,17–29). The autobiographical frame repeats the Discourse (Parts II and IV, AT VI 13–22, 31–32), now stripped of the provisional morality that there accompanied the doubt.

(AT 18,4–14) The overthrow does not require showing that all his opinions are false, which he could perhaps never do. Reason already persuades him to withhold assent from what is not completely certain and indubitable as carefully as from what is plainly false, so it will suffice to find some reason for doubt in each. Nor need he examine each opinion singly, which would be endless: since whatever is built on a foundation collapses of itself when the foundation is undermined, he will attack at once the principles on which everything he once believed rested. Doubtful and false are made equivalent with respect to assent, not with respect to belief: a ground for doubt need not show an opinion false, only that it might be. And the attack is on sources rather than propositions, so that a single ground of doubt can cover a whole class of beliefs. Descartes's own gloss is the basket of apples. Someone afraid that some of his apples are rotten tips them all out and then puts back one by one only those he finds sound; so too the person who has never philosophized rightly rejects all his opinions at once as uncertain and readmits only those he recognizes as true (Seventh Replies, AT 481,4–19). The image refutes Bourdin's charge that the method rejects truths along with falsehoods: nothing is discarded for good, only removed for inspection.

(AT 18,15–18) Whatever Descartes had accepted as most true he received from the senses or through the senses, and he has found that the senses sometimes deceive; prudence never trusts completely those who have deceived us even once. "From the senses or through the senses" divides what he has seen for himself from what he has learned by hearing from parents and teachers, as AT's note points out by referring to the Conversation with Burman (AT V 146). Testimony therefore falls with sense. The ground of doubt here is a maxim of prudence, not a proof, and Gassendi finds it weak: he has not always been deceived, so why distrust the senses always? Descartes answers that a discovered error suffices to ground doubt, because when we err we do not notice that we err; error consists precisely in its not appearing as error (Fifth Replies, AT 354,11–21).

(AT 18,19–19,7) An objection follows. Though the senses sometimes deceive about small or distant things, there are many other things, received from the same senses, that cannot be doubted at all: that I am here, sitting by the fire, wearing a winter dressing gown, handling this paper, and the like. How could I deny that these hands and this whole body are mine, unless I compared myself to the insane, whose brains are so disturbed by the vapour of black bile that they insist they are kings when they are paupers, or dressed in purple when naked, or that their heads are made of clay, or that they are pumpkins or made of glass? But they are mad, and I would seem no less mad if I took them as a model for myself. The Meditation is dialogical: a voice of common sense objects, and the meditator answers. Madness is set aside not because it is impossible that I am mad but because supposing it would disqualify the one who supposes it: the inquirer who took the madman as his example would himself be demented (demens), and his doubt would no longer be the doubt of a reasoner. The exclusion is the subject of the debate between Foucault, who read it as reason's founding exclusion of unreason, and Derrida, who argued that the dream and the evil genius then reintroduce, in a form a reasoner can adopt, all that madness had threatened. The text supports Derrida's structural point, since the very next sentence finds within sanity an experience that does everything madness was invoked to do.

(AT 19,8–22) As if I were not a man who sleeps at night and experiences in sleep everything that the insane experience awake, and sometimes less plausible things still! How often does the quiet of the night convince me of these ordinary things—that I am here, in my gown, sitting by the fire—when in fact I am lying undressed in bed. But now, the objector says, I am certainly looking at this paper with waking eyes; this head I move is not asleep; I stretch out this hand deliberately and feel it; things so distinct would not happen to someone asleep. As if I did not remember having been deceived in sleep by similar thoughts! Considering this more attentively, I see so plainly that waking can never be distinguished from sleep by certain signs that I am stunned, and this very stupor almost confirms me in thinking that I am asleep. The dream does inside sanity what madness could only do outside it: the experience is universal and the subject remains a reasoner. The conclusion is carefully weak. It is not that I am dreaming but that there are no certain signs (certis indiciis) by which to tell waking from sleep at the moment of judging, so that no particular sensory belief is certified by its content. The objector's criterion—waking experience is more distinct—is refuted by memory of dreams equally vivid, but it will return in the Sixth Meditation in a different form: waking perceptions connect by memory with the whole rest of my life, as dreams never do (AT 89,10–90,12). Hobbes grants the argument's truth and adds a conclusion Descartes does not draw here, that the phantasms we have waking are not accidents inhering in external objects (Third Objections I, AT 171,4–9).

(AT 19,23–20,14) Let us suppose, then, that we are dreaming and that these particulars are false—that we open our eyes, move our heads, stretch out our hands—and perhaps that we do not even have such hands or such a body. Still, the things seen in sleep are like painted images, which could only have been formed on the likeness of true things, so at least these general things—eyes, head, hands and the whole body—are not imaginary but true and existing things. For painters, even when they try to depict sirens and satyrs in the most unusual forms, cannot give them natures new in every respect; they only mix the limbs of different animals. Or if they invent something so new that nothing like it was ever seen, so that it is wholly fictitious and false, at least the colours from which they compose it must be true. In the same way, even if general things such as eyes, head and hands could be imaginary, we must admit that certain other things, still simpler and more universal, are true, from which, as from true colours, all the images of things in our thought, whether true or false, are formed. The regress has two steps, from composites to general kinds and from general kinds to the simplest elements, and it rests on a compositional model of imagination: the imagination can recombine but not create its elements. The model is the Regulae's doctrine of simple natures, from which all knowledge is composed (Rule XII, AT X 418–425). What the elements are "true" of is left open: the painter's colours are real colours, but whether the simple elements exist outside thought is not asserted. The premise that dream images are formed "on the likeness of true things" is itself a received opinion; the Third Meditation will expose the assumption that ideas resemble their causes (AT 38,11–40,4).

(AT 20,15–19) Of this kind seem to be corporeal nature in general and its extension; the shape of extended things; their quantity, that is, their size and number; the place in which they exist; the time through which they last; and the like. The list omits every sensible quality, though the painter's analogy spoke of colours. In applying the analogy Descartes has already sorted the simple elements into the mathematical determinations of body, so that "corporeal nature in general" is identified with extension before any argument has been given. The Fifth Meditation will recover exactly these items as the object of pure mathematics and the clear content of the idea of body (AT 63,16–64,6), and Gassendi will press the difficulty that pure mathematics treats points and lines that cannot exist in nature (Fifth Objections, AT 329).

(AT 20,20–31) Hence we may conclude that physics, astronomy, medicine and all other disciplines that depend on considering composite things are doubtful, but that arithmetic, geometry and others of the kind, which treat only the simplest and most general things and care little whether these exist in nature or not, contain something certain and indubitable. Whether I am awake or asleep, two and three added together make five, and a square has no more than four sides; it seems impossible that truths so transparent should incur any suspicion of falsity. The line between doubtful and certain sciences is drawn by their objects: composites depend on existence, simples do not. Mathematics survives the dream argument because its truths do not depend on whether their objects exist, so the dream, which attacks only judgments of existence, cannot touch them. The verdict is offered as provisional ("we may perhaps conclude"), and the next paragraph overturns it. Descartes's own physics will later escape the condemnation by being built from the simple natures alone, with extension as the essence of body.

(AT 21,1–16) Yet there is fixed in my mind an old opinion that there is a God who can do everything, and by whom I was made such as I am. How do I know he has not brought it about that there is no earth, no sky, no extended thing, no shape, no size, no place, and yet that all these seem to exist exactly as they do now? Indeed, as I sometimes judge that others go wrong about things they think they know perfectly, how do I know that he has not made me go wrong every time I add two and three, or count the sides of a square, or do something simpler still, if anything simpler can be imagined? Perhaps God did not want me deceived in this way, since he is said to be supremely good. But if it were contrary to his goodness to make me such that I am always deceived, it would seem equally foreign to it to permit me to be deceived sometimes, and that cannot be said. The doubt now reaches the simple natures, and it does so by a new route: not through the content of my ideas but through the origin of my nature. Whatever is evident to me might be evident because of how I was made. For the first time the existence of an extended world as such is called in question, along with mathematics. The hypothesis is the believer's God, presented as an "old opinion" rather than a demonstrated truth. The goodness reply is blocked by parity: God does permit occasional error, so his goodness cannot exclude constant error. This sets the Fourth Meditation's problem of reconciling error with God's goodness (AT 53,24–55,20). The Second Objectors press the theological side: Gabriel Biel, Gregory of Rimini and others held that God can lie by his absolute power, and Scripture shows him deceiving Pharaoh and the Ninevites as a physician deceives the sick for their good (Second Objections, AT 125,16–126,6). Descartes answers that he spoke not of verbal lying, which may be harmless, but of the formal malice inherent in deception, which is incompatible with supreme being (Second Replies, AT 142,14–143,17).

(AT 21,17–22,2) Some might prefer to deny so powerful a God rather than believe everything else uncertain. Descartes does not resist them: let everything said here about God be a fiction, and let them suppose that I reached my present state by fate, by chance, by a continuous chain of events, or in some other way. Since being deceived and erring seem to be imperfections, the less powerful the author they assign to my origin, the more probable it will be that I am so imperfect as to be deceived always. To these arguments I have no answer, and I am forced to admit that there is nothing among what I once believed true that I may not doubt, not out of thoughtlessness or levity but for strong and considered reasons; so I must henceforth withhold assent from these things no less carefully than from what is plainly false, if I want to find anything certain. The argument is a dilemma: whether my origin is an omnipotent God or an unguided process, I lack a guarantee of my faculties, and the atheist's alternative makes things worse, not better. Here lies the root of the thesis that an atheist geometer can know clearly that a triangle's angles equal two right angles but cannot have scientific knowledge (scientia) of it, since nothing assures him that he is not deceived even in what seems most evident (Second Replies, AT 141,3–13). Bourdin takes "strong and considered reasons" to mean that Descartes seriously doubts whether there is an earth or whether he has a body. Descartes replies that the reasons are strong only for the supreme, metaphysical and hyperbolic doubt, which is never to be carried over into life, and for which any ground of suspicion, however slight, counts as strong enough (Seventh Objections with Notes, AT 459,25–460,12).

(AT 22,3–22) Noticing this is not enough; I must take care to remember it. Habitual opinions keep returning and occupy my credulity, which they hold bound by long use and the right of familiarity, almost against my will. I will never stop assenting to them and trusting them so long as I take them for what they are: doubtful in a way, as has been shown, but highly probable, and much more reasonable to believe than to deny. So I will do well to turn my will in the opposite direction, deceive myself, and pretend for a time that they are entirely false and imaginary, until the weight of prejudice is equal on both sides and no bad habit any longer bends my judgment away from the right perception of things. No danger or error will follow, for I cannot indulge distrust too much, since I am attending now not to action but only to knowledge. Doubt alone does not dislodge belief: habit holds credulity by a quasi-legal title, and the probable keeps its grip even when shown uncertain. So the will must act as a counterweight. This presupposes the theory of judgment the Fourth Meditation will state, on which assent is an act of will that the will can withhold. Descartes defends the device to Gassendi as bending a crooked stick the other way to make it straight (Fifth Replies, AT 349,23–26), and against Bourdin he insists that treating the doubtful as false means only turning thought away from it, not believing its opposite (Seventh Objections with Notes, AT 461,1–10; 462,5–9). The final clause states the restriction that makes the whole procedure innocent: the doubt concerns the search for truth, not the conduct of life, where it would be absurd not to trust the senses (Fifth Replies, AT 350,22–351,11; Fourth Replies, AT 248,1–10).

(AT 22,23–23,18) I will suppose, then, not that God, who is supremely good and the source of truth, but that some evil genius, supremely powerful and cunning, has employed all his industry in deceiving me. I will think that the sky, the air, the earth, colours, shapes, sounds and all external things are nothing but delusions of dreams by which he lays snares for my credulity; I will regard myself as having no hands, no eyes, no flesh, no blood, no senses, but as falsely believing I have all these. I will stay obstinately fixed in this meditation, and so, if it is not in my power to know anything true, at least it is in my power not to assent to falsehoods, and with a resolute mind I will take care that this deceiver, however powerful and cunning, cannot impose anything on me. But the undertaking is laborious, and a kind of laziness brings me back to ordinary life. Like a prisoner who enjoyed an imaginary freedom in his sleep and, when he begins to suspect he is asleep, fears to wake and goes along with the pleasant illusions, I slide back of my own accord into my old opinions and fear to wake up, lest the peaceful rest give way to laborious waking spent not in any light but among the inextricable darkness of the difficulties just raised. The evil genius is not a new ground of doubt. The logical work was done by the hypothesis of an omnipotent deceiver and its atheist alternative; the genius is the counterweight the previous paragraph called for, a supposition vivid enough to hold the will in place, and it relieves the hypothesis of ascribing malice to God. Its scope as stated covers external things and my body, not arithmetic, and when the Third Meditation recalls the doubt about two and three it returns to "some God" (AT 36,7–15). The residue the paragraph secures is practical, not theoretical: before anything is known, it is in my power not to assent. The freedom to suspend judgment is the first thing the meditator finds he possesses, and the Fourth Meditation will make it the mark of the will's infinity. The closing image reverses the dream argument: here the sleeper fears waking, and truth is imagined as a darkness worse than illusion. Gassendi calls the whole apparatus a recourse to machinery and trickery unworthy of philosophical candour (Fifth Objections, AT 258,13–18). Descartes answers that philosophers, like astronomers who imagine the equator and the zodiac and geometers who add lines to figures, often assume falsehoods usefully in order to bring out the truth (Fifth Replies, AT 349,26–350,8).

### Second Meditation: The Nature of the Human Mind, and That It Is Better Known than the Body

(AT 23,22–24,13) Yesterday's meditation has thrown Descartes into doubts so great that he can neither forget them nor see how to resolve them; like someone who has suddenly fallen into a deep whirlpool, he can neither touch bottom nor swim to the surface. He will struggle on along the same road, removing everything that admits the least doubt exactly as if he had found it to be false, until he knows something certain, or, if nothing else, at least knows for certain that nothing is certain. Archimedes asked only for one firm and immovable point in order to move the whole earth; great things may be hoped for if Descartes finds even the least thing that is certain and unshakable. The method is not abandoned at the moment of crisis but applied again. Its minimal goal—to know for certain that nothing is certain—would already be a certainty, and so cannot be the Academic outcome it resembles; the formulation anticipates the reflexive structure of the first truth. The Archimedean point states the logic of foundations: in an order of reasons, one indubitable item is enough to begin, because everything else is to be derived from it.

(AT 24,14–18) I suppose, then, that everything I see is false; I believe that nothing my lying memory represents ever existed; I have no senses at all; body, shape, extension, motion and place are chimeras. What then will be true? Perhaps only this, that nothing is certain. Memory falls under the supposition for the first time, and this matters for what follows: the certainty about to be found must be one that needs no memory, and the Second Replies will confine God's guarantee to remembered conclusions for exactly this reason (AT 140,12–18). The simple natures of the First Meditation—extension, shape, place—are now listed as chimeras, so the doubt reaches the very elements the painter's analogy had spared.

(AT 24,19–25,13) But how do I know there is not something different from all these, about which there is not the slightest occasion for doubt? Is there not some God, or whatever I may call him, who puts these very thoughts into me? But why should I think so, when I myself may perhaps be their author? Am I not, then, at least something? But I have already denied that I have any senses or any body. Yet I hesitate, for what follows? Am I so bound to body and senses that I cannot exist without them? But I persuaded myself that there is nothing at all in the world—no sky, no earth, no minds, no bodies; did I not then persuade myself that I do not exist? No: if I persuaded myself of something, then certainly I existed. But there is some deceiver, supremely powerful and supremely cunning, who is deliberately deceiving me all the time. Then without doubt I exist too, if he deceives me; and let him deceive me as much as he can, he will never bring it about that I am nothing so long as I think that I am something. So, after weighing everything enough and more than enough, I must finally conclude that this proposition, "I am, I exist," is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind. The certainty is reached through three trials, each of which turns the doubt into evidence for the doubter: the thoughts may come from God, but then I may be their author; I may depend on a body I have denied, but my denying requires me; I may be deceived, but deception requires someone deceived. The formula is "I am, I exist," not "I think, therefore I am," and its necessity is tied to the occasion of its thinking ("whenever," quoties): it is not a necessary truth about me in general but one that cannot be false while I think it. Descartes denies that it is inferred by syllogism, since then the major premise, "whatever thinks exists," would have to be known first, whereas we learn the general proposition from experiencing in our own case that we cannot think without existing (Second Replies, AT 140,18–141,2). Hobbes accepts the step, on the ground that what thinks is not nothing (Third Objections II, AT 172,12–16). Gassendi objects that existence could have been inferred from any other action. Descartes answers that he is certain of no action but thought: "I walk, therefore I am" holds only insofar as the awareness of walking is a thought, since in dreams I seem to walk when no body moves (Fifth Objections, AT 259,1–4; Fifth Replies, AT 352,6–18). The Sixth Objectors raise a regress: to know that I think I must know what thinking and existence are, and know that I know this, and so on. Descartes replies that what suffices is the inner knowledge (cognitio interna) of thought and existence that always precedes reflective knowledge and is innate in everyone (Sixth Objections, AT 413,1–11; Sixth Replies, AT 422,6–22). Arnauld notes Augustine's anticipation—if you did not exist, you could not be deceived (De libero arbitrio II.3)—and Descartes accepts the authority in a sentence without discussing it (Fourth Objections, AT 197,24–198,8; Fourth Replies, AT 219,6–8).

(AT 25,14–24) But I do not yet understand well enough who I am, I who now necessarily exist, and I must take care not to put something else imprudently in my place and so go astray even in the knowledge I claim to be the most certain and evident of all. So I will consider again what I once believed myself to be before I fell into these thoughts, and subtract from it everything that could be weakened in the least by the reasons just given, so that what remains at the end is precisely and only what is certain and unshakable. Existence is known before essence. The meditator knows that he is before he knows what he is, and the method for the second question is subtraction: take the old self-conception and remove whatever the doubt can touch. The danger named—putting something else in place of myself—is the one the objectors will press, that the "I" thus known might in fact be a body. The procedure answers it only within the order of knowledge: what survives subtraction is what I know myself to be, which is not yet all that I may be.

(AT 25,25–26,23) What, then, did I formerly think I was? A man. But what is a man? Shall I say a rational animal? No, for then I would have to ask what an animal is and what rational is, and so slide from one question into many harder ones; I have no time to waste on such subtleties. I will rather attend to what came into my mind of its own accord and under nature's guidance whenever I considered what I was. First, that I had a face, hands, arms and this whole machine of limbs, such as is seen even in a corpse, which I called the body. Next, that I was nourished, walked, sensed and thought, actions I attributed to the soul. What this soul was I either did not notice or imagined as something tenuous, like wind or fire or ether, diffused through my grosser parts. About the body I had no doubt at all and thought I knew its nature distinctly. Had I tried to describe it, I would have said: by body I understand whatever can be bounded by some shape and circumscribed by a place, and can fill a space so as to exclude every other body from it; can be perceived by touch, sight, hearing, taste or smell; and can be moved in many ways, not by itself but by something else that touches it. For I judged that the power of moving itself, and likewise of sensing or thinking, did not belong to the nature of body at all; I rather wondered that such faculties were found in certain bodies. The scholastic definition is refused not as false but as a method: defining by genus and difference explains the obscure by the more obscure. The meditator turns instead to the spontaneous self-conception, and finds that it already contains a kind of dualism: a body known as an inert machine, "such as is seen even in a corpse," and a soul whose functions span the Aristotelian range from nutrition to thought and which is imagined as a subtle body. The common notion of body includes sensibility and passive motion, and the "wonder" that some bodies sense and move themselves registers the mechanist denial of active powers in matter before any argument for it. Descartes later insists that he reported this conception from common opinion in order to correct it, and that he himself refers nutrition to the body alone and walking and sensing mostly to the body, attributing to the soul nothing but thought (Fifth Replies, AT 351,12–27). He traces the confusion to the word "soul," which the first men used both for the principle of nutrition and for the principle of thought; he therefore uses "mind" for the latter, understood not as a part of the soul but as the whole soul that thinks (Fifth Replies, AT 355,27–356,22).

(AT 26,24–27,17) But what am I now, when I suppose that some supremely powerful and, if it is permissible to say so, malicious deceiver has deliberately tried in every way to deceive me? Can I affirm that I have even the least of the things I said belong to the nature of body? I attend, I think, I go over them again: nothing occurs to me, and I tire of repeating the same thing in vain. What of the things I attributed to the soul? Being nourished or walking? Since I now have no body, these too are mere fictions. Sensing? This too does not happen without a body, and in sleep I seemed to sense many things that I later realized I had not sensed. Thinking? Here I find it: thought exists; it alone cannot be torn from me. I am, I exist; that is certain. But for how long? For as long as I think; for it might perhaps happen that if I ceased entirely to think, I would at once cease entirely to be. I now admit nothing except what is necessarily true; I am, then, precisely only a thing that thinks, that is, a mind, or soul, or intellect, or reason—words whose meaning I did not know before. I am a true thing and truly existing; but what kind of thing? I have said: a thinking thing. The subtraction runs through the soul's functions in Aristotelian order and stops at thought, which alone cannot be separated from the "I" because doubting it is itself thinking. Sensing is excluded here as a bodily process; it will be readmitted below as a mode of thought. The temporal limit—"for as long as I think"—binds the certainty of existence to the present act, and the speculation that I might cease to be if I ceased to think seeds the later thesis that the mind always thinks. Descartes defends that thesis to Gassendi: a thinking substance must always think, and we do not remember the thoughts of the womb or of lethargy because memory requires traces in the brain that an infant's or a lethargic's brain cannot receive (Fifth Replies, AT 356,23–357,6). "Precisely" (praecise) marks abstraction within the order of knowledge, not exclusion in reality (Second Replies, AT 129,6–18), and "words whose meaning I did not know before" empties the inherited terms so that they mean exactly "thinking thing" and no more. Bourdin's claim that Descartes asked whether he was a mind and triumphantly answered yes is rejected on this ground (Seventh Objections with Notes, AT 491,11–25). Hobbes objects that "I am thinking, therefore I am thought or intellect" is no better than "I am walking, therefore I am a walk" (Third Objections II, AT 172,16–21). Descartes answers that he used the four terms for things endowed with the faculty of thinking, not for faculties; that "walking" signifies only an act, while "thought" can signify the act, the faculty, or the thing that has the faculty; and that he chose the most abstract words available to signify a substance stripped of everything not belonging to it, as Hobbes chooses the most concrete—subject, matter, body—to keep it from being separated from body (Third Replies, AT 174,4–24).

(AT 27,18–28,19) What else am I? I will use my imagination. I am not that assemblage of limbs called the human body; nor am I some thin air diffused through these limbs, nor wind, nor fire, nor vapour, nor breath, nor anything I can picture, for I have supposed all these to be nothing. The supposition stands, and still I am something. Yet perhaps these very things that I suppose to be nothing, because they are unknown to me, are in reality not different from the "I" that I know? I do not know, and I am not arguing about that now; I can judge only of what is known to me. I know that I exist; I ask who this "I" is that I know. It is quite certain that knowledge of it, taken precisely so, does not depend on things whose existence I do not yet know, and therefore not on any of the things I fashion in imagination. And the word "fashion" (effingo) shows my mistake: I would indeed be fashioning something if I imagined myself to be anything, since imagining is simply contemplating the shape or image of a bodily thing. I now know for certain that I exist and also that all such images, and in general whatever relates to the nature of body, could be mere dreams. So it seems no less foolish to say "I will imagine, in order to recognize more distinctly who I am" than to say "I am now awake and see something true, but since I do not yet see it clearly enough, I will deliberately go to sleep so that my dreams may represent it more truly and evidently." I therefore recognize that nothing I can grasp with the imagination belongs to the knowledge I have of myself, and the mind must be turned away from such things with the greatest care if it is to perceive its own nature as distinctly as possible. The reservation—"perhaps these things are in reality not different from the I that I know"—leaves the ontological question explicitly open, and Descartes cites it against every objector who accuses him of assuming the real distinction: the Second Objectors (Second Replies, AT 129,10–18), Gassendi (Fifth Replies, AT 357,7–20) and Bourdin (Seventh Objections with Notes, AT 491,5–10). Arnauld, by contrast, locates exactly here the gap the Sixth Meditation must close (Fourth Objections, AT 198,20–199,18). The distinction on which the passage turns is stated to Gassendi: belonging to me is one thing, belonging to the knowledge I have of myself another (Fifth Replies, AT 357,21–28). When the Second Objectors ask how Descartes proves that body cannot think, he answers that the question belongs to the Sixth Meditation, and that the opinion that parts of the brain concur in forming thoughts arises not from any positive reason but from our never having been without a body, as someone fettered from infancy might think his fetters part of his body and necessary for walking (Second Objections, AT 122,24–123,6; Second Replies, AT 131,19–22; 133,5–16). The definition of imagining as contemplating a bodily shape prepares the Sixth Meditation's contrast between imagining and understanding a chiliagon. Hobbes's charge that Descartes never explains how imagining differs from conceiving receives that contrast as its answer (Third Objections IV, AT 178,1–4; Third Replies, AT 178,19–24). The dream simile turns the First Meditation against the imagination: to seek self-knowledge in images is to go to sleep in order to see better.

(AT 28,20–22) What then am I? A thinking thing. What is that? A thing that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, wills, refuses, and also imagines and senses. The definition proceeds by enumeration of acts, not by genus and difference. Intellectual acts come first, then acts of will, then imagining and sensing—admitted as modes of thought immediately after imagination has been excluded as a source of self-knowledge. The geometrical exposition appended to the Second Replies gives the corresponding definition: by "thought" Descartes means everything in us of which we are immediately aware, so that all operations of will, intellect, imagination and the senses are thoughts, while "immediately" excludes their consequences, such as voluntary movement, which has thought as its principle but is not itself thought (AT 160,7–13). To Hobbes he names the common feature that unites these acts: they all fall under the common concept of thought, perception or consciousness, as figure and motion fall under that of extension, and we name substances by the acts we know them through before asking whether the names pick out one thing or two (Third Replies, AT 176,1–29).

(AT 28,23–29,18) That is no small list, if all of it belongs to me. And why should it not? Am I not the very one who now doubts nearly everything, who nonetheless understands something, who affirms this one thing to be true and denies the rest, who wants to know more, who does not want to be deceived, who imagines many things even against my will, and who notices many things as coming from the senses? Even if I am always asleep, even if my creator is deceiving me as much as he can, which of these is not as true as that I exist? Which of them can be distinguished from my thought? Which can be said to be separate from me? That it is I who doubt, understand and will is so evident that nothing could make it plainer. But I am also the same one who imagines: even if, as I supposed, no imagined thing is true, the power of imagining really exists and is part of my thought. Finally, I am the same one who senses, that is, who notices bodily things as if through the senses: I now see light, hear a noise, feel heat. These are false, since I am asleep. But I certainly seem to see, to hear, to be warmed. This cannot be false; this is what is properly called sensing in me; and taken precisely so, it is nothing other than thinking. All the modes belong to one and the same "I" (idem ego): the subject is not a collection of acts but the one that doubts, imagines and senses. Hobbes reads the question "what is distinguished from my thought?" as an identification of the thinker with his thinking. Descartes answers that he distinguishes himself from his thought as a thing from its mode, and that the question concerns the modes just listed, none of which is separable from him (Third Objections III, AT 177,1–13; Third Replies, AT 177,15–21). And sensing is recovered as seeming. "I seem to see" is immune to the dream because it claims nothing about the eye or the object, only the presence of an appearance. Descartes later makes this precise by distinguishing three grades of sense: the motion of the bodily organ; the immediate result in the mind, such as the perception of colour or pain, which arises from the mind's union with the body; and the judgments about size, distance and shape that habit makes so quick we ascribe them to sense, though they belong to the intellect (Sixth Replies, AT 436,26–438,11). The "seeming" of this paragraph is the second grade considered as thought, abstracted from its bodily cause. Against Gassendi's taunt that a mind without eyes or hands cannot see or touch wax, Descartes replies that he spoke here not of seeing and touching by means of organs but of the thought of seeing and touching, which dreams show needs no organs (Fifth Replies, AT 360,20–27).

(AT 29,19–30,2) From this I begin to know somewhat better who I am. But it still seems, and I cannot help thinking, that bodily things, whose images are formed by thought and which the senses themselves examine, are known much more distinctly than this unknown part of me that does not fall under the imagination. It is strange that things I notice to be doubtful, unknown and foreign to me should be grasped more distinctly than what is true and known, than I myself. But I see what is happening: my mind enjoys wandering and will not yet let itself be confined within the limits of truth. So be it; let us give it the loosest rein once more, so that when the reins are drawn in at the right moment it will more readily allow itself to be ruled. The resistance is reported, not refuted: the felt distinctness of bodies outweighs the demonstrated priority of the self, because familiarity passes for clarity. The concession is a tactic, borrowed from the training of horses: the wax will be examined on the ground where the imagination feels strongest, so that it is defeated on its own terms.

(AT 30,3–25) Let us consider the things commonly thought to be grasped most distinctly of all: bodies, the ones we touch and see. Not bodies in general, for general perceptions are usually rather more confused, but one in particular. Take this wax. It has just been taken from the honeycomb; it has not yet lost all the taste of its honey; it keeps some of the scent of the flowers from which it was gathered; its colour, shape and size are plain to see; it is hard, cold, easily handled, and if you strike it with a knuckle it makes a sound. It has everything that seems required for a body to be known as distinctly as possible. But while I am speaking it is put near the fire: what is left of the taste is purged, the smell goes, the colour changes, the shape is lost, the size increases; it becomes liquid and hot, can hardly be touched, and no longer makes a sound when struck. Does the same wax remain? It must be admitted that it does; no one denies it, no one thinks otherwise. What was it, then, in the wax that was grasped so distinctly? Certainly none of the things I reached with the senses, for whatever came under taste, smell, sight, touch or hearing has now changed, and the wax remains. The test is run on the imagination's best case, a single body present to all five senses, and the description runs through the senses one by one before the fire changes every quality. The datum the argument rests on is not a sensation but a judgment of identity—the same wax remains—which no one disputes and no sense supplies. Hobbes draws an Aristotelian moral, that substance is not perceived by the senses but gathered by reasoning (Third Objections IV, AT 178,1–6). Descartes resists the generalization: he proved by the wax only that colour, hardness and shape do not belong to the formal concept of the wax, and he did not treat the formal concept of mind or of body there (Third Replies, AT 175,15–21).

(AT 30,26–31,28) Perhaps the wax was what I now think: not the sweetness of honey, the fragrance of flowers, the whiteness, the shape or the sound, but a body that a little earlier appeared to me in these ways and now appears in others. But what exactly is it that I imagine in this way? Let us attend, remove whatever does not belong to the wax, and see what is left: nothing but something extended, flexible and changeable. What is this flexible and changeable thing? Is it what I imagine when I picture the wax changing from round to square or from square to triangular? Not at all, for I understand that it is capable of innumerable changes of this kind, and I cannot run through innumerable changes in imagination, so this understanding is not achieved by the faculty of imagining. What is "extended"? Is the extension itself unknown? It increases when the wax melts, increases further when it boils, and further still if the heat grows; I would not be judging correctly what the wax is unless I thought it capable of more variations in extension than I have ever taken in by imagining. I must therefore admit that I do not even imagine what this wax is, but perceive it by the mind alone—I mean this particular wax, for the point is still clearer about wax in general. But what is this wax that is perceived only by the mind? It is the same wax I see, touch and imagine, the same I thought it was from the start. And yet—this must be noted—the perception of it is not sight, not touch, not imagination, and never was, though it seemed so before, but an inspection of the mind alone (solius mentis inspectio), which can be imperfect and confused, as it was before, or clear and distinct, as it is now, according as I attend less or more to the things of which it consists. The residue—extended, flexible, changeable—names determinables, not determinations, and the decisive argument is modal. To grasp the wax as capable of innumerable shapes and sizes is to grasp a range of possibilities, and no finite run of images can exhaust it; so what I understand of the wax outruns what I can imagine. The conclusion is not that there are two waxes, one sensed and one understood, but that one wax has always been perceived by the mind, more or less clearly. Sense experience itself turns out to have contained an inspection of the mind that was merely confused. Gassendi charges that Descartes abstracted the concept of the wax from the concept of its accidents. Descartes replies that he meant rather to show how the wax's substance is manifested through its accidents, and how a reflective and distinct perception of it differs from the ordinary confused one (Fifth Replies, AT 359,3–10). The passage does not yet establish that extension is the essence of body: the Synopsis assigns the distinct concept of corporeal nature partly to this Meditation and partly to the Fifth and Sixth (AT 13,13–15).

(AT 31,29–32,12) Meanwhile I am amazed at how prone my mind is to error. Even when I consider these things silently, I get caught up in words and am almost deceived by ordinary ways of speaking. We say that we see the wax itself, if it is there, not that we judge it to be there from its colour or shape. From this I might at once conclude that the wax is known by the eye's vision and not by the inspection of the mind alone—had I not happened to look out of the window at people passing in the street, whom I also say I see, just as I say I see the wax. Yet what do I see except hats and coats, which could conceal automata? I judge that they are men. So what I thought I saw with my eyes I in fact grasp by the faculty of judging alone, which is in my mind. Ordinary language carries the prejudice that perception is sight, and the example that breaks it is chosen with care. The passers-by are not another body like the wax but other human beings, so that perception of other minds falls under the same analysis: what is seen is clothing, what is judged is a man. The automata point back to the Discourse's tests for telling a machine from a man (Part V, AT VI 56–59). The "faculty of judging" plays here the role the previous paragraph gave to the mind's inspection; the Sixth Replies will assign such judgments to the third grade of sense and to the intellect (AT 437,20–438,11).

(AT 32,13–28) Someone who wants to know more than the crowd should be ashamed to seek grounds for doubt in the crowd's ways of speaking. Let us go on and consider whether I perceived what the wax was more perfectly and evidently when I first looked at it and believed I knew it by the external sense itself, or at least by what is called the common sense, that is, the imaginative power, or whether I perceive it better now, after investigating more carefully both what it is and how it is known. It would be foolish to doubt the answer. What was distinct in the first perception? What was there that any animal could not seem to have? But when I distinguish the wax from its external forms and consider it naked, as if its clothes had been taken off, then, though there may still be error in my judgment, I cannot perceive it in this way without a human mind. The identification of the common sense with the imaginative power collapses the scholastic inner senses into a single bodily faculty, as the Regulae had already done (Rule XII, AT X 413–415). The criterion of the first perception—something any animal might seem to have—shows that the confused perception of bodies is the part of perception that men share with animals, while the distinct perception requires a human mind. Gassendi objects that a dog judges in the same way when it recognizes its master by his clothes (Fifth Objections, AT 272,1–6). Descartes answers that he observes no mind in a dog, and that the meditating mind can experience its own thinking but can learn whether animals think only afterwards, from their operations (Fifth Replies, AT 358,5–10; 359,10–16). The concession "there may still be error in my judgment" keeps the order of reasons: clarity is a property of the perception, but its truth is not yet guaranteed, and the wax may not exist at all. The clothing image answers the hats and coats of the previous paragraph. In the passers-by the clothes conceal a man; in the wax the sensible forms are the clothes, and the mind sees the body underneath.

(AT 33,1–29) But what am I to say about this mind itself, about myself? For as yet I admit nothing in me except mind. What, I say, of this "I" that seems to perceive this wax so distinctly? Do I not know myself not only far more truly and certainly but also far more distinctly and evidently? For if I judge that the wax exists because I see it, it follows much more evidently that I myself exist because I see it. It may be that what I see is not really wax; it may be that I have no eyes with which to see anything; but it cannot be that, when I see, or (I do not now distinguish the two) when I think I see, I who think am not something. The same follows if I judge that the wax exists because I touch it, or imagine it, or for any other reason, and the same holds of everything outside me. Further, if my perception of the wax seemed more distinct once it became known to me not by sight or touch alone but from many sources, how much more distinctly I must now be admitted to know myself, since every consideration that can help in perceiving the wax or any other body proves the nature of my mind even better! And there are so many other things in the mind itself by which knowledge of it can be made more distinct that what flows into it from the body hardly seems worth counting. Every judgment that a body exists, whatever its ground, entails that I exist, but no judgment about myself entails that a body exists; this asymmetry makes the mind more certain than any body. Its greater distinctness needs a further argument. Gassendi complains that the wax shows only that I exist, not what I am, and asks for something like a chemist's analysis of the mind (Fifth Objections, AT 275–277). Descartes replies that a substance is known only through its attributes, and the more attributes we know the better we know its nature; for every attribute I know in the wax there is a corresponding power in the mind that knows it—to know its whiteness, its hardness, its melting—so that no thing has as many known attributes as the mind, and its nature is the best known of all (Fifth Replies, AT 359,17–360,19). Descartes declines to distinguish seeing from thinking that one sees because the argument does not need the distinction: whether seeing is a bodily process or a thought, it entails a thinker.

(AT 33,30–34,9) I have now come back of my own accord to where I wanted to be. Since I now know that bodies themselves are perceived not, strictly speaking, by the senses or the faculty of imagining but by the intellect alone, and that they are perceived not because they are touched or seen but only because they are understood, I recognize plainly that nothing can be perceived by me more easily or more evidently than my own mind. But since an old habit of opinion cannot be laid aside so quickly, I will stop here, so that this new knowledge may be fixed more deeply in my memory by long meditation. The title's thesis is now stated: the mind is "better known" than the body. The Second Replies define the comparative: we know a thing better the more of its properties we perceive, as we know people we have lived with better than those whose faces we have only seen, and in this sense the mind considered apart from what belongs to body is better known than body considered apart from mind (AT 129,25–130,5). The conclusion generalizes from the wax to all bodies, which are perceived by the intellect alone; the Third Meditation's opening, closing the eyes and stopping the ears, starts from this result (AT 34,12–18). The pause for memory is pedagogy, not evidence: memory here fixes a habit and guarantees nothing. Descartes explains to the Second Objectors why the Second Meditation treats nothing else. No one before him had shown how the mind is to be led away from the senses, and the true way, the only one in his judgment, is contained in this Meditation; but it must be gone over and repeated often, so that a few days' contrary habit can undo a lifetime's habit of confusing intellectual with bodily things (Second Replies, AT 130,30–131,18).

### Third Meditation: Of God, That He Exists

(AT 34,12–35,2) I will now close my eyes, stop my ears and turn away all my senses; I will even blot out from my thought all images of bodily things or, since that is hardly possible, regard them as empty and false and count them as nothing. Talking with myself alone and looking more deeply into myself, I will try to make myself gradually better known and more familiar to myself. I am a thinking thing, that is, a thing that doubts, affirms, denies, understands a few things, is ignorant of many, wills, refuses, and also imagines and senses. For, as I noticed before, even if what I sense or imagine is perhaps nothing outside me, I am certain that the modes of thinking I call sensations and imaginations, insofar as they are only modes of thinking, are in me. The Meditation opens by enacting the Second's result: if bodies are known by the intellect alone, the intellect can turn from the senses without losing anything it needs. The list of modes adds two items to the Second Meditation's—"understands a few things, is ignorant of many"—which announce the theme of finitude on which the proof of God will turn. Sensations and imaginations are kept as modes of thinking, abstracted from their objects, so that the inventory of ideas the Meditation is about to take has a secure starting point.

(AT 35,3–15) In these few words I have listed everything I truly know, or at least everything I have so far noticed that I know. Now I will look around more carefully to see whether there may be other things in me that I have not yet considered. I am certain that I am a thinking thing. Do I not then also know what is required for me to be certain of anything? In this first item of knowledge there is nothing but a clear and distinct perception of what I affirm; this would not be enough to make me certain of the truth of the matter if it could ever happen that something I perceived so clearly and distinctly was false. So I now seem able to lay it down as a general rule that whatever I perceive very clearly and distinctly is true. The rule is extracted from the case: the first certainty is examined for what made it certain, and clarity and distinctness is the only feature found. The rule is stated hesitantly ("I seem able"), and the sentence names the condition under which it would fail—that a clear and distinct perception could be false—which the next two paragraphs show has not yet been excluded. Gassendi objects that great minds who must have perceived much clearly still held that truth lies hidden in God or at the bottom of a well, and that what is needed is not the rule but a method for telling when we really perceive clearly. Descartes replies that the meditating mind is not supposed to know that other people ever existed, so their authority is out of place, and that he did supply the method, by first removing all prejudices and then sorting clear ideas from obscure and confused ones (Fifth Replies, AT 361,8–362,3).

(AT 35,16–29) But I previously accepted as wholly certain and evident many things that I later found to be doubtful. What were they? The earth, the sky, the stars and everything else I took in with the senses. What did I perceive clearly about them? Only that ideas or thoughts of such things appeared to my mind; and even now I do not deny that these ideas are in me. But there was something else I used to affirm, and which, through a habit of belief, I thought I perceived clearly, though I did not really perceive it: that there were things outside me from which these ideas came and which they wholly resembled. On this I was either mistaken or, if I judged truly, it was not by the force of my perception. The paragraph separates what was perceived from what was added. The clear content of sensory belief was only the presence of ideas; the external cause and the resemblance were supplied by judgment out of habit, and habit counterfeits clarity. Even a true judgment of this kind would have been true by luck, not by the force of perception. The Second Replies go further: every belief we have ever had in things outside the mind rests solely on the principle that the objective reality of ideas must exist formally or eminently in their causes, since we suspected those things existed only because their ideas reached the mind through the senses (AT 135,19–26; 165,13–27).

(AT 35,30–36,29) What about the very simple and easy things I considered in arithmetic and geometry, such as that two and three make five? Did I not see them clearly enough to affirm them as true? My only reason for later judging them doubtful was that it occurred to me that some God could have given me a nature such that I was deceived even about what seemed most evident. Whenever this preconceived opinion of God's supreme power occurs to me, I must admit that it would be easy for him, if he wished, to make me err even in what I think I see most evidently with the eye of the mind. But whenever I turn to the things themselves that I think I perceive very clearly, I am so convinced by them that I spontaneously break out: let whoever can deceive me, he will never bring it about that I am nothing while I think I am something, or that it should ever be true that I never existed, since it is now true that I exist, or perhaps that two and three make more or less than five, or anything else in which I recognize a manifest contradiction. And since I have no reason to think there is a deceiving God, and do not yet even know whether there is any God, the reason for doubting that depends only on this opinion is very slight and, so to speak, metaphysical. To remove even this, I must examine, as soon as I can, whether there is a God and, if there is, whether he can be a deceiver; for without knowing this, it seems I can never be quite certain of anything else. The paragraph states the structure the charge of circularity misses. Two attitudes alternate. Attending to the clear things themselves, I cannot doubt them; considering in general the possibility of a deceiving God, I can. The doubt is real only in the second attitude, and it is "very slight" and "metaphysical" because it rests on an unexamined opinion. God is needed to remove the doubt that arises when I am not attending, not to supply the evidence present when I am. This is how Descartes answers Arnauld's charge of a circle—that clear and distinct perception is guaranteed only by God, while God is known only by clear and distinct perception. He distinguishes what we actually perceive clearly from what we remember having perceived clearly: the proof of God is certain while we attend to its reasons, and afterwards God's veracity lets memory of a clear perception suffice (Fourth Objections, AT 214,7–15; Fourth Replies, AT 245,25–246,9; Second Replies, AT 140,12–18; 146,14–28). The examples of what I cannot doubt while attending include the first certainty itself and an eternal truth about the past ("it will never be true that I never existed"), which links the first certainty to the indestructibility of the past.

(AT 36,30–37,12) Order now seems to require that I first divide my thoughts into kinds and ask in which of them truth or falsity properly lies. Some are, as it were, images of things, and to these alone the name "idea" properly applies, as when I think of a man, a chimera, the sky, an angel or God. Others have other forms besides: when I will, fear, affirm or deny, I always apprehend something as the subject of my thought, but my thought embraces something more than the likeness of that thing. Of these, some are called volitions or emotions, others judgments. The classification is functional. An idea is a thought considered as representing; volitions and judgments add an attitude toward what is represented. "As it were images" (tanquam imagines) must be read with care, since God and an angel are on the list and have no images. Hobbes takes "idea" to mean a picture in the corporeal imagination and concludes that we have no idea of God or angels (Third Objections V, AT 179,13–180,8). Descartes answers that he uses "idea" for whatever is immediately perceived by the mind, borrowing the term because philosophers already used it for the forms of perception in the divine mind, where there is no imagination (Third Replies, AT 181,1–14). When Hobbes adds that fear is only the idea of the lion plus a bodily effect, and that affirmation is only the doubling of names, Descartes replies that it is self-evident that seeing a lion and fearing it are different, and that affirming that one sees a man running is different from seeing him, and needs no words (Third Objections VI, AT 182,1–20; Third Replies, AT 182,21–183,2).

(AT 37,13–28) Ideas considered in themselves alone, and not referred to anything else, cannot strictly be false, since whether I imagine a goat or a chimera, it is equally true that I imagine one. Nor is there falsity to fear in the will or the emotions, for even if I desire wicked things or things that exist nowhere, it is still true that I desire them. So only judgments remain in which I must take care not to be deceived. And the chief and most frequent error in them consists in my judging that the ideas in me resemble or conform to things outside me; for if I considered ideas only as modes of my thought and did not refer them to anything else, they could hardly give me any matter for error. Truth and falsity are located in judgment, which refers an idea to something outside it. Two points prepare later doctrine. The qualification "hardly" (vix) leaves room for the material falsity introduced six pages later, so the two passages are consistent. And the chief error named here, the resemblance judgment, is what the Sixth Meditation will correct when it allows that bodies cause our sensory ideas without allowing that they resemble them (AT 80,4–10; 83,1–23).

(AT 37,29–38,10) Of these ideas, some seem to be innate, some adventitious, and some made by me. My understanding what a thing is, what truth is, what thought is, seems to come from nothing but my own nature. My hearing a noise now, seeing the sun or feeling the fire I have so far judged to come from things outside me. Sirens, hippogriffs and the like I make up myself. Or perhaps I could think them all adventitious, or all innate, or all made, since I have not yet clearly seen their true origin. The threefold division is offered as appearance only ("seem"), and the next sentence suspends it. The examples of innate ideas—thing, truth, thought—are the most general notions, implicit in any act of thinking; they anticipate the claim that the idea of God is innate like the idea of myself (AT 51,6–14). Gassendi argues that all ideas are adventitious, since even the chimera is composed of parts received from outside. Descartes answers that by this reasoning Praxiteles made no statues because he did not make his marble: the form of a chimera lies in the composition, not in the parts (Fifth Replies, AT 362,4–18).

(AT 38,11–22) The main question concerns the ideas I regard as taken from things existing outside me: what reason makes me think they resemble those things? I seem to have been taught so by nature; and I find that they do not depend on my will and so not on me, since they often come to me against my will—now, whether I want to or not, I feel heat, and so I think this sensation or idea of heat comes to me from something other than me, namely the heat of the fire I am sitting by; and nothing seems more obvious than to judge that this thing sends me its own likeness rather than something else. Three grounds are isolated: a natural teaching, the involuntariness of sensory ideas, and the apparent obviousness of resemblance. The first two will be examined in turn; the third, which is really the conclusion the others are meant to support, falls with them.

(AT 38,23–39,5) Are these reasons firm enough? When I say I have been taught so by nature, I mean only that a certain spontaneous impulse leads me to believe it, not that some natural light shows me it is true. The two are very different. Whatever the natural light shows me—for instance, that from my doubting it follows that I exist—cannot be doubted at all, since there can be no other faculty I trust as much as that light, and none that could teach me that what it shows is not true. But as for natural impulses, I have often judged in the past that they pushed me towards the worse when it was a question of choosing the good, and I do not see why I should trust them more in anything else. The distinction between the natural light (lumen naturale) and natural impulse (impetus naturalis) is the methodological hinge of the Meditation: the causal principle will be accepted precisely because it is shown by the natural light. Its example—"from my doubting it follows that I exist"—is a rare place where Descartes states the first certainty as an inference, which shows that "follows" here names a connection seen by intuition, not a syllogism. Impulses are disqualified by their record in practical life. The Sixth Meditation will rehabilitate "what nature teaches" in a narrowed sense, as the teaching of the composite of mind and body about what is beneficial or harmful, not about the nature of bodies (AT 80,27–83,23).

(AT 39,6–14) Next, although these ideas do not depend on my will, it does not follow that they must come from things outside me. Just as the impulses I have been speaking of seem different from my will though they are in me, so there may perhaps be some other faculty in me, not yet well enough known to me, that produces these ideas, just as it has always seemed to me that they are formed in me without any help from external things when I dream. The argument from involuntariness fails because involuntariness shows only independence from the will, not independence from me. The dream supplies the counterexample. The unknown faculty is left as a bare possibility. The Sixth Meditation will close it off by showing that the passive faculty of sensing requires an active faculty that cannot be in me, since it presupposes no act of understanding and produces ideas against my will (AT 79,6–31).

(AT 39,15–29) Finally, even if these ideas did come from things different from me, it would not follow that they must resemble those things. In many cases I seem to have noticed a great difference. For example, I find in myself two different ideas of the sun. One is, as it were, drawn from the senses and belongs above all to those I consider adventitious; through it the sun appears very small to me. The other is taken from astronomical reasoning, that is, drawn from certain notions innate in me or made by me in some other way; through it the sun is shown to be several times larger than the earth. Both cannot resemble the same sun existing outside me, and reason persuades me that the one that seems to have come most directly from the sun is the one that least resembles it. The two suns break the link between causal origin and resemblance: the idea most directly caused by the object is the least like it. The astronomical idea is called innate or made, which shows that "innate" covers ideas elicited by reasoning from innate notions. Hobbes objects that there is only one idea of the sun at a time, and that astronomy does not produce a second idea but shows that the sensory idea is deceptive. Descartes answers that what Hobbes describes is exactly what he calls an idea (Third Objections VIII, AT 184,1–16). Against Gassendi's claim that both ideas are one because they refer to one sun, he replies that this is like saying true and false do not differ because they are affirmed of the same subject (Fifth Replies, AT 363,19–364,2).

(AT 39,30–40,4) All this shows well enough that until now I have believed that there are things distinct from me which send me ideas or images of themselves through the sense organs or in some other way, not from certain judgment but only from a blind impulse. The verdict is harsh but limited: it concerns the grounds of the belief, not its truth. The belief in an external world is left standing as an opinion without a foundation, which is the condition the next paragraph sets out to remedy.

(AT 40,5–20) But another way occurs to me of inquiring whether any of the things whose ideas are in me exist outside me. Insofar as these ideas are only modes of thinking, I recognize no inequality among them, and they all seem to come from me in the same way. But insofar as one represents one thing and another another, they plainly differ greatly from one another. Those that show me substances are something more and contain in themselves, so to speak, more objective reality than those that represent only modes or accidents; and again, the idea by which I understand a supreme God, eternal, infinite, omniscient, omnipotent and creator of all things other than himself, certainly has more objective reality in it than those through which finite substances are shown. The new way turns from the origin of ideas to their content. Taken as acts, all ideas are equal; taken as representations, they differ in degree of objective reality according to the degree of reality of what they represent—mode, finite substance, infinite substance. The geometrical exposition defines objective reality as the being of the thing represented insofar as it is in the idea (Second Replies, Definition III, AT 161,4–9), and lays down degrees of reality as an axiom (Axiom VI, AT 165,28–166,2). Hobbes asks whether reality admits more and less and denies that we have an idea of substance at all, since substance is known only by reasoning. Descartes answers that whatever is known by reasoning is also what he calls an idea, and that it is self-evident that a substance is more of a thing than a mode, an incomplete substance more than a mode but less than a complete one, and an infinite independent substance more than a finite dependent one (Third Objections IX, AT 185,5–17; Third Replies, AT 185,18–28).

(AT 40,21–41,29) It is manifest by the natural light that there must be at least as much reality in the efficient and total cause as in its effect. For where could the effect get its reality if not from its cause? And how could the cause give it unless it also had it? It follows that something cannot come from nothing, and also that what is more perfect, that is, contains more reality, cannot come from what is less perfect. This is plainly true not only of effects whose reality is actual or formal but also of ideas, in which only objective reality is considered. A stone that did not exist before cannot begin to exist unless it is produced by something containing, formally or eminently, everything that is in the stone; heat cannot be produced in a subject that was not hot before except by something of at least as perfect an order as heat; and likewise the idea of heat or of a stone cannot be in me unless it was put there by some cause containing at least as much reality as I conceive to be in the heat or the stone. Although that cause transfers none of its actual or formal reality into my idea, it should not be thought less real on that account; the nature of an idea is such that it requires no formal reality of its own except what it borrows from my thought, of which it is a mode. But that this idea contains this or that objective reality rather than another must come from some cause in which there is at least as much formal reality as the idea contains objectively. For if we suppose that something is found in the idea that was not in its cause, it would have it from nothing; and however imperfect this mode of being by which a thing is objectively in the intellect through an idea, it is certainly not nothing, and so cannot come from nothing. The principle is presented as a corollary of "nothing comes from nothing," and its extension to ideas is the decisive step. The idea as an act needs only my thought as its cause; the idea as a content needs a cause adequate to what it represents, because representational content is a way of being and so falls under the principle that being does not arise from nothing. "Formally or eminently" admits causes that contain a perfection not in kind but in a higher degree, able to supply its place (Second Replies, Definition IV, AT 161,10–13). The principle concerns efficient causes. Gassendi reads it as a principle about material causes, which only receive form; Descartes replies that the perfection of a form can pre-exist only in an efficient cause (Fifth Replies, AT 366,4–8). Caterus's denial that objective being needs a cause, already met in the Preface, is answered by the machine: one may rightly ask what causes the ingenious design in an idea, and "the intellect" is no answer, since the question concerns not the operation but the design it contains (First Replies, AT 103,13–104,15). The Second Objectors' flies, bred by sun, rain and earth though alive, are dismissed as both a question about sensible things, out of place for a mind withdrawn from the senses, and no counterexample, since we do not know that sun, rain and earth are the adequate causes (Second Replies, AT 133,27–134,18).

(AT 41,30–42,15) Nor should I suspect that, since the reality I consider in my ideas is only objective, the same reality need not be formally in their causes, but that it is enough for it to be there objectively. Just as the objective mode of being belongs to ideas by their nature, so the formal mode of being belongs to the causes of ideas, at least the first and principal ones, by their nature. And though one idea may perhaps arise from another, there can be no infinite regress here; we must finally reach a first idea whose cause is like an archetype, in which all the reality that is in the idea only objectively is contained formally. So it is clear to me by the natural light that ideas are in me like images, which can easily fall short of the perfection of the things from which they were taken but cannot contain anything greater or more perfect. The paragraph blocks the escape that an idea might be explained by another idea. Ideas can derive from ideas—copies from copies, a pupil's knowledge from his teacher's—but the chain must end in something whose being is formal, because objective being is derivative by nature. The comparison of ideas to images here concerns their relation to their causes, not their pictorial character. The principle of the archetype answers the Second Objectors' suggestion that Descartes received the idea of God from books and conversations: the same question can be put to those from whom he received it, and whoever first had it must have had it from God (Second Replies, AT 136,11–18; Fifth Replies, AT 364,18–23).

(AT 42,16–28) The longer and more carefully I examine all this, the more clearly and distinctly I recognize it to be true. What then shall I conclude? That if the objective reality of some idea of mine is so great that I am certain it is not in me either formally or eminently, and so that I cannot myself be its cause, it necessarily follows that I am not alone in the world, but that some other thing that is the cause of this idea also exists. If no such idea is found in me, I will have no argument at all to make me certain of the existence of anything other than myself, for I have searched most carefully and have so far found no other. The conclusion takes the form of a test. Every idea must be checked for whether its objective reality could be contained in me, and only an idea that fails the test can break the solitude of the cogito. The phrase "I am not alone in the world" shows what is at stake: the proof of God is also the first escape from solipsism. The renewed reflection ("the longer I examine it, the more clearly I see it") is a reminder that the causal principle is held by present attention, as the circle discussion requires.

(AT 42,29–43,4) Besides the idea that shows me myself, which raises no difficulty here, I have others that represent God, bodily and inanimate things, angels, animals and finally other human beings like me. The idea of myself "raises no difficulty" because I am its cause and its object, so its objective reality is plainly contained in me formally. The list is ordered for the elimination that follows: it begins with God and ends with other people, and the next paragraphs will dispose of every item except the first.

(AT 43,5–9) As for the ideas that show other men, or animals, or angels, I easily understand that they could be composed from the ideas I have of myself, of bodily things and of God, even if there were no men besides me, no animals and no angels in the world. The ideas of intermediate beings are composites of ideas the meditator already has, so they cannot prove that anything exists. The Second Objectors' remark that the idea of an angel need not be caused by an angel, though angels are more perfect than we are, is accepted for this reason: the idea is composed from ideas of God and of man (Second Replies, AT 138,26–139,4). It follows that the existence of other people cannot be established by their ideas alone; the Meditations never return to the question.

(AT 43,10–44,8) As for the ideas of bodily things, nothing in them seems so great that it could not have come from me. If I look into them more closely and examine each as I examined the idea of the wax yesterday, I notice that there are very few things in them I perceive clearly and distinctly: size, or extension in length, breadth and depth; shape, which arises from the boundaries of this extension; position, which shaped things have in relation to one another; and motion, or change of position; to which may be added substance, duration and number. Other things, such as light and colours, sounds, smells, tastes, heat and cold and other tactile qualities, I think of only very confusedly and obscurely, so that I do not even know whether they are true or false, that is, whether the ideas I have of them are ideas of things or of non-things. For though I noted a little earlier that falsity in the strict sense, formal falsity, can be found only in judgments, there is another, material, falsity in ideas when they represent a non-thing as a thing. For example, my ideas of heat and cold are so unclear and indistinct that I cannot learn from them whether cold is only the absence of heat, or heat the absence of cold, or whether both are real qualities, or neither. And since there can be no ideas that are not, as it were, of things, if it is true that cold is nothing but the absence of heat, the idea that represents it to me as something real and positive will rightly be called false, and so with the rest. The wax analysis now yields its harvest: the clear content of the ideas of bodies is geometrical and kinematic, and sensible qualities are confused. Material falsity is introduced to describe ideas whose confusion lets them present what may be a privation as a positive quality. Arnauld objects that this collapses idea into judgment: an idea is the thing as it is objectively in the intellect, so if cold is a privation there can be no positive idea of it, and if the idea is positive it is a true idea of something else; moreover, a positive objective being caused by nothing would destroy Descartes's own causal principle (Fourth Objections, AT 206,5–207,24). Descartes replies that ideas of sense are often referred to something other than what they are really ideas of, and that he calls them materially false only because their obscurity gives occasion to judge that what they show is positive outside my sense when it may be a privation. Their positive being is the sensation itself, and only their obscurity, not anything positive, comes from nothing. The ideas that give most matter for error are those of sensory appetite, such as the dropsical patient's thirst (Fourth Replies, AT 232,7–234,23).

(AT 44,9–17) I need not assign to these ideas any author other than myself. If they are false, that is, represent no things, I know by the natural light that they come from nothing, that is, they are in me only because something is lacking in my nature, which is not wholly perfect. If they are true, they still show me so little reality that I cannot even distinguish it from a non-thing, so I see no reason why they could not come from me. The dilemma eliminates the sensory qualities from the proof either way: false ideas need no positive cause, and true ones contain too little reality to need one outside me. "They come from nothing" means that their defect is explained by my imperfection, not that their being is uncaused, which is the point Arnauld's objection forces Descartes to make explicit.

(AT 44,18–45,8) Of the things that are clear and distinct in my ideas of bodies, I seem able to have borrowed some from the idea of myself: substance, duration, number and the like. When I think that a stone is a substance, a thing capable of existing by itself, and also that I am a substance, then even though I conceive myself as thinking and not extended and the stone as extended and not thinking, so that the two concepts differ greatly, they seem to agree in the concept of substance. Likewise, when I perceive that I exist now and remember that I existed for some time before, and when I have various thoughts whose number I understand, I acquire the ideas of duration and number, which I can then transfer to anything else. As for everything else from which the ideas of bodily things are composed—extension, shape, position and motion—these are not contained in me formally, since I am only a thinking thing; but because they are only modes of a substance and I am a substance, they seem able to be contained in me eminently. The common notions—substance, duration, number—are drawn from self-knowledge and transferred to bodies, a remarkable inversion of empiricist genetics. The definition of substance as "a thing capable of existing by itself" anticipates the Principles; to Hobbes and Gassendi, who deny that we have an idea of substance at all, Descartes replies that substance is perceived by the intellect, not by the imagination, and that accidents borrow whatever reality they are said to have from the idea of substance, not the reverse (Fifth Replies, AT 364,3–17). The claim that extension could be in me eminently keeps open, for now, the possibility that I produce the ideas of bodies myself; the Sixth Meditation closes it (AT 79,6–80,4). Gassendi argues that the mind cannot produce ideas in itself, since nothing acts on itself, as a finger does not strike itself. Descartes answers that a spinning top acts on itself, and adds that he never claimed the ideas of material things come from the mind, only that nothing in them is so great as to prove they could not (Fifth Replies, AT 366,21–367,18).

(AT 45,9–18) So there remains only the idea of God, in which I must consider whether there is anything that could not have come from me. By the name "God" I understand a substance that is infinite, independent, supremely intelligent, supremely powerful, and by which both I myself and everything else that exists, if anything else does, was created. All these attributes are such that the more carefully I attend to them, the less it seems they could have come from me alone. So, from what has been said, it must be concluded that God necessarily exists. The elimination has left one idea, and its content is given as a definition. Each attribute is a contrary of what the meditator has just found in himself: infinite against finite, independent against the dependence the second proof will establish, supremely intelligent against "understanding a few things." The geometrical exposition defines God as the substance we understand to be supremely perfect and in which we conceive nothing that implies any defect or limitation (Second Replies, Definition VIII, AT 162). Hobbes claims that each attribute comes from external objects or names our own limits: "infinite" means that I cannot imagine its end, "omnipotent" is inferred from memory of past actions (Third Objections X, AT 186,10–187,28). Descartes answers that nothing in God can come from external things as from a model, since nothing in God resembles bodies, and that the idea of divine understanding is formed by extending indefinitely the idea of understanding each of us has; creation follows from the idea itself, since it is incompatible with a God so powerful that anything else exist without being created by him (Third Replies, AT 188,5–27).

(AT 45,19–22) For though the idea of substance is in me because I am a substance, it would not be the idea of an infinite substance, since I am finite, unless it came from some substance that really is infinite. The paragraph isolates exactly what in the idea of God exceeds me. "Substance" I can supply from myself, but "infinite" I cannot, because the eminent containment that worked for extension (a mode of substance) does not work for infinity, which exceeds every finite substance.

(AT 45,23–46,4) I must not think that I perceive the infinite not by a true idea but only by negating the finite, as I perceive rest and darkness by negating motion and light. On the contrary, I clearly understand that there is more reality in an infinite substance than in a finite one, and so that in some way my perception of the infinite, that is of God, is prior to my perception of the finite, that is of myself. For how could I understand that I doubt and desire, that is, that I lack something and am not wholly perfect, if there were no idea in me of a more perfect being by comparison with which I recognize my defects? The infinite is positive and the finite its limitation, so the order of concepts inverts the order of discovery: I found myself first, but I could understand myself as finite only through an idea of the infinite already in me. Doubt, which opened the inquiry, turns out to presuppose the idea of God, since doubting is a way of lacking. Hobbes and Gassendi say that "infinite" signifies only the negation of limits; Descartes replies that it is false that the infinite is understood by negating limitation, since every limitation contains a negation of the infinite (Fifth Replies, AT 365,6–7). The First Replies refine the point: infinity as such, the formal concept, we understand only negatively, by noticing no limitation in the thing, but the thing that is infinite we understand positively, though not adequately (AT 113,9–17).

(AT 46,5–28) Nor can it be said that this idea of God is perhaps materially false and so could come from nothing, as I noticed just now about the ideas of heat and cold. On the contrary, since it is supremely clear and distinct and contains more objective reality than any other, no idea is more true in itself or less open to the suspicion of falsity. This idea of a supremely perfect and infinite being is, I say, supremely true; for though it might perhaps be imagined that such a being does not exist, it cannot be imagined that its idea shows me nothing real, as I said of the idea of cold. It is also supremely clear and distinct, since whatever I clearly and distinctly perceive that is real and true and implies some perfection is wholly contained in it. Nor does it matter that I do not comprehend the infinite, or that there are countless other things in God that I can neither comprehend nor perhaps even reach in thought; for it belongs to the nature of the infinite not to be comprehended by me, who am finite. It is enough that I understand this and judge that all the things I clearly perceive and know to imply some perfection, and perhaps countless others I do not know, are in God formally or eminently, for the idea I have of him to be the truest, clearest and most distinct of all my ideas. The idea of God is the contrary of a materially false idea: it cannot present a non-thing as a thing because its content is reality itself, and its clarity is the sum of every clear perfection I have. The distinction between understanding and comprehending makes incomprehensibility part of the idea's content rather than a defect in it. The First Replies give the image: we see the sea without taking in its vastness, and from close by, looking at part of it, see that part clearly; so the mind can understand the infinite clearly without encompassing it (AT 113,17–114,10). To Gassendi, who says that we know at most a tiny part of the infinite, as a hair's portrait represents a whole man, Descartes answers that the idea represents the whole infinite in the way a human idea can, as someone ignorant of geometry has the idea of the whole triangle when he understands a figure bounded by three lines (Fifth Replies, AT 367,19–368,19). To Hobbes's appeal to the Christian teaching that God is inconceivable, Descartes replies that this concerns adequate comprehension (Third Replies, AT 189,17–21). The idolaters' confused ideas of gods, Descartes concedes to Arnauld, can be called materially false, since they furnish matter for false judgment; the clear idea of the true God cannot be referred to anything it does not fit (Fourth Replies, AT 233,15–21).

(AT 46,29–47,8) But perhaps I am something greater than I understand, and all the perfections I attribute to God are in me potentially, though they do not yet show themselves or become actual. For I already experience my knowledge gradually increasing, and I see nothing to prevent its increasing more and more to infinity, nor why, with knowledge so increased, I could not acquire by it all the other perfections of God, nor finally why the potential for these perfections, if it is already in me, should not be enough to produce their idea. The objection proposes that the idea of God is my own idealized future. It is the strongest alternative to the proof, since it explains the idea's content by a real feature of the mind, its capacity to grow, rather than by another being. The Second Objectors give it its classic form: from the degree of perfection I find in myself I can add degree to degree without end and so form the idea of a perfect being, as I can imagine endless degrees of heat or light from one degree (Second Objections, AT 123,7–24).

(AT 47,9–23) But none of this can be so. First, even if it is true that my knowledge increases gradually and that much is in me potentially that is not yet actual, none of this belongs to the idea of God, in which nothing at all is potential; indeed, gradual increase is the surest sign of imperfection. Further, even if my knowledge always increases, I understand that it will never thereby become actually infinite, since it will never reach a point at which it is incapable of further increase; whereas I judge God to be actually infinite, so that nothing can be added to his perfection. Finally, I perceive that the objective being of an idea cannot be produced by merely potential being, which strictly speaking is nothing, but only by actual or formal being. Three replies, each decisive alone. The idea of God contains no potentiality, so a potential being cannot be its model. Indefinite growth never yields actual infinity, so the indefinite is not the infinite, a distinction the First Replies draw explicitly (AT 113,1–8). And potentiality cannot cause, since the cause of objective reality must be actual. To the Second Objectors Descartes adds that the very faculty of amplifying every created perfection, of conceiving something greater, would not be in us unless the idea of something greater, God, were already in us (Second Replies, AT 133,17–26; Fifth Replies, AT 365,8–19; 370,19–371,3). He also denies that the idea is built by successive amplification: it is grasped whole, by the mind's reaching an infinite being incapable of any increase, as the idea of a triangle does not grow when we discover new properties (Fifth Replies, AT 371,8–26).

(AT 47,24–48,2) There is nothing in all this that is not manifest by the natural light to anyone who attends carefully. But when I attend less, and the images of sensible things blind the eye of the mind, I do not so easily remember why the idea of a being more perfect than I must come from a being that really is more perfect. So I would like to inquire further whether I myself, who have this idea, could exist if no such being existed. The second proof is introduced as a concession to inattention, not as a new demonstration. The First Replies say so expressly: Descartes asked whether he could exist if God did not "not so much to give a different reason as to explain the same one more completely" (AT 106,1–4), and the Second Replies call it a more palpable proof for those whose natural light is weak (AT 136,4–9). The geometrical exposition nonetheless presents the two as separate propositions (Propositions II and III, AT 167,10–168,26).

(AT 48,3–6) From what, then, would I derive my existence? From myself, or from my parents, or from some other things less perfect than God; for nothing more perfect than God, or even as perfect, can be thought or imagined. The candidates for the cause of my existence are exhaustively enumerated: myself, my parents, other lesser causes, or God, since nothing equal or greater can be conceived. The proof will eliminate each in turn.

(AT 48,7–24) If I derived my existence from myself, I would not doubt or desire, and I would lack nothing at all; for I would have given myself all the perfections of which I have any idea, and so I myself would be God. I must not suppose that what I lack might be harder to acquire than what I already have. On the contrary, it is manifest that it was far harder for me, a thinking thing or substance, to emerge from nothing than to acquire knowledge of the many things I do not know, which are only accidents of that substance. And certainly, if I had the greater from myself, I would not have denied myself at least what is easier to have, nor any of the other things I perceive to be contained in the idea of God, since none of them seems harder to do; and if any were harder, they would certainly seem harder to me, if I had from myself the rest that I have, since I would experience my power as limited in them. The argument combines two principles the geometrical exposition sets out as axioms: whoever can do the greater can do the lesser, and creating or conserving a substance is greater than creating its attributes (Second Replies, Axioms VIII–IX, AT 166,8–12); and the will tends infallibly towards a good clearly known, so a being that could give itself perfections would give them (Axiom VII, AT 166,3–7). Self-creation is the hardest thing; a being able to create itself could give itself omniscience. The last sentence answers the objection that some perfections might be beyond even a self-creating being: if they were, I would experience my power as limited there, and that experience of limit would itself show I am not self-caused.

(AT 48,25–49,11) Nor do I escape the force of these reasons by supposing that perhaps I have always been as I am now, as if it followed that no author of my existence need be sought. For the whole time of my life can be divided into innumerable parts, each of which in no way depends on the others, so from my having existed a little while ago it does not follow that I must exist now, unless some cause as it were creates me afresh at this moment, that is, preserves me. It is clear to anyone who attends to the nature of time that the same power and action are needed to preserve anything at each moment it lasts as would be needed to create it anew if it did not yet exist; so that preservation differs from creation only in thought, and this too is one of the things manifest by the natural light. The doctrine of continuous creation removes the hypothesis of an eternal, uncaused self, and it turns the proof from the past to the present: what needs explaining is not how I began but how I continue. Time is treated as composed of mutually independent parts, a thesis the geometrical exposition lays down as an axiom (Axiom II, AT 165,4–6). Gassendi objects that we have a power to persevere unless a destructive cause intervenes, and that the parts of time follow one another necessarily. Descartes answers that the question concerns not time in the abstract but the duration of an enduring thing, any moment of which can be separated from the next; and that Gassendi gives the creature the creator's perfection of persisting independently, and the creator the creature's imperfection of having to act positively to annihilate (Fifth Replies, AT 369,14–370,11). Builders and fathers are causes of their effects' coming to be, the sun and God causes of their being, which therefore must act continuously (Fifth Replies, AT 369,17–25).

(AT 49,12–20) So I must now ask myself whether I have some power by which I can bring it about that I, who now exist, will still exist a little later. For since I am nothing but a thinking thing—or at least, since I am now dealing precisely only with that part of me which is a thinking thing—if any such power were in me, I would certainly be aware of it. But I experience none, and from this I know most evidently that I depend on some being distinct from me. The inference from the absence of awareness to the absence of the power relies on the thesis that nothing can be in the mind of which it is not aware. The parenthetical qualification ("or at least…") is the Second Meditation's reservation again: the question concerns the thinking thing precisely taken. Arnauld challenges the thesis with the infant's mind, which has the power of thinking in the womb without being aware of it. Descartes restricts it: we are always actually aware of the mind's acts or operations, but of its faculties or powers only potentially, in that as soon as we set about using a faculty that is in the mind we become actually aware of it (Fourth Objections, AT 214,14–22; Fourth Replies, AT 246,9–247,2). The restriction is enough for the proof, since a power of self-preservation would have to be exercised at every moment and so would be an act.

(AT 49,21–50,10) But perhaps that being is not God, and I was produced by my parents or by other causes less perfect than God. Yet, as I said before, it is clear that there must be at least as much in the cause as in the effect. So, since I am a thinking thing having in me an idea of God, whatever cause is finally assigned to me must also be a thinking thing and have the idea of all the perfections I attribute to God. And the question can be asked of it again whether it derives its existence from itself or from another. If from itself, it is plain from what has been said that it is God, since having the power to exist by itself it certainly also has the power to possess actually all the perfections of which it has the idea, that is, all those I conceive to be in God. If from another, the same question will be asked of this other, until we finally reach the ultimate cause, which will be God. For it is clear enough that there can be no infinite regress here, especially since I am dealing not only with the cause that once produced me but above all with the one that preserves me at the present time. The second proof reconnects with the first: my cause must contain, at least objectively, the idea of God, since I contain it, and so must be a thinking thing. The regress is cut not by the impossibility of an infinite series in the past, which Descartes elsewhere declines to assert, but by the simultaneity of preservation: a present effect needs a present sufficient cause. The First Replies explain why Descartes avoids Thomas's argument from the series of efficient causes in sensible things: from my inability to comprehend an infinite series of causes it follows only that my intellect is finite, not that there is a first cause; so he based his argument on his own existence, which depends on no series of causes, and on what preserves him now (AT 106,13–107,7). "From itself" (a se) is taken positively. Caterus and Arnauld object that nothing can be the efficient cause of itself. Descartes answers that God's immense power is the cause or reason why he needs no cause, so that he does in a way stand to himself as an efficient cause to its effect; the natural light requires that we be able to ask of anything why it exists, and the answer in God's case is his essence, which is a formal cause analogous to an efficient one, as the sphere in Archimedes is treated as a polygon with infinitely many sides (First Replies, AT 108,7–111,19; Fourth Objections, AT 207,25–214,6; Fourth Replies, AT 235–245).

(AT 50,11–24) Nor can it be supposed that several partial causes may have combined to produce me, and that from one I received the idea of one of the perfections I attribute to God and from another the idea of another, so that all those perfections are found somewhere in the universe but not all joined together in one being who would be God. On the contrary, the unity, simplicity or inseparability of everything in God is one of the chief perfections I understand him to have. And the idea of this unity of all his perfections could not have been put in me by any cause from which I did not also receive the ideas of the other perfections; for it could not have made me understand them as joined and inseparable without at the same time making me know what they were. The polytheist alternative fails because unity is itself a perfection in the idea, and the idea of the unity of the perfections cannot be received separately from the ideas of the perfections it unites. The Second Objectors suggest that this unity is formed by the intellect, as Porphyry's universals are; Descartes answers that it designates a particular positive perfection in God, whereas generic unity adds nothing real to the individuals (Second Objections, AT 124,23–27; Second Replies, AT 140,6–11).

(AT 50,25–51,5) As for my parents, even if everything I ever believed about them is true, they certainly do not preserve me, nor did they in any way make me insofar as I am a thinking thing. They only placed certain dispositions in the matter in which I have judged that I, that is, my mind, which alone I now take to be myself, am contained. So there can be no difficulty here about them; and it must be concluded that from the mere fact that I exist and that there is in me an idea of a supremely perfect being, that is of God, it is most evidently demonstrated that God also exists. Parents are causes of becoming, not of being: they arrange matter, and do not create or preserve the mind. The proof is thus independent of every truth about human generation. The conclusion has two premises only, my existence and my idea of God, which is why the Fifth Replies can call the faculty of amplifying human perfections into something more than human "enough by itself to demonstrate God's existence" (AT 370,24–371,4).

(AT 51,6–14) It only remains to examine how I received this idea from God. I did not take it in through the senses, nor did it ever come to me unexpectedly, as the ideas of sensible things usually do when those things strike, or seem to strike, the external sense organs. Nor was it made by me, for I can neither take anything away from it nor add anything to it. So it remains that it is innate in me, just as the idea of myself is innate in me. The triple division of ideas returns and settles the idea of God as innate by elimination. "I can neither take anything away from it nor add anything to it" is the mark of a true and immutable nature, which the Fifth Meditation will use for the ontological argument. Gassendi says the idea of God grows as we learn more; Descartes replies that essences are indivisible, so that adding to or subtracting from an idea makes it the idea of another thing, as Pandora and the false gods are made, while discovering new perfections only makes the true idea more distinct (Fifth Replies, AT 371,8–26). Hobbes asks whether the souls of deep sleepers think, since what is innate must always be present. Descartes answers that an innate idea is not one always before the mind but one we have the faculty of eliciting from ourselves (Third Objections X, AT 187,28–188,4; Third Replies, AT 189,1–4). The Second Objectors point to the Hurons and Canadians, who show no such idea (AT 124,10–13), and Gassendi asks why others do not understand God as Descartes does if the idea is impressed on all; Descartes answers that everyone knows the idea of a triangle, yet not all notice equally much in it and some reason falsely about it (Fifth Replies, AT 374,1–5).

(AT 51,15–52,9) Certainly it is no wonder that God, in creating me, put this idea in me to be, as it were, the mark of the craftsman stamped on his work; nor need the mark be anything distinct from the work itself. From the mere fact that God created me, it is very believable that I was made in some way in his image and likeness, and that this likeness, in which the idea of God is contained, is perceived by me through the same faculty by which I perceive myself. That is, when I turn the eye of the mind on myself, I understand not only that I am an incomplete thing dependent on something else and aspiring without limit to ever greater or better things, but at the same time that the one on whom I depend has all these greater things in himself, not indefinitely and potentially but actually and infinitely, and so that he is God. The whole force of the argument lies in this: I recognize that I could not exist with the nature I have, that is, having in me the idea of God, unless God really existed—the God, I mean, whose idea is in me, who has all those perfections I cannot comprehend but can in some way reach in thought, and who is subject to no defects whatever. From this it is clear enough that he cannot be a deceiver, since it is manifest by the natural light that all fraud and deception depend on some defect. The mark of the craftsman is not a signature added to the work but the work's own structure: the mind's self-perception includes the idea of God because to know oneself as dependent, incomplete and aspiring is already to have in view the one who is complete. The "image and likeness" is Genesis, stated as a philosophical claim about self-knowledge. Gassendi asks what form the mark has and how it was impressed, and concludes that if the mark is not distinct from the work, the mind is nothing but an idea. Descartes answers with Apelles: one may recognize an inimitable artistry in a painting as the painter's mark without asking its form, and the artistry is no other thing than the painting, which does not make the painting nothing but artistry (Fifth Objections, AT 306; Fifth Replies, AT 372,1–19). Likeness, he adds, does not require sameness in everything, only imitation in some respects, and the most perfect power of thinking in God is represented by our less perfect one (Fifth Replies, AT 372,20–373,6). The Second Replies specify the mark: God's absolute immensity, simplicity and unity, which have no example in us and show that nothing we attribute to God applies to him and to us univocally (AT 137,12–20). The final sentence draws the conclusion the First Meditation required—God is no deceiver—from the idea's own content, since deception implies defect. Its consequences for error are the business of the Fourth Meditation.

(AT 52,10–20) Before examining this more carefully and inquiring into the other truths that can be gathered from it, I would like to pause here for some time in the contemplation of God himself, to weigh his attributes in myself, and to gaze at, wonder at and adore the beauty of this immense light, so far as the eye of my darkened mind can bear it. For just as we believe by faith that the supreme happiness of the other life consists solely in the contemplation of the divine majesty, so we now experience that from the same contemplation, though much less perfect, we can derive the greatest pleasure of which we are capable in this life. The proof ends in contemplation, and the change of register is not ornament. The meditation that began by closing the senses ends by turning the mind's eye on a light it cannot bear, the reverse of the First Meditation's fear of waking into darkness (AT 23,14–18). The comparison with the beatific vision keeps the boundary drawn in the Epistle: faith teaches the happiness of the next life, while natural reason finds here only an imperfect anticipation of it. The pause also has a methodological function, matching the end of the Second Meditation: what has been demonstrated must become habitual before the Fourth Meditation uses it to reconstruct the rule of truth.

### Fourth Meditation: Of the True and the False

(AT 52,23–53,22) In these past days Descartes has grown so used to withdrawing his mind from the senses, and has noticed so carefully how little is truly perceived about bodies, how much more is known about the human mind and more still about God, that he can now turn his thought without difficulty from imaginable things to things purely intelligible and separate from all matter. His idea of the human mind, insofar as it is a thinking thing, not extended in length, breadth and depth and having nothing else of body, is much more distinct than his idea of any bodily thing. And when he attends to the fact that he doubts, that is, that he is an incomplete and dependent thing, so clear and distinct an idea of an independent and complete being, God, presents itself, and from the mere fact that this idea is in him, or that he exists having it, he concludes so plainly that God exists and that his whole existence depends on God at every moment that he is confident nothing more evident or more certain can be known by human wit. He now seems to see a way by which, from this contemplation of the true God, in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge, one can come to the knowledge of other things. The recapitulation ranks the three objects by how well they are known—bodies least, the mind more, God most—which is the ranking the Synopsis said the whole work exists to establish (AT 16,1–8). Two phrases go beyond the Second Meditation. The mind is now described as "not extended … and having nothing else of body," a negative characterization the Second Meditation avoided; it describes the idea, not yet the thing, and the real distinction still waits for the Sixth. The self-description "incomplete and dependent" (incompleta & dependens) concerns the mind's relation to God, not to body. When Arnauld presses the notion of a complete thing, Descartes keeps the senses apart: a substance cannot be incomplete in the sense of being unable to exist by itself, but mind and body may be called incomplete in relation to the human being they compose, while each is complete taken alone (Fourth Replies, AT 222,5–24). The quotation from Colossians 2:3 announces the reversal of order the Meditations carry out: knowledge of God comes first and the knowledge of everything else is to be derived from it.

(AT 53,23–29) First, Descartes recognizes that God can never deceive him, since in all fraud or deception some imperfection is found; and though being able to deceive may seem a sign of cleverness or power, wanting to deceive undoubtedly shows malice or weakness and so cannot be found in God. The distinction between being able to deceive and wanting to deceive concedes something to the First Meditation's omnipotent deceiver: capacity is not the issue, will is. The Second Objectors' list of theologians who allowed God to lie, and of scriptural deceptions, is answered by the same distinction, now framed as one between deceptive words, which may be blameless, and the formal malice of deception, which is incompatible with supreme being (Second Replies, AT 142,14–143,17). Hobbes asks whether "God cannot deceive us in any case" is true universally, since physicians deceive patients and fathers their children for their good; Descartes replies that his conclusion requires not that we can never be deceived—he admits we often are—but that we are not deceived in a case where our error would show a will to deceive in God (Third Objections XV, AT 194,16–195,8; Third Replies, AT 195,9–14).

(AT 53,30–54,3) Next, Descartes experiences that he has a faculty of judging, which, like everything else in him, he received from God; and since God does not want to deceive him, he certainly did not give him one such that, while using it rightly, he could ever err. The qualification "while using it rightly" (dum eâ recte utor) holds the whole theodicy of error in advance. The faculty is guaranteed, but its use is not; error will be located in use, so that God's veracity and human error can both stand.

(AT 54,4–30) No doubt would remain about this, except that it seems to follow that he can never err at all; for if everything in him comes from God, and God gave him no faculty for erring, it seems he could never err. And indeed, so long as he thinks only of God and turns wholly towards him, he finds no cause of error or falsity. But when he turns back to himself, he experiences that he is subject to innumerable errors. Looking for their cause, he notices that there appears to him not only a real and positive idea of God, the supremely perfect being, but also, so to speak, a certain negative idea of nothing, of what is supremely remote from all perfection, and that he is so constituted as a mean between God and nothing, between supreme being and non-being, that insofar as he was created by the supreme being there is nothing in him by which he could be deceived or led into error, while insofar as he also in some way participates in nothing or non-being, that is, insofar as he is not himself the supreme being and lacks a great many things, it is not surprising that he is deceived. So he understands error, as error, not as something real that depends on God but as merely a defect; and so he needs no faculty given him by God for the purpose of erring, but errs because the faculty of judging truly that he has from God is not infinite in him. The scale of being from God to nothing is borrowed from the Neoplatonic and scholastic tradition, and the solution it offers is the Augustinian one: error, like evil, is not a being and so needs no divine cause. The movement between two attentions—turned to God, no error appears; turned back to myself, innumerable errors—repeats the alternation of the Third Meditation's opening, now with God as the object of attention. Gassendi asks what idea of nothing we have and how we participate in non-being. Descartes answers that he called the idea negative, and that it signifies only that we are not the supreme being and lack a great many things (Fifth Replies, AT 374,9–13). Hobbes objects that error requires a positive faculty of reasoning or imagining, since stones cannot err; Descartes answers that error requires a faculty of judging only as its defect, as blindness is a defect though stones are not called blind (Third Objections XII, AT 190,5–13; Third Replies, AT 190,22–191,4).

(AT 54,31–55,13) But this is not yet fully satisfactory. Error is not a pure negation but a privation, the lack of some knowledge that in some way ought to be in me. And attending to God's nature, it does not seem possible that he should have put in me a faculty that is not perfect in its kind, or that lacks some perfection due to it. For if the more skilled the craftsman, the more perfect the works he produces, what could the supreme maker of all things have made that is not complete in every respect? There is no doubt that God could have created me such that I was never deceived, and no doubt that he always wills what is best. Is it then better for me to be deceived than not? The scholastic distinction between negation and privation reopens the problem. A negation is a mere absence—a stone's lack of sight; a privation is the absence of what ought to be there—blindness in an eye. If error is privation, the first solution's appeal to finitude no longer suffices, since finitude explains negations, not privations. The paragraph thus frames the remainder of the Meditation: error must be shown to involve no privation in anything God made, only in what I do.

(AT 55,14–26) When he weighs this more attentively, it occurs to him first that he should not be surprised if God does things whose reasons he does not understand, nor doubt God's existence because he perhaps experiences other things of which he does not grasp why or how God made them. Since he now knows that his nature is very weak and limited and God's immense, incomprehensible and infinite, he knows well enough that God can do innumerable things whose causes he does not know; and for this reason alone he considers the whole class of causes that are usually sought from the end to have no use in physics, for he cannot without rashness think he can investigate the ends of God. The first answer is the incomprehensibility principle announced in the Preface (AT 9,8–18), and it has a far-reaching corollary: final causes are excluded from physics. The exclusion is epistemic, not ontological. God may well have ends; we cannot know them, so they cannot ground physical explanation. Gassendi defends the argument from design, citing the use of the parts of plants and animals. Descartes answers that such considerations lead us to admire God as efficient cause and to glorify him from his works, not to guess the end for which he made each thing; in ethics, where conjecture is often permitted, it may be pious to consider what end God might have had, but in physics, where everything must rest on the firmest reasons, it is absurd (Fifth Objections, AT 308–310; Fifth Replies, AT 374,19–375,13).

(AT 55,27–56,8) It also occurs to him that whenever we ask whether God's works are perfect, we must look not at one creature in isolation but at the whole universe of things. What might rightly seem very imperfect if it were alone is most perfect as having the role of a part in the world. And though, since he resolved to doubt everything, he has so far known for certain only that he and God exist, he cannot deny, having noticed God's immense power, that many other things have been made by him, or at least can be, so that he occupies the place of a part in the universe of things. The second answer is the aesthetic or totalizing theodicy: the perfection of the whole may require imperfect parts. Descartes applies it with care to his own epistemic situation, since he does not yet know that anything but himself and God exists; the argument therefore needs only the possibility of other creatures, which God's power guarantees. Gassendi compares the universe to a commonwealth with bad citizens. Descartes rejects the analogy: the citizens' malice is something positive in relation to the commonwealth, whereas our liability to error is only the absence of a greater perfection, and a better comparison is with someone who would have the whole human body covered with eyes because no part looks finer than an eye (Fifth Replies, AT 376,1–14). He also denies having said that some of God's works are not wholly complete; he said only that they might seem imperfect if considered as a whole rather than as parts (Fifth Replies, AT 374,14–18).

(AT 56,9–58,13) Next, coming closer to himself and investigating what his errors are like (they alone show some imperfection in him), he notices that they depend on two concurrent causes: the faculty of knowing that is in him and the faculty of choosing, or freedom of choice (arbitrii libertas), that is, the intellect and the will together. Through the intellect alone he only perceives ideas about which he can make judgments, and in the intellect so considered no error properly so called is found. For although there may be innumerable things of which he has no ideas, he must be said not to be deprived of them but only negatively to lack them, since he can give no reason to prove that God ought to have given him a greater faculty of knowing than he did; however skilled he understands the craftsman to be, he does not think the craftsman had to put into each of his works all the perfections he can put into some. Nor can he complain that he received from God a will or freedom of choice not ample and perfect enough, since he experiences it as circumscribed by no limits. And it seems to him very noteworthy that nothing else in him is so perfect or so great that he does not understand it could be still more perfect or greater. If he considers the faculty of understanding, he at once recognizes that in him it is very small and very finite, and at the same time forms the idea of another much greater, indeed supreme and infinite, which he perceives to belong to God's nature from the very fact that he can form its idea. In the same way, if he examines memory, imagination or any other faculty, he finds none that he does not understand to be slight and limited in him and immense in God. The will alone, or freedom of choice, he experiences as so great that he can grasp the idea of none greater, so that it is above all by reason of the will that he understands himself to bear a certain image and likeness of God. For although the will is incomparably greater in God than in him, both because of the knowledge and power joined to it, which make it firmer and more effective, and because of its object, since it extends to more things, it does not seem greater when considered formally and precisely in itself. It consists only in our being able to do or not do the same thing (that is, to affirm or deny, pursue or avoid), or rather only in this: that we are carried towards what the intellect proposes for affirmation or denial, pursuit or avoidance, in such a way that we feel ourselves determined to it by no external force. For to be free I need not be capable of being moved either way; on the contrary, the more I incline to one side, whether because I clearly understand the reasons of truth and goodness in it, or because God so disposes the inmost parts of my thought, the more freely I choose it. Neither divine grace nor natural knowledge ever diminishes freedom; rather they increase and strengthen it. But the indifference I experience when no reason pushes me one way rather than the other is the lowest grade of freedom, and shows no perfection in it, only a defect or a certain negation in knowledge; for if I always saw clearly what was true and good, I would never deliberate about what to judge or choose, and so, though entirely free, I could never be indifferent. The analysis divides judgment between two faculties and assigns each a different relation to infinity. The intellect is finite, but its finitude is negation, not privation, since no creature is owed more understanding than it has. The will is formally infinite: it is an all-or-nothing power to affirm or deny, which cannot be had in part. That makes the will the seat of the image of God, an unusual claim against the scholastic tradition, which located the image chiefly in the intellect. Two definitions of freedom are given in one sentence and corrected by a "rather" (vel potius). The first, the two-way power to do or not do, is replaced by the second, spontaneity without external constraint, which is compatible with being determined by evidence or by grace. On that definition indifference—equipoise of reasons—is the lowest grade of freedom. Hobbes notes that freedom is assumed without proof and against the Calvinists. Descartes answers that he assumed only what everyone experiences in himself, that it is most evident by the natural light, and that, though many cannot see how freedom is consistent with God's preordination, no one considering himself alone fails to experience that being voluntary and being free are the same (Third Objections XII, AT 190,15–20; Third Replies, AT 191,5–14). The Sixth Objectors argue that if clear vision removes indifference, God, who sees everything most clearly yet was indifferent to creating this world or another or none, would not be free. Descartes replies that freedom is entirely different in God and in us. God's will was indifferent from eternity to everything that has been or will be done, since nothing true or good can be conceived whose idea was in the divine intellect before his will determined it to be so; God did not will the angles of a triangle to equal two right angles because he knew it could not be otherwise, but it cannot be otherwise because he willed it. Supreme indifference in God is the supreme sign of his omnipotence. Man, finding the nature of the true and the good already fixed by God, embraces them more freely the more clearly he sees them, and is never indifferent except when he does not know which is better or truer (Sixth Objections, AT 416,23–417,12; Sixth Replies, AT 431,26–433,10). The reply joins the Fourth Meditation's theory of human freedom to the doctrine of the creation of the eternal truths stated in the letters to Mersenne of 1630 (AT I 145–153). The later letters to Mesland complicate the picture by allowing a "positive faculty" of determining oneself either way even in the face of evidence (9 February 1645, AT IV 173–175); here the will's power of withholding assent is affirmed against weak evidence, not against clear perception.

(AT 58,14–21) From this Descartes perceives that the power of willing he has from God, considered in itself, is not the cause of his errors, since it is most ample and perfect in its kind; nor is the power of understanding, since whatever he understands he understands rightly, because he has from God that he understands, and in this he cannot be deceived. Whence then do his errors arise? From this alone: since the will extends more widely than the intellect, he does not keep it within the same limits but extends it also to things he does not understand; and being indifferent to these, it easily turns away from the true and the good, and so he is deceived and sins. The cause of error is a disproportion between two faculties, each faultless taken alone. The intellect, insofar as it understands, cannot err, because understanding is a gift of God; the will, being infinite, cannot be limited by nature to what the intellect perceives, so it must limit itself. The final word, "and I sin" (et pecco), extends the analysis from theoretical error to moral fault, which seems to contradict the bracket of the Synopsis restricting the Meditation to speculative truth (AT 15,5–12). The two can be reconciled: the sin in question is the misuse of judgment, the culpable assent that the next paragraphs describe, not the moral error of pursuing good and evil that the Synopsis excludes; Arnauld's request for the bracket shows how easily the sentence could be read the other way (Fourth Objections, AT 215,15–216,9; Fourth Replies, AT 247,24–248,10). Gassendi objects that the will cannot extend beyond the intellect, since we will nothing of which we do not understand something. Descartes concedes that we will nothing of which we understand nothing at all, but denies that we understand as much as we will: we can will many things about the same object and know only a few. When someone judges that the mind is a subtle body, he understands that the mind is a thinking thing and that a subtle body is an extended thing, but he does not understand that one and the same thing thinks and is extended; he only wants to believe it because he believed it before. And when he judges that a fruit that may be poisoned is good to eat, he understands that its smell and colour are pleasant, not that it is wholesome (Fifth Objections, AT 314–316; Fifth Replies, AT 376,19–377,16).

(AT 58,22–59,4) For example, when he examined in these past days whether anything in the world existed, and noticed that from the very fact that he examined it, it plainly followed that he existed, he could not but judge that what he understood so clearly was true. This was not because he was compelled by some external force, but because a great light in the intellect was followed by a great inclination in the will, and so he believed it the more spontaneously and freely the less indifferent he was to it. The first certainty becomes the paradigm of free judgment. The assent of the Second Meditation was necessitated, yet free, because the necessity came from the intellect's light and not from outside. Descartes again states the first certainty as a consequence ("it plainly followed that I existed"), which, read with the Second Replies (AT 140,18–141,2), shows that "follows" names an immediately seen connection. Hobbes says that "great light in the intellect" is a metaphor and therefore no argument, that the obstinate believer claims the same light, and that assent is not in the power of the will, since we believe what is proved whether we want to or not. Descartes answers that the phrase is explanatory, which is all it needs to be; that the light means the perspicuity of knowledge, which those who think they have it may lack, but which is still very different from obstinate opinion; and that to say we assent to clearly seen things "whether we want to or not" is like saying we desire a clearly known good whether we want to or not—"not wanting" has no place there, since it would mean willing and not willing the same thing (Third Objections XIII, AT 191,16–192,14; Third Replies, AT 192,16–27).

(AT 59,5–14) But now he not only knows that he exists insofar as he is a thinking thing; an idea of bodily nature also presents itself to him, and he happens to doubt whether the thinking nature that is in him, or rather that he himself is, is different from this bodily nature, or whether the two are the same. Suppose no reason yet occurs to his intellect that persuades him of one rather than the other: then he is indifferent to affirming or denying either, or even to judging nothing about the matter. The case chosen to illustrate indifference is the question of the Sixth Meditation. Descartes thus states, within the Fourth, that the real distinction has not yet been established: the Second Meditation gave a clear idea of the mind, but no reason yet compels a verdict on whether the thinking nature is identical with the bodily one. The correction "or rather that I myself am" (vel potius quae ego ipse sum) shows the order of reasons in motion: the identification of the "I" with the thinking nature is held, while its relation to body is suspended. Gassendi's example of the person who judges the mind to be a subtle body is the same case decided wrongly, by a will that outruns the intellect (Fifth Replies, AT 376,21–377,3).

(AT 59,15–27) Indeed, this indifference extends not only to things of which the intellect knows nothing at all, but generally to everything the intellect does not know clearly enough at the time the will deliberates about it. However probable the conjectures that draw me to one side, the mere knowledge that they are conjectures and not certain and indubitable reasons is enough to push my assent the other way. I experienced this well enough in these past days, when I supposed to be wholly false everything I had previously believed most true, solely because I had found that it could be doubted in some way. The First Meditation is retroactively explained by the theory of judgment: the will's power to withhold or reverse assent in the face of mere probability is what made the method of doubt possible. Indifference is defined broadly, as any lack of perfect clarity at the moment of deliberation, so that it covers the probable as well as the unknown. The claim that knowing a reason to be only conjectural can push assent to the opposite matches the First Meditation's turning of the will "in the opposite direction" (AT 22,13–16). Gassendi's denial of indifference meets Descartes's refusal to argue: these things must be experienced by each person in himself rather than proved, and Gassendi, he says, may be unfree if he likes; he himself will enjoy his freedom. Descartes adds that Gassendi's own admission that we can take care not to persist in error requires exactly the will's power to move itself without the intellect's determination (Fifth Replies, AT 377,17–378,17).

(AT 59,28–60,10) When he does not perceive clearly and distinctly enough what is true, then if he abstains from judging, it is clear that he acts rightly and is not deceived. But if he affirms or denies, he does not use his freedom of choice rightly; and if he turns to the side that is false, he will plainly be deceived, while if he embraces the other, he will hit on the truth by chance, but will not be without fault, since it is manifest by the natural light that the perception of the intellect must always precede the determination of the will. In this incorrect use of free choice lies the privation that constitutes the form of error: the privation, I say, lies in the operation itself insofar as it proceeds from me, but not in the faculty I received from God, nor in the operation insofar as it depends on him. The paragraph states the norm of judgment and locates the privation precisely. A true belief formed without clear perception is still a fault, since the norm concerns the order of the faculties, not the outcome. This is an ethics of belief: truth by luck is blameworthy, as the Third Meditation said of the old belief in external things (AT 35,26–29). The privation that had made the problem acute (AT 54,31–55,1) is found at last: not in any faculty, but in the use I make of the will, insofar as that use proceeds from me. God concurs in the act as an act, which is real and good; he does not concur in the privation, which is nothing.

(AT 60,11–19) He has no reason to complain that God did not give him a greater power of understanding or a greater natural light than he did, since it belongs to the concept of a finite intellect that it does not understand many things, and to the concept of a created intellect that it is finite. He has reason to thank God, who never owed him anything, for what he has given, not to think himself deprived of what God did not give or to think God took it from him. The first of three complaints is disposed of by the concept of creation: a created intellect is necessarily finite, so its limits are negations, not privations. The theodicy turns into gratitude, a reminder that the Meditations remain a religious exercise even where they argue most technically.

(AT 60,20–25) Nor has he reason to complain that God gave him a will that extends more widely than the intellect. Since the will consists of only one thing and is, as it were, indivisible, its nature does not seem to allow that anything be taken from it; and the more ample it is, the more thanks he owes its giver. The second complaint is answered by the will's indivisibility: a power to affirm or deny cannot be given in part, so a will narrower than the intellect is not a possible alternative. The argument presupposes the formal definition of the will given above (AT 57,20–27), on which the will has no degrees in itself, only in the knowledge and power joined to it.

(AT 60,26–61,26) Nor should he complain that God concurs with him in eliciting those acts of will, those judgments, in which he is deceived; for those acts are wholly true and good insofar as they depend on God, and there is in a way more perfection in him because he can elicit them than if he could not. The privation, in which alone the formal concept of falsity and fault consists, needs no concurrence from God, since it is not a thing, and, related to God as its cause, should be called not a privation but only a negation. There is no imperfection in God in his having given him the freedom to assent or not to assent to things of which he did not put a clear and distinct perception in his intellect; but there is undoubtedly imperfection in him in not using that freedom well and in judging things he does not rightly understand. He sees, however, that God could easily have brought it about that, though remaining free and of finite knowledge, he never erred: either by endowing his intellect with a clear and distinct perception of everything about which he would ever deliberate, or simply by impressing so firmly on his memory that he should never judge anything he did not clearly and distinctly understand that he could never forget it. And he easily understands that, considered as a whole by himself, he would have been more perfect than he is if God had made him so. But he cannot deny that there is in a way greater perfection in the whole universe of things because some of its parts are not immune from error and others are, than if all were exactly alike. And he has no right to complain that God wanted him to play in the world a role that is not the most important and most perfect of all. The privation is relative: in relation to me it is a privation, since I ought to use my will rightly; in relation to God, who owed me nothing, it is a negation. Descartes then grants the strongest form of the objection—God could have made a free and finite being that never errs, either by giving clear perception of everything it deliberates about, or by fixing the rule of judgment unforgettably in its memory—and answers only by returning to the universe as a whole and to the variety of roles in it. The concession is notable. The theodicy does not claim that error is necessary to freedom, only that a world with fallible creatures can be more perfect than one without them. The second of God's possible remedies, an unforgettable rule, anticipates the remedy the next paragraph says is in our own power.

(AT 61,27–62,7) Moreover, even if he cannot abstain from errors in the first way, which depends on an evident perception of everything about which he must deliberate, he can do so in the other way, which depends only on his remembering that whenever the truth of a matter is not clear he must abstain from judging. For though he experiences in himself the weakness of not being able always to keep his attention fixed on one and the same piece of knowledge, he can, by attentive and often repeated meditation, bring it about that he remembers it whenever the need arises, and so acquire a certain habit of not erring. What God did not do, the meditator can do for himself. The second remedy God omitted becomes a task: to make the rule of judgment habitual. The weakness of attention that the circle discussion turns on returns here as a practical limit, and the remedy is the same as there—memory, now trained into habit. The Meditations' insistence on long and repeated meditation (AT 34,6–9; Second Replies, AT 130,17–131,18) receives its justification: the purpose is to form a disposition (habitus), so that the norm of judgment operates even when the reasons for it are not present to mind.

(AT 62,8–26) Since the greatest and chief perfection of man consists in this, he thinks he has gained not a little by today's meditation, since he has investigated the cause of error and falsity. And there can certainly be no other cause than the one he has explained. For whenever he restrains his will in making judgments so that it extends only to what the intellect shows it clearly and distinctly, it cannot happen that he errs; for every clear and distinct perception is undoubtedly something, and so cannot come from nothing, but necessarily has God for its author—God, I say, who is supremely perfect and to whom it is repugnant to be a deceiver; therefore it is undoubtedly true. And today he has learned not only what to avoid in order never to be deceived, but also what to do to reach the truth; for he will certainly reach it if only he attends enough to everything he understands perfectly and separates it from the rest, which he apprehends more confusedly and obscurely. He will take care to do this from now on. The general rule of truth, laid down tentatively at the opening of the Third Meditation (AT 35,13–15), is now proved, and the proof uses the causal principle once more: a clear and distinct perception is something real, so it needs a cause; its cause is God, who cannot deceive; therefore it is true. The proof makes the Fourth Meditation the point of transition announced in the Synopsis, which said it was needed both to secure what precedes and to understand what follows (AT 15,3–6). The highest human perfection is placed in the right use of judgment, a claim that carries the ethics of belief into the definition of human excellence, and that the later letters and the Passions of the Soul will extend into the doctrine of generosity, the firm resolve to use one's free will well. The double result—what to avoid and what to do—gives the method of the Fifth and Sixth Meditations: to attend to what is clearly understood and to separate it from what is confused, beginning with the ideas of material things.

### Fifth Meditation: Of the Essence of Material Things, and Again of God, That He Exists

(AT 63,4–11) Much remains to be investigated about God's attributes and about the nature of Descartes himself, that is, of his mind; but he may take this up another time. Now that he has noticed what to avoid and what to do in order to reach the truth, nothing seems more urgent than to try to emerge from the doubts into which he fell in the past days, and to see whether anything certain can be had about material things. The Meditation's announced subject is material things, and the proof of God it contains is offered as a second theme, "again of God," not as its centre. The postponed investigations of God's attributes and of the mind are left to "another time"; the Sixth Meditation will take up the mind only in its relation to body. The phrase "what to avoid and what to do" picks up the double result of the Fourth Meditation (AT 62,20–26), so that the method now applied is the rule of attending to the clearly perceived and separating it from the confused.

(AT 63,12–15) Before inquiring whether any such things exist outside him, he must consider their ideas insofar as they are in his thought, and see which of them are distinct and which confused. The order essence before existence is the order the whole work follows for each of its objects. For the mind, existence came first because the "I am" was the first certainty; for bodies, the question of existence is postponed until their essence is fixed, so that the Sixth Meditation will know what it is proving to exist.

(AT 63,16–21) He distinctly imagines quantity, which the philosophers commonly call continuous, or the extension of this quantity, or rather of the thing that has quantity, in length, breadth and depth; he counts various parts in it; he assigns to these parts any sizes, shapes, positions and local motions, and to these motions any durations. The list repeats the First Meditation's simple natures (AT 20,15–19) and the clear contents of the ideas of bodies in the Third (AT 43,18–23), now under the verb "imagine." The correction "or rather of the thing that has quantity" (aut potius rei quantae) rejects the scholastic separation of quantity from the substance that has it: extension is not an accident added to body but the extended thing considered as extended. Imagining here is distinct imagination of geometrical objects, which the Sixth Meditation will distinguish from pure understanding by the example of the chiliagon (AT 72,4–73,4).

(AT 63,22–64,5) Not only are these things, considered in general, plainly known and evident to him, but by attending he perceives innumerable particulars about shapes, number, motion and the like, whose truth is so open and so suited to his nature that, when he first discovers them, he seems not so much to learn anything new as to remember what he already knew, or to notice for the first time what was in him long before, though he had not yet turned the eye of the mind to it. The language of recollection is Platonic and deliberate: mathematical discovery feels like remembering because its objects are innate. Descartes keeps the comparison as appearance ("I seem") and adds the alternative he holds, that he notices what was already in him. The doctrine of innateness is thus a doctrine of attention: an innate idea is one the mind can elicit from itself by turning towards it, as he told Hobbes (Third Replies, AT 189,1–4).

(AT 64,6–24) What he thinks most worth considering here is that he finds in himself innumerable ideas of certain things which, even if they perhaps exist nowhere outside him, cannot be called nothing; and though he can think them in a way at will, they are not made up by him but have their own true and immutable natures. For example, when he imagines a triangle, even if perhaps no such figure exists or ever has existed anywhere outside his thought, it certainly has a determinate nature, or essence, or form, immutable and eternal, which was not made by him and does not depend on his mind. This is clear from the fact that various properties of the triangle can be demonstrated—that its three angles equal two right angles, that its greatest side subtends its greatest angle, and the like—which he now clearly recognizes whether he wants to or not, even if he never thought of them before when he imagined the triangle, so that they were not made by him. The distinction between thinking at will and inventing is the central idea of the Meditation. I am free to think of a triangle or not, but not free to determine what follows from it, and the mark of a true and immutable nature is that it yields properties I did not put into it and cannot refuse. Its being does not depend on existence outside thought: the triangle "cannot be called nothing" even if no triangle exists. Hobbes replies that if a triangle exists nowhere it has no nature, since what is nowhere is not; that the eternal truth of "a triangle has three angles equal to two right angles" is only the eternal connection of names; and that essence apart from existence is our fiction. Descartes answers in two lines: the distinction between essence and existence is known to everyone, and what Hobbes says of eternal names in place of concepts or ideas of eternal truth has already been refuted (Third Objections XIV, AT 193,2–194,10; Third Replies, AT 194,11–14). Gassendi finds it hard to posit anything immutable and eternal besides God. Descartes replies that he does not make the essences independent of God: as the poets imagine that the fates were established by Jupiter, who then bound himself to keep them, so the essences and the mathematical truths are immutable and eternal because God so willed and so disposed (Fifth Objections, AT 319–320; Fifth Replies, AT 380,1–12). The Sixth Replies make the same point in the language of causes: God is the author of the eternal truths as a king is author of a law, and it would be unreasonable to doubt what we rightly understand merely because we do not understand how God could have made it otherwise (AT 435,22–436,25).

(AT 64,25–65,15) It is no answer to say that this idea of a triangle perhaps came to him from external things through the sense organs, because he has sometimes seen bodies of triangular shape. He can think up innumerable other figures that he could not suspect ever reached him through the senses, and still demonstrate various properties of them no less than of the triangle. These properties are certainly all true, since he knows them clearly, and so they are something, not mere nothing; for it is obvious that whatever is true is something, and he has fully demonstrated that everything he clearly knows is true. And even if he had not demonstrated it, the nature of his mind is such that he could not but assent to them, at least while he perceives them clearly; and he remembers that even before, when he was most attached to the objects of the senses, he always held truths of this kind, about shapes or numbers or other matters of arithmetic, geometry or pure and abstract mathematics, to be the most certain of all. The empiricist origin of mathematical ideas is rejected by counterexample: figures never seen yield demonstrations as well as the triangle does. "Whatever is true is something" makes truth a property of being, which grounds the reality of essences that may not exist. The paragraph then lays out the two sources of the meditator's assent to mathematics in their proper order. He has demonstrated that clear perception is true; and even without that demonstration, he cannot help assenting while he perceives clearly. The second clause anticipates the circle passage below: the present evidence of clear perception never depended on God. Gassendi insists that geometrical ideas are abstracted from sensible bodies. Descartes answers that no truly straight line ever reached our senses, since lines that look straightest prove irregular under a magnifying glass; the triangle drawn on paper could no more teach us the true triangle than rough wood contains a Mercury. We recognize the geometrical triangle in the drawing because its idea was already in us, as we see a face in a few ink lines because we know faces better than lines (Fifth Objections, AT 321–322; Fifth Replies, AT 381,20–382,23).

(AT 65,16–66,1) Now if, from the mere fact that he can draw the idea of something from his thought, it follows that everything he clearly and distinctly perceives to belong to that thing really does belong to it, can he not also derive from this an argument proving God's existence? He certainly finds in himself the idea of God, of a supremely perfect being, no less than the idea of any figure or number; and he understands no less clearly and distinctly that it belongs to God's nature that he always exists than that what he demonstrates of some figure or number belongs to the nature of that figure or number. So, even if not everything he has meditated in the past days were true, God's existence ought to have for him at least the same degree of certainty that mathematical truths have had so far. The argument is generated by the doctrine of essences: whatever is clearly seen to belong to a true and immutable nature can be affirmed of it, and existence is seen to belong to the nature of God. Its premise is the idea of God as found in me, "no less than" a mathematical idea, so that the proof inherits the status of mathematics. The striking claim is that it would hold even if the earlier Meditations were wrong: the a priori proof does not depend on the causal argument of the Third. The geometrical exposition gives it as Proposition I, prepared by Axiom X, that possible or contingent existence is contained in the concept of every thing and necessary existence in the concept of God alone (Second Replies, AT 166,14–167,9). The fifth postulate goes further: whoever contemplates the nature of the supremely perfect being long enough will know without any reasoning that God exists, as some things are self-evident to some people and known only by reasoning to others (AT 163,22–164,3). The modification "that he always exists" (ut semper existat) matters: what belongs to the essence is eternal existence, not existence at some time. The first edition read "that he exists actually" (existat actu), as AT notes.

(AT 66,2–15) At first sight, however, this is not wholly clear; it has the look of a sophism. Since he is used to distinguishing existence from essence in all other things, he easily persuades himself that existence can be separated from God's essence too, and so that God can be thought of as not existing. But on closer attention it is plain that existence can no more be separated from God's essence than the fact that its three angles equal two right angles can be separated from the essence of a triangle, or the idea of a valley from the idea of a mountain; so that it is no less contradictory to think of a God (that is, a supremely perfect being) lacking existence (that is, lacking some perfection) than to think of a mountain without a valley. The first objection is the habit of distinguishing essence from existence, valid for every created thing. Descartes answers by attention, not by argument: on closer inspection the separation turns out to be impossible in this one case. The parenthesis "that is, lacking some perfection" carries the proof's controversial premise, that existence is a perfection. Gassendi denies it: existence is not a perfection either in God or in anything else, but that without which there are no perfections; a thing that does not exist has neither perfection nor imperfection. Descartes replies that existence can be called a property as well as omnipotence can, taking "property" for any attribute, and that necessary existence is a property in God in the strictest sense, since it belongs to him alone and forms part of his essence; possible existence too is a perfection in the idea of a triangle, making it better than the idea of a chimera, whose existence is supposed impossible (Fifth Objections, AT 322–325; Fifth Replies, AT 382,24–383,20).

(AT 66,16–25) But even if he cannot think of God except as existing, as he cannot think of a mountain without a valley, it certainly seems not to follow, just as from his thinking of a mountain with a valley it does not follow that there is any mountain in the world, that from his thinking of God as existing God exists. For his thought imposes no necessity on things, and just as he may imagine a winged horse though no horse has wings, so perhaps he can attach existence to God though no God exists. The second objection is the one Caterus brings from Thomas: even granting that the name "God" signifies that than which nothing greater can be thought, it does not follow that what the name signifies exists in reality, only in the apprehension of the intellect; the concept of existence is inseparably joined to the concept of the supreme being, but that does not make the supreme being exist (First Objections, AT 99,3–20). Caterus adds a sharper case: the complex "existing lion" contains existence essentially, yet no lion follows from it.

(AT 66,26–67,11) But here the sophism lies hidden. From his inability to think of a mountain except with a valley it does not follow that a mountain and a valley exist anywhere, but only that mountain and valley, whether they exist or not, cannot be separated from each other. From his inability to think of God except as existing, however, it follows that existence is inseparable from God, and so that he really exists; not that his thought brings this about or imposes any necessity on anything, but on the contrary because the necessity of the thing itself, namely of God's existence, determines him to think it. For he is not free to think of God without existence (that is, of a supremely perfect being without supreme perfection), as he is free to imagine a horse with or without wings. The reply turns the analogy into a disanalogy. What the mountain case shows is a necessary connection between two items, which holds whether they exist or not; in God's case the connected item is existence itself, so the necessary connection is the existence. The necessity runs from the thing to the thought: I am determined to think God as existing by God's necessity, as I am determined to think the triangle's angles equal to two right angles by the triangle's nature. The test of freedom recurs from the triangle paragraph: I can think a horse with or without wings because the winged horse is my composition, but I cannot think God without existence. The First Replies supply the criterion that distinguishes such compositions from true natures. Ideas composed by the intellect can be divided by it, not merely by abstraction but by a clear and distinct operation, so that I can as easily think a horse without wings or a lion not existing; what the intellect cannot divide in this way, it did not compose. That answers Caterus's existing lion. The First Replies add the distinction between possible existence, contained in the idea of everything clearly conceived, and necessary existence, contained in the idea of God alone, and they restate the proof through power: whatever can exist by its own power always exists, so the idea of a supremely powerful being contains necessary existence by its true and immutable nature (First Replies, AT 116,8–119,26). Against Thomas, Descartes notes that the argument Thomas refutes has a fault of form, concluding from what the name signifies to what is, whereas his own syllogism runs from what is clearly understood to belong to a true and immutable nature (AT 115,9–116,5).

(AT 67,12–68,20) Nor should it be said here that he must indeed suppose God to exist once he has supposed that God has all perfections, since existence is one of them, but that the first supposition was not necessary; as it is not necessary to think that all quadrilaterals can be inscribed in a circle, but if he does think so he will have to admit that a rhombus can be inscribed in a circle, which is plainly false. For though it is not necessary that he ever come to think of God, whenever he chooses to think of the first and supreme being and to bring its idea out of the treasury of his mind, he must attribute all perfections to it, even if he does not then enumerate them all or attend to each; and this necessity is quite enough to make him rightly conclude, when he later notices that existence is a perfection, that the first and supreme being exists. In the same way it is not necessary for him ever to imagine a triangle, but whenever he wants to consider a rectilinear figure with only three angles, he must attribute to it what entitles him to infer that its three angles are not greater than two right angles, even if he does not notice this at the time. But when he examines which figures can be inscribed in a circle, it is in no way necessary to think all quadrilaterals are among them; indeed he cannot even pretend so, as long as he wants to admit nothing but what he clearly and distinctly understands. So there is a great difference between such false suppositions and the true ideas innate in him, of which the first and chief is the idea of God. For he understands in many ways that this idea is not something fictitious depending on his thought but the image of a true and immutable nature: first, because he can think of nothing else to whose essence existence belongs except God alone; next, because he cannot understand two or more Gods of this kind, and because, supposing one exists now, he plainly sees that he must have existed from eternity and will remain for eternity; and finally, because he perceives many other things in God, none of which he can take away or change. The third objection is that the definition of God as having all perfections is an arbitrary starting point, like the false supposition that all quadrilaterals are inscribable. Descartes concedes that thinking of God is not necessary but denies that the content of the idea is arbitrary: once I think of the supreme being, all perfections come with it implicitly, as the triangle's properties come with the triangle. The quadrilateral case differs because the supposition cannot even be entertained clearly. Four marks show that the idea of God is a true nature: uniqueness of essence-involving existence, uniqueness of God, eternity, and the impossibility of adding or subtracting anything. Gassendi charges that existence is counted among God's perfections by begging the question. Descartes answers that it is no more a petitio principii than counting the equality of the angles among the triangle's properties, and that in God, unlike the triangle, essence and existence cannot be thought apart, since God is his own being (Fifth Replies, AT 383,8–15). The Second Objectors propose that the argument shows only that God exists if his nature is possible, that is, not contradictory, and that the minor premise is precisely what atheists deny. Descartes replies that if "possible" means what does not conflict with human concepts, the nature of God as he described it is plainly possible, since it contains nothing but what we clearly perceive to belong to it. If "possible" refers to some other possibility on the side of the object, unknowable to us, it would as well overturn every other item of human knowledge, including that a triangle's angles equal two right angles or that someone who is thinking exists. Every contradiction lies in our concepts when we join incompatible ideas, and arises only from obscurity; so it is enough that we understand the little we perceive of God clearly, though not adequately (Second Objections, AT 127,3–29; Second Replies, AT 149,22–152,25).

(AT 68,21–69,9) But whatever method of proof he uses, it always comes back to this: only what he clearly and distinctly perceives fully convinces him. Of the things he perceives in this way, some are obvious to everyone, while others are discovered only by those who look more closely and investigate carefully; but once discovered, the latter are held to be no less certain than the former. In a right-angled triangle, though it is not so easy to see that the square on the base equals the squares on the sides as that the base subtends the greatest angle, it is believed no less once it has been grasped. As for God, if he were not overwhelmed by prejudices and if images of sensible things did not besiege his thought on every side, he would know nothing sooner or more easily than God; for what is more manifest in itself than that the supreme being exists, that is, God, to whose essence alone existence belongs? Every proof reduces to clear perception, and the difference between easy and hard truths is a difference of access, not of certainty. The comparison with Pythagoras's theorem ranks the ontological argument among truths that are certain once seen but hard to see, which is why the Synopsis expected difficulties with it (AT 15,15–16). The claim that God would be known first and most easily without prejudices restates the postulate that God's existence can be self-evident (Second Replies, AT 163,22–164,3). It also explains why Descartes hesitated to use the argument: those who do not remember everything needed for its proof will take it for a sophism and may distrust the rest (First Replies, AT 120,3–13). There are only two ways of proving God's existence, Descartes adds there, from his effects and from his essence, and having taken the first in the Third Meditation he could not omit the second (AT 120,8–13).

(AT 69,10–15) Although he needed careful consideration to perceive this, he is now not only as certain of it as of anything that seems most certain; he also notices that the certainty of everything else depends on it, so that without it nothing could ever be known perfectly. The paragraph announces the epistemological consequence the Synopsis attributed to this Meditation, that even geometrical certainty depends on knowledge of God (AT 15,17–19). "Known perfectly" (perfecte sciri) prepares the distinction the next paragraph makes between perceiving clearly and having knowledge in the strict sense.

(AT 69,16–70,9) For though he is of such a nature that, as long as he perceives something very clearly and distinctly, he cannot help believing it true, he is also of such a nature that he cannot always fix the eye of his mind on the same thing in order to perceive it clearly, and the memory of a judgment previously made often comes back when he is no longer attending to the reasons for which he made it. Then other reasons can be brought forward that would easily dislodge him from his opinion if he did not know God, and so he would never have true and certain knowledge of anything, only vague and changeable opinions. For example, when he considers the nature of a triangle, it appears most evident to him, being versed in the principles of geometry, that its three angles equal two right angles, and he cannot help believing it true as long as he attends to its demonstration. But as soon as he turns the eye of his mind away from it, however well he remembers having perceived it most clearly, it can easily happen that he doubts whether it is true, if he does not know God. For he can persuade himself that he was made by nature so as to be deceived sometimes in what he thinks he perceives most evidently, especially as he remembers that he often took things to be true and certain which he later judged false for other reasons. This is the passage on which Descartes rests his answer to the charge of circularity. Present clear perception compels assent and needs no guarantee. What needs one is the memory of a demonstration once the reasons are no longer attended to, because the bare memory that I perceived something clearly can be met by the general doubt about my nature, which present evidence cannot be. Knowledge in the strict sense (scientia) is defined by stability: it is persuasion that no reason can overturn, as Descartes tells the Second Objectors when he denies that an atheist can have it (Second Replies, AT 141,3–13). The atheist geometer can perceive the theorem clearly; what he cannot have is certainty that survives the withdrawal of attention (Sixth Replies, AT 428,1–8). Arnauld's charge of a circle is met in exactly these terms (Fourth Replies, AT 245,25–246,9). Gassendi points to Diagoras, Theodorus and other atheists who were no less certain of geometry for doubting God. Descartes answers with the Skeptics, who did doubt geometrical demonstrations, and who would not have done so had they known God; and he adds that one thing is not shown to be better known than another because more people think it true (Fifth Objections, AT 327–328; Fifth Replies, AT 384,7–15).

(AT 70,10–71,2) But once he has perceived that God exists, and understood at the same time that everything else depends on him and that he is not a deceiver, and has inferred from this that everything he clearly and distinctly perceives is necessarily true, then even if he no longer attends to the reasons for which he judged it true, provided only he remembers that he perceived it clearly and distinctly, no contrary reason can be brought that would push him to doubt; he has true and certain knowledge of it. And not only of this, but of everything else he remembers having once demonstrated, such as the truths of geometry and the like. For what will now be objected to him? That he is so made that he is often deceived? But he now knows that he cannot be deceived in what he understands clearly. That he previously took many things as true and certain that he later found to be false? But he had perceived none of them clearly and distinctly; ignorant of this rule of truth, he had believed them for other reasons, which he later found less firm. What then will be said? That perhaps he is dreaming, as he recently objected to himself, or that everything he now thinks is no more true than what occurs to someone asleep? Even this changes nothing; for even if he were dreaming, whatever is evident to his intellect is entirely true. The objections of the First Meditation are taken up one by one and each loses its grip on remembered knowledge. The defective nature is answered by God's veracity, the past errors by their lack of clarity, and the dream by the independence of intellectual evidence from waking. The last answer is the most far-reaching: the dream argument never touched intellectual truth, only the judgments of sense and existence, so even a dreaming geometer knows geometry. What divine veracity adds is permanence. Knowledge that once required present attention can now be held in memory, which makes possible a science consisting of long chains of reasons.

(AT 71,3–9) So he sees plainly that the certainty and truth of all knowledge depend on the knowledge of the true God alone, so that before he knew God he could know nothing perfectly about anything else. But now innumerable things can be plainly known and certain to him, both about God himself and other intellectual things and about the whole of corporeal nature that is the object of pure mathematics. The conclusion is stated in its strongest form—"before he knew God, he could know nothing perfectly"—but "perfectly" restricts it to knowledge in the strict sense, so it does not contradict the present certainty of the "I am" and of mathematics while attended to. The domain now open to knowledge is described with care: God, other intellectual things, and corporeal nature as the object of pure mathematics. That last phrase leaves open whether such a nature exists, which is the question of the Sixth Meditation. Corporeal nature is now known as an essence, extension with its modes, and the Sixth will open from exactly this point: material things can exist insofar as they are the object of pure mathematics, because I perceive them clearly and God can make whatever I can clearly perceive (AT 71,13–20).

### Sixth Meditation: Of the Existence of Material Things, and of the Real Distinction of the Mind from the Body

(AT 71,13–72,3) It remains to examine whether material things exist. Descartes already knows at least that they can exist insofar as they are the object of pure mathematics, since he perceives them clearly and distinctly; for there is no doubt that God can produce everything Descartes can perceive in this way, and he has never judged that anything could not be done by God except on the ground that it could not be distinctly perceived by him. Further, the faculty of imagining, which he finds himself using when he deals with material things, seems to imply that they exist; for when he considers attentively what imagination is, it appears to be nothing but a certain application of the knowing faculty to a body intimately present to it, and so existing. The Meditation begins with possibility, which the Fifth has already secured: a clearly perceived essence is a possible existent, because God can make whatever I can clearly perceive. The geometrical exposition draws the same corollary from the proof of God (Second Replies, AT 169,6–17). The principle that the limits of God's power coincide with the limits of distinct perception will carry the proof of the real distinction below. The second sentence opens the first of two arguments for the existence of bodies, from imagination, which yields only probability. Its definition of imagination—an application of the knowing faculty to a body intimately present to it—already contains the conclusion it is meant to support, which is why the argument is first made plausible and then judged insufficient.

(AT 72,4–73,4) To make this clear, he first examines the difference between imagination and pure understanding. When he imagines a triangle, he not only understands that it is a figure bounded by three lines, but also looks at the three lines as if present with the eye of the mind, and this is what he calls imagining. If he wants to think of a chiliagon, he understands as well that it is a figure of a thousand sides as he understands that a triangle has three; but he does not imagine the thousand sides in the same way or look at them as if present. Though, from the habit of always imagining something when he thinks of a bodily thing, he may then represent some figure to himself confusedly, it is plainly not a chiliagon, since it is in no way different from the figure he would represent if he thought of a myriagon or any other figure with many sides, and it does nothing to help him recognize the properties by which a chiliagon differs from other polygons. With a pentagon, he can understand its figure without imagination, like the chiliagon's, but he can also imagine it, by applying the eye of the mind to its five sides and the area they enclose; and here he plainly notices that imagining requires a peculiar effort of mind that he does not use for understanding, and this new effort shows clearly the difference between imagination and pure understanding. The chiliagon separates understanding from imagining by a case in which they come apart. The understanding of a thousand-sided figure is fully determinate, sufficient for demonstrating its properties, while the image is indeterminate between a chiliagon and a myriagon. The pentagon shows the converse: where both are possible, imagination adds an effort (contentio) that understanding does not require, so the two differ not in degree of clarity but in kind of act. Gassendi holds that our understanding of the chiliagon is confused, a matter of the name only. Descartes answers that many properties of the chiliagon can be demonstrated, which could not be done if it were perceived confusedly or only by name; we understand the whole clearly at once though we cannot imagine it at once, so understanding and imagining differ not by more and less but as two wholly different modes of operating. In understanding, the mind uses itself alone; in imagining, it contemplates a bodily form; and though geometrical figures are wholly corporeal, the ideas by which they are understood, when they do not fall under imagination, are not corporeal (Fifth Objections, AT 330–331; Fifth Replies, AT 384,21–385,12). Hobbes's challenge to say how imagining differs from conceiving (Third Objections IV, AT 178,1–4) receives its full answer here.

(AT 73,5–28) Descartes also considers that this power of imagining, insofar as it differs from the power of understanding, is not required for his own essence, that is, for the essence of his mind; for even if he lacked it, he would undoubtedly remain the same as he now is. From this it seems to follow that it depends on something different from him. And he easily understands that, if some body exists to which the mind is so joined that it can apply itself to inspect it at will, it may be that by this very means he imagines bodily things; so that this mode of thinking differs from pure understanding only in that the mind, in understanding, in a way turns towards itself and looks at one of the ideas that are in it, while in imagining it turns towards the body and looks at something in it that conforms to an idea either understood by itself or perceived by sense. He easily understands, he says, that imagination could be accomplished in this way if body exists; and since no other equally suitable way of explaining it occurs to him, he conjectures from this with probability that body exists—but only with probability, and however carefully he investigates everything, he does not yet see that from the distinct idea of corporeal nature he finds in his imagination any argument can be drawn that necessarily concludes that some body exists. The argument from imagination is an inference to the best explanation, and Descartes rates it exactly: probable, not demonstrative. Two of its steps matter later. Imagination is inessential to the mind, since without it I would be the same; this is the first statement of the doctrine that the mind's faculties of imagining and sensing are modes that presuppose the mind, not parts of its essence (AT 78,21–28). And imagination is described as the mind's turning towards the body to inspect an image there, as understanding is its turning towards itself. Gassendi asks how an unextended subject can receive the image of an extended body. Descartes answers that no bodily image is received in the mind. Pure understanding of bodily as of incorporeal things takes place without any bodily image; imagination does need an image that is a real body, but one to which the mind applies itself, not one received in the mind (Fifth Replies, AT 387,6–14). The understanding of extension, he adds, does not require an extended image in the mind, any more than understanding the size of the earth requires the mind to be larger than the earth (Fifth Replies, AT 389,3–6; 390,9–14).

(AT 74,1–10) Besides the corporeal nature that is the object of pure mathematics, he usually imagines many other things—colours, sounds, tastes, pain and the like—but none so distinctly; and since he perceives these better by sense, from which they seem to have reached the imagination with the help of memory, he must for convenience treat sense at the same time, and see whether, from what is perceived by the mode of thinking he calls sense, he can have any certain argument for the existence of bodily things. The move from imagination to sense follows the order of evidence: the sensible qualities, which imagination holds only confusedly, come from sense and must be examined at their source. Sense is called a "mode of thinking," as the Second Meditation required (AT 29,11–18). The question is now whether sense, which failed as a criterion of truth in the First Meditation, can furnish a certain argument for existence once God's veracity is known.

(AT 74,11–16) First he will recall what he previously took to be true as perceived by sense, and why; then he will weigh the reasons for which he later called these things in doubt; and finally he will consider what he must now believe about them. The three-step plan—former beliefs, the reasons for doubting them, present verdict—makes the Sixth Meditation a reckoning with the First. It applies the Fourth Meditation's method to sense: the inquiry is not whether to trust the senses but which of their deliverances are clear and which are confused.

(AT 74,17–76,20) First, then, he sensed that he had a head, hands, feet and the other limbs making up the body he regarded as part of himself, or perhaps as his whole self. He sensed that this body was situated among many other bodies by which it could be affected in various beneficial or harmful ways, measuring the beneficial ones by a sensation of pleasure and the harmful ones by a sensation of pain. Besides pain and pleasure he sensed in himself hunger, thirst and other such appetites, and certain bodily inclinations towards cheerfulness, sadness, anger and other such emotions. Outside, besides the extension, shapes and motions of bodies, he sensed in them hardness, heat and other tactile qualities, and also light, colours, smells, tastes and sounds, by whose variety he distinguished the sky, earth, seas and other bodies from one another. Not without reason, given the ideas of all these qualities that presented themselves to his thought, and which alone he properly and immediately sensed, did he think he sensed things wholly different from his thought, namely bodies from which these ideas proceeded. For he experienced that they came to him without his consent, so that he could neither sense any object, however much he wanted to, unless it was present to the sense organ, nor fail to sense it when it was present. And since the ideas perceived by sense were much more lively and vivid, and in their own way more distinct, than any he deliberately formed by meditating or found impressed on his memory, it seemed impossible that they came from himself, so it remained that they came from other things. Since he had no knowledge of these things except from those ideas, nothing else could occur to him but that they resembled them. And since he remembered that he had used his senses before his reason, and saw that the ideas he formed himself were not as vivid as those he perceived by sense and were mostly composed of their parts, he easily persuaded himself that he had nothing in his intellect that he had not first had in sense. Nor was it without reason that he judged the body he called his own by a special right to belong to him more than any other; for he could never be separated from it as from the others; he sensed all appetites and emotions in it and for it; and he noticed pain and the titillation of pleasure in its parts, not in bodies outside it. But why a sadness of mind should follow from that sensation of pain, and a joy from the sensation of titillation, or why that twitching of the stomach he calls hunger should prompt him to eat, and dryness of the throat to drink, he had no reason except that nature taught him so; for there is no affinity, at least none he understands, between that twitching and the will to eat, or between the sensation of something causing pain and the thought of sadness arising from it. Everything else he judged about the objects of the senses he seemed to have learned from nature, since he had persuaded himself that it was so before weighing any reasons that would prove it. The reconstruction of naive belief is generous: each belief had a reason, and the reasons are those the Third Meditation discounted—involuntariness, vividness, resemblance as the only available model, and the scholastic maxim that nothing is in the intellect that was not first in sense. The paragraph adds what the Third Meditation passed over, the special relation to one's own body: inseparability, the location of appetites and emotions in it, and the felt location of pain. The last observation is the most important for what follows. Between bodily states and the sensations or emotions that follow them there is no intelligible affinity; the connection is learned "from nature." The Sixth Meditation will not dissolve this lack of affinity but explain it, by the institution of nature in a composite of mind and body.

(AT 76,21–77,27) Later, many experiences gradually undermined all the trust he had placed in the senses. Towers that had seemed round from afar appeared square from close by, and very large statues on their tops did not seem large to someone looking from the ground; in countless such cases he found the judgments of the external senses deceived. And not only of the external senses but of the internal ones too: for what can be more inward than pain? Yet he had heard from people whose leg or arm had been amputated that they sometimes still seemed to feel pain in the part they lacked, so it seemed not quite certain to him that a limb of his was in pain even though he felt pain in it. To these he recently added two very general causes of doubt. The first was that everything he ever believed he sensed while awake he could sometimes think he sensed while asleep, and since he did not believe that what he seemed to sense in dreams came from things outside him, he did not see why he should believe it of what he seemed to sense awake. The second was that, since he did not yet know the author of his origin, or at least pretended not to know it, he saw nothing to prevent his having been so constituted by nature that he was deceived even in what appeared most true to him. As for the reasons by which he had previously persuaded himself of the truth of sensible things, he answered them without difficulty: since nature seemed to push him towards many things that reason advised against, he did not think he should trust much in what nature teaches; and though the perceptions of the senses did not depend on his will, he did not think it should be concluded that they came from things different from him, since perhaps there was some faculty in him, not yet known to him, that produced them. The paragraph collects the doubts in ascending order of generality: particular illusions of the external senses, the phantom limb as an illusion of internal sense, then the dream and the unknown author of my nature from the First Meditation, and the refutations of the Third. The phantom limb is new, and it matters for the account of pain's location below (AT 86,24–87,18), which will explain it. The phrase "or at least pretended not to know" marks the deceiver hypothesis as a methodological fiction, consistent with the First Meditation's own description (AT 22,13–16).

(AT 77,28–78,1) But now that he is beginning to know himself and the author of his origin better, he thinks he should not rashly admit everything he seems to have from the senses, nor call everything in doubt. The verdict is a middle course, and it frames the rest of the Meditation: some deliverances of sense will be restored, others corrected, and the principle of division will come from knowledge of God and of the self.

(AT 78,2–20) First, since he knows that everything he clearly and distinctly understands can be made by God just as he understands it, it is enough that he can clearly and distinctly understand one thing apart from another to be certain that one is different from the other, since they can be placed apart at least by God; and it does not matter by what power this is done for them to be judged different. So, from the very fact that he knows he exists, and that meanwhile he notices nothing else at all to belong to his nature or essence except that he is a thinking thing, he rightly concludes that his essence consists in this alone, that he is a thinking thing. And although perhaps (or rather, as he will say later, certainly) he has a body very closely joined to him, nevertheless, since on the one hand he has a clear and distinct idea of himself insofar as he is only a thinking thing, not extended, and on the other a distinct idea of body insofar as it is only an extended thing, not thinking, it is certain that he is really distinct from his body and can exist without it. Here the promise of the Preface is kept: from my knowing nothing else to belong to my essence, it follows that nothing else does (AT 8,6–15). The bridge is God's power, which makes separability in thought a guarantee of separability in reality, so that real distinction can be known from clear and distinct conceivability of each without the other. The geometrical exposition gives the same argument as Proposition IV and adds that divine power is used as the middle term not because any extraordinary force is needed to separate mind from body, but because, having dealt only with God, Descartes had nothing else to use; and it does not matter by what power two things are separated for us to know them really distinct (Second Replies, AT 169,19–170,17). Arnauld's objections fall on this paragraph. First, the argument requires adequate, not merely clear and distinct, knowledge, since abstraction can separate in thought what is united in reality; and someone might clearly perceive a triangle in a semicircle to be right-angled while doubting or denying that the square on its base equals the squares on its sides, and conclude by the same reasoning that God could make a right triangle without that property. Second, body might relate to mind as genus to species. Third, the argument proves too much, leading to the Platonic view that a human being is a soul using a body (Fourth Objections, AT 199,26–203,25). Descartes replies that what is required is not adequate knowledge—which only God can know himself to have—but knowledge we have not made inadequate by an abstraction of the intellect, that is, knowledge of each thing as a complete thing, a substance clothed with attributes enough to recognize it as a substance (Fourth Replies, AT 219,10–222,7). The triangle fails as a parallel in three ways: the property is not a complete thing; we cannot clearly understand a triangle whose base-square equals the squares of its sides without noticing that it is right-angled, whereas we clearly perceive mind without body and body without mind; and the triangle cannot be understood if every proportion between its squares is denied, whereas everything belonging to body can be denied of mind (Fourth Replies, AT 224,8–225,25; 227,1–10). The genus-species model fails because a species cannot be thought without its genus, while mind can be understood completely without any attribute of body (AT 223,8–24). And the argument does not prove too much, since the same Meditation proves that the mind is substantially united to the body: as someone who says an arm is a substance really distinct from the rest of the body does not deny that it belongs to the whole human being, so the real distinction is compatible with the union (AT 227,20–228,18). Descartes adds why the proof had to wait for the Sixth Meditation: had he wanted only ordinary certainty, the Second Meditation would have sufficed, since we commonly judge things to be in reality as they are in our perception; but the hyperbolic doubt reached exactly this, so everything about God and truth in the Third, Fourth and Fifth Meditations contributes to the conclusion (Fourth Replies, AT 226,7–25). The Sixth Replies recount how he came to accept the conclusion fully. He had at first been compelled by the reasons but not persuaded, like astronomers who prove the sun larger than the earth but cannot help judging it smaller when they look at it. He never saw a human body think, only that the same men have both thought and a body, and from seeing two things often together one cannot infer that they are the same, whereas from seeing one sometimes without the other it is rightly inferred that they are different (Sixth Replies, AT 440,1–11; 444,3–445,6).

(AT 78,21–80,10) Further, he finds in himself faculties for certain special modes of thinking, namely the faculties of imagining and sensing, without which he can clearly and distinctly understand himself as a whole, but which he cannot understand without himself, that is, without an understanding substance in which they inhere; for they include some intellection in their formal concept, so he perceives that they are distinguished from him as modes from a thing. He also recognizes certain other faculties, such as changing place and taking on various shapes, which likewise cannot be understood apart from some substance in which they inhere, nor therefore exist without it; but it is plain that these, if they exist, must inhere in a bodily or extended substance, not an understanding one, since some extension, but no intellection at all, is contained in their clear and distinct concept. Now there is in him a certain passive faculty of sensing, that is, of receiving and knowing the ideas of sensible things; but he could make no use of it unless there were also, either in him or in something else, an active faculty of producing or making these ideas. This cannot be in him, since it presupposes no intellection at all, and the ideas are produced without his cooperation and often against his will. So it must be in some substance different from him, in which there must be, formally or eminently, all the reality that is objectively in the ideas produced by that faculty, as he noted above. This substance is either body, that is, corporeal nature, in which everything is contained formally that is in the ideas objectively; or God, or some creature nobler than body, in which it is contained eminently. But since God is not a deceiver, it is quite manifest that he does not send these ideas to me immediately by himself, nor through some creature in which their objective reality is contained not formally but only eminently. For since he has given me no faculty for recognizing this, but on the contrary a great inclination to believe that they are sent from bodily things, I do not see how he could be understood not to be a deceiver if they were sent from elsewhere than from bodily things. So bodily things exist. Yet perhaps not all of them exist exactly as I grasp them by sense, since this grasp by the senses is in many cases very obscure and confused; but at least everything is in them that I clearly and distinctly understand, that is, everything, considered generally, that is comprised in the object of pure mathematics. The proof of the existence of bodies has three premises. The ideas of sense require an active cause outside me, since their production presupposes no act of understanding and occurs against my will; this closes the possibility, left open in the Third Meditation, of an unknown faculty in me (AT 39,6–14). The cause must contain the ideas' objective reality formally or eminently, which leaves three candidates: body, God, or a nobler creature. And God has given me a strong inclination to believe the ideas come from bodies and no faculty for correcting that belief, so if they did not, he would be a deceiver. The third premise does not license trusting every natural inclination, which the Third Meditation disqualified (AT 38,29–39,5). It concerns an inclination that no faculty I have can correct, so that its falsity would be God's doing rather than my misuse of judgment. The conclusion is limited at once: bodies exist and have at least the properties of the object of pure mathematics; their sensible qualities are not thereby certified. The first part of the paragraph argues the modal status of imagination and sense: they are modes of a thinking substance, as motion and shape are modes of an extended one, which disposes of the worry that sensing might require the mind to be bodily. Hobbes's objection that God might deceive for our good, as physicians and fathers do, is answered by the restriction that the conclusion requires only that God not deceive where the error would show a will to deceive (Third Objections XV, AT 194,16–195,8; Third Replies, AT 195,9–14). The real distinction makes the cause of my sensory ideas something other than me; the doctrine of union, below, will specify that one particular body stands in a privileged relation to me.

(AT 80,11–26) As for other things that are either only particular—that the sun is of such a size or shape, and the like—or less clearly understood, such as light, sound, pain and the like, though they are very doubtful and uncertain, the very fact that God is not a deceiver, and that therefore no falsity can be found in my opinions unless there is also some faculty given me by God to correct it, gives me a certain hope of reaching the truth even in these. And there is certainly no doubt that everything I am taught by nature has some truth. By nature taken generally I now understand nothing but God himself or the coordination of created things instituted by God; and by my nature in particular, nothing but the complex of all the things God has given me. The principle of correctability is the Meditation's most important epistemological addition: God's veracity does not guarantee my opinions but guarantees that wherever falsity exists I have a means of correcting it. Applied to sense, it turns the problem of particular sensory judgments into a task for the faculties together. The two definitions of nature—God or the order he instituted, and the complex of what God has given me—replace the "blind impulse" of the Third Meditation (AT 39,30–40,4) with a teaching that must have some truth because it comes from God. Which truth it has is the question of the next paragraphs, and the answer depends on a narrower sense of "my nature" (AT 82,13–25).

(AT 80,27–31) There is nothing that this nature teaches me more expressly than that I have a body, which is in a bad way when I feel pain, which needs food or drink when I suffer hunger or thirst, and the like; so I should not doubt that there is some truth in this. The first teaching restored is the existence of my own body, known through pain and the bodily appetites rather than through external sense. The order matters: my body is known first as what is affected, not as an object among others, which prepares the doctrine of union.

(AT 81,1–14) Nature also teaches me by these sensations of pain, hunger, thirst and so on that I am not present in my body only as a sailor is present in a ship, but that I am very closely joined to it and, so to speak, mixed with it, so that I make up one thing with it. Otherwise, when the body was hurt, I, who am nothing but a thinking thing, would not then feel pain, but would perceive the injury by pure understanding, as a sailor perceives by sight if something in the ship is broken; and when the body needs food or drink, I would understand this explicitly and not have confused sensations of hunger and thirst. For these sensations of thirst, hunger, pain and so on are nothing but certain confused modes of thinking arising from the union and, as it were, mixture of the mind with the body. The sailor image, from Aristotle's De anima (II.1, 413a8–9) and the Platonic tradition, states the view Descartes rejects and Arnauld feared he held (Fourth Objections, AT 203,14–19). The argument against it is phenomenological: if I were only in my body as a pilot in a ship, I would know bodily damage as information, not suffer it as pain. The confusion of sensations is therefore not a defect of knowledge but the mark of a third kind of thing, neither pure thought nor pure extension, arising from the union. "As it were mixed" (quasi permixtum) is hedged, since the mind has no parts to mix. Gassendi asks how a mixture of an unextended mind with an extended body is possible. Descartes answers that no comparison should be drawn between the mixture of two bodies and the union of mind and body, since they are wholly different in kind, and that the difficulty arises only from wanting to subject to imagination what by nature does not fall under it (Fifth Replies, AT 389,9–390,14). The Sixth Replies give his most revealing account of the union. His childhood idea of gravity as a real quality spread through a body, whole in the whole and whole in each part, moving it towards the centre of the earth as though it knew where to go, was borrowed from the idea he had of the mind; and that is in fact how he still understands the mind to be coextensive with the body (AT 441,23–442,25). In the letters to Elisabeth of 1643 he will call the union a third primitive notion, known clearly by the senses and obscurely by the understanding (21 May 1643, AT III 665; 28 June 1643, AT III 691–692).

(AT 81,15–27) I am also taught by nature that various other bodies exist around my body, some of which I should pursue and others avoid. And from the fact that I sense very different colours, sounds, smells, tastes, heat, hardness and the like, I rightly conclude that there are in the bodies from which these various perceptions come certain variations corresponding to them, even if perhaps not resembling them; and from the fact that some of these perceptions are pleasant to me and others unpleasant, it is quite certain that my body, or rather I as a whole insofar as I am composed of body and mind, can be affected by the surrounding bodies in various beneficial and harmful ways. The second teaching restores other bodies and the correspondence of sensory variety to bodily variety, while explicitly withholding resemblance. Sensible qualities become signs: differences in sensation signal differences in their causes, without picturing them. The correction "or rather I as a whole, insofar as I am composed of body and mind" introduces the subject of these teachings—the human being as composite—and with it the narrowing of "nature" that follows.

(AT 82,1–83,23) But there are many other things that I seem to have been taught by nature but in fact received not from it but from a habit of judging rashly, and so it easily happens that they are false: that every space in which nothing occurs that moves my senses is empty; that in a hot body there is something wholly similar to the idea of heat in me; that in a white or green body there is the same whiteness or greenness I sense, in a bitter or sweet body the same taste, and so on; that stars, towers and other distant bodies are of just the size and shape that they show my senses; and other such things. To perceive nothing in this matter not distinctly enough, I must define more precisely what I mean when I say I am taught something by nature. I take nature here more strictly than for the complex of everything God has given me. That complex contains many things that belong to the mind alone, such as my perceiving that what has been done cannot be undone and everything else known by the natural light, which are not at issue here; and many things that concern the body alone, such as its tending downwards, which I am not dealing with either; but only those things God has given me as composed of mind and body. This nature teaches me to avoid what causes a sensation of pain and to pursue what causes a sensation of pleasure, and so on; but it does not appear to teach us to conclude anything about things outside us from these sensory perceptions without prior examination by the intellect, since knowing the truth about them seems to belong to the mind alone, not to the composite. So although a star affects my eye no more than the flame of a small torch, there is no real or positive inclination in me to believe it is no larger; I have judged so without reason from my earliest years. And though when I approach a fire I feel heat, and when I come too close I feel pain, there is no reason to persuade me that there is in the fire something like that heat, any more than something like that pain, but only that there is something in it, whatever it may be, which causes these sensations of heat or pain in us. And though there is nothing in some space that moves the senses, it does not follow that there is no body in it. I see that in these and many other cases I am accustomed to pervert the order of nature, because I use the perceptions of the senses, which are properly given by nature only to signify to the mind what is beneficial or harmful to the composite of which it is part, and are clear and distinct enough for that, as though they were sure rules for immediately discerning the essence of bodies outside us, about which they signify nothing except very obscurely and confusedly. The narrowed concept of nature resolves the conflict between the Third Meditation's distrust of natural impulse and the Sixth's trust in natural teaching. What nature teaches the composite concerns benefit and harm; what it seems to teach about the essence of external things is a habit of judgment formed in childhood. Sensation is thus given a function—to signify to the mind what is good or bad for the composite—and its clarity is measured relative to that function. The fire is the decisive example: heat and pain are on a level, and since no one supposes the pain to be in the fire, neither should the heat be. The empty space shows that the habit misreads absence of sensation as absence of body, an error with consequences for physics, where Descartes denies the void. "Perverting the order of nature" names the error precisely: a sign of utility is used as a rule of essence. The Sixth Objectors argue that the senses correct one another and that the intellect cannot correct the senses, as when touch corrects sight about the stick bent in water. Descartes answers with the three grades of sense. Only the third grade, judgment, can be false; and even when touch makes us judge the stick straight, we need a reason telling us to believe the judgment from touch rather than the judgment from sight, and that reason belongs to the intellect, so the intellect alone corrects the error (Sixth Objections, AT 418,10–22; Sixth Replies, AT 438,15–439,15). Gassendi's tower, square when seen close and touched, is answered in the same spirit: what cannot be doubted is that things appear as they appear, which Descartes asserted in the Second Meditation; the question here is the truth of things outside us (Fifth Replies, AT 385,23–386,10).

(AT 83,24–84,7) I have already seen well enough how, notwithstanding God's goodness, my judgments can be false. But a new difficulty arises here about the very things nature presents to me as to be pursued or avoided, and also about the internal senses, in which I seem to have found errors: as when someone, deceived by the pleasant taste of some food, takes in poison hidden inside it. But in that case he is driven by nature only to desire that in which the pleasant taste consists, not the poison, of which it knows nothing; and nothing can be concluded from this except that this nature is not omniscient, which is not surprising, since man is a limited thing and only limited perfection belongs to him. The Fourth Meditation explained errors of judgment; the new difficulty concerns errors in the teaching of nature itself, which God is responsible for. The poisoned food is the easy case. Nature's signal is correct—the taste is pleasant—but limited: it signals the taste, not the poison. Limitation is negation, not privation, so God's goodness is not touched. Gassendi's poisoned fruit, which the Fifth Replies treated as an error of judgment (AT 377,3–8), here becomes an example of nature's finite range.

(AT 84,8–85,17) But we often err even in things to which nature drives us, as when sick people desire food or drink that will shortly harm them. It might be said that they err because their nature is corrupted; but this does not remove the difficulty, since a sick man is no less truly a creature of God than a healthy one, so it seems no less repugnant that he should have a deceptive nature from God. And just as a clock made of wheels and weights observes all the laws of nature no less exactly when it is badly made and does not tell the time correctly than when it satisfies its maker's wishes in every respect, so, if I consider the human body as a kind of machine composed of bones, nerves, muscles, veins, blood and skin, so constructed that even if there were no mind in it, it would still have all the same motions as now occur in it not by command of the will and so not from the mind, I easily recognize that it would be just as natural for it, if it suffered for example from dropsy, to undergo the dryness of the throat that usually brings the sensation of thirst to the mind, and also to have its nerves and other parts so disposed by that dryness that it takes a drink by which the disease is increased, as it would be, when there is no such defect in it, to be moved by a similar dryness of the throat to take a drink that is useful to it. And although, looking at the clock's intended use, I might say that when it does not tell the time correctly it departs from its nature; and similarly, considering the machine of the human body as made for the motions that usually occur in it, I might think it too departs from its nature if its throat is dry when drink does not help its preservation; I notice well enough that this last sense of nature differs greatly from the other. This one is nothing but a denomination depending on my thought, which compares a sick man and a badly made clock with the idea of a healthy man and a well-made clock, and is extrinsic to the things of which it is said; whereas by the other I understand something really found in things, and so having some truth. The hard case is the dropsical patient, whose thirst drives him to drink what harms him. Descartes first considers the body alone, as a machine, and for a machine there is no error: a broken clock obeys the laws of nature as exactly as a working one, and "departing from its nature" is an extrinsic denomination, a comparison made by the mind with an ideal. Mechanism thus dissolves the problem of malfunction for the body considered alone. The machine body "so constructed that even if there were no mind in it, it would still have all the same motions" that do not come from the will, restates the doctrine of the Treatise of Man and the Discourse (Part V, AT VI 55–56). Arnauld raises the difficulty for animals: it seems incredible that, with no soul at all, light reflected from a wolf should move the sheep's optic nerves and its animal spirits so as to make it flee (Fourth Objections, AT 205,6–12). Descartes replies that many of our own movements—heartbeat, digestion, breathing in sleep, even walking and singing done without attention, and the hands thrown out in a fall—occur without the mind, so it is no wonder that the light from a wolf has the same force in a sheep (Fourth Replies, AT 229,10–230,11).

(AT 85,18–27) And certainly, even if, with respect to the dropsical body, it is only an extrinsic denomination to say that its nature is corrupted because it has a dry throat and yet does not need drink, still with respect to the composite, that is, to the mind united to such a body, it is not a pure denomination but a true error of nature that it is thirsty when drink will harm it. So it remains to be inquired here how God's goodness does not prevent nature taken in this way from being deceptive. The problem survives mechanism because the composite is not a machine. For the body alone, malfunction is an extrinsic denomination; for the mind united to the body, a sensation that signals benefit where there is harm is a real error, because sensation has a function, signification for the good of the composite, that the mere machine does not have. The union thus introduces genuine teleology into nature at exactly the point where Descartes excluded final causes from physics (AT 55,20–26): the function of sensation is known not by guessing God's purposes but from the experience of what sensations are for.

(AT 85,28–86,15) First, I notice here that there is a great difference between mind and body, in that body is by its nature always divisible, while the mind is wholly indivisible. For when I consider the mind, that is, myself insofar as I am only a thinking thing, I can distinguish no parts in myself but understand myself to be something wholly one and entire; and though the whole mind seems to be united to the whole body, if a foot or an arm or any other part of the body is cut off, I know that nothing has been taken away from the mind; nor can the faculties of willing, sensing, understanding and so on be called its parts, since it is one and the same mind that wills, senses and understands. On the other hand, there is no bodily or extended thing I can think of that I cannot easily divide in thought into parts, and by this very fact I understand it to be divisible; and this alone would suffice to teach me that the mind is entirely different from the body, if I did not already know it well enough from elsewhere. The argument from divisibility, promised in the Synopsis (AT 13,20–14,1), is offered as confirmation, not as the main proof. It turns on the unity of the subject established in the Second Meditation (AT 28,23–29,10): faculties are not parts, since one and the same mind exercises them all. The amputation example connects back to the phantom limb (AT 77,1–7), now with a different lesson: the loss of a limb subtracts nothing from the mind. Its place in the argument is to answer the dropsy problem. Because the mind is indivisible and the body divisible, the mind cannot be affected by every part of the body directly, which leads to the doctrine of transmitted signals in the next three paragraphs.

(AT 86,16–23) Next, I notice that the mind is not affected immediately by all parts of the body but only by the brain, or perhaps only by one small part of it, namely the one in which the common sense is said to be; whenever this part is disposed in the same way, it presents the same thing to the mind, even if the other parts of the body are meanwhile disposed differently, as countless experiments show which need not be reported here. The "small part" is the pineal gland of the Treatise of Man and the Dioptrics, named here only by its function, the seat of the common sense. The identification of the common sense with a single bodily site carries out the reduction announced in the Second Meditation, where the common sense was called the imaginative power (AT 32,17–19). The thesis that the same brain state always presents the same thing to the mind is the premise of the explanation of sensory error that follows.

(AT 86,24–87,18) I notice further that the nature of body is such that no part of it can be moved by another part somewhat distant from it without its being possible for it to be moved in the same way by any of the parts in between, even if the more distant part does nothing. For example, in a cord A, B, C, D, if its last part D is pulled, the first part A will move in no other way than it could also be moved if one of the intermediate parts B or C were pulled and the last part D remained unmoved. In the same way, when I feel a pain in the foot, physics has taught me that this sensation happens by means of nerves spread through the foot, which, stretched like cords from there to the brain, pull the innermost parts of the brain to which they extend when they are pulled in the foot, and excite in them a certain motion which nature has instituted to affect the mind with a sensation of pain as if existing in the foot. But since these nerves must pass through the shin, the thigh, the loins, the back and the neck to reach the brain from the foot, it can happen that even if the part of them in the foot is not touched, but only one of the intermediate parts, exactly the same motion occurs in the brain as occurs when the foot is badly affected, so that the mind will necessarily feel the same pain. And the same must be thought of any other sense. The cord model gives a mechanical explanation of referred pain and so of the phantom limb: the brain cannot discriminate between a disturbance at the end of the nerve and one along its length. "Physics has taught me" is significant: the Meditation now uses the physics that its metaphysics was meant to found, so that the explanation of sensory error relies on the existence of bodies established a few pages earlier. The phrase "instituted by nature" (institutus a natura) marks the link between the brain's motion and the sensation as a divine institution rather than a resemblance, which explains the lack of affinity between bodily states and sensations noticed at AT 76,6–15.

(AT 87,19–88,18) Finally I notice that, since each of the motions occurring in the part of the brain that immediately affects the mind brings it only one sensation, nothing better can be thought of in this matter than that it should bring the one that, of all those it could bring, is most and most often conducive to the preservation of a healthy man. And experience testifies that all the senses given us by nature are of this kind, so that nothing at all is found in them that does not testify to God's power and goodness. For example, when the nerves in the foot are moved violently and beyond the ordinary, their motion, reaching the innermost parts of the brain through the spinal marrow, gives the mind there a sign to feel something, namely pain as if existing in the foot, by which the mind is stirred to do what it can to remove its cause as harmful to the foot. God could have constituted man's nature so that the same motion in the brain showed the mind something else: either the motion itself as it is in the brain, or as it is in the foot, or in one of the places in between, or finally anything else; but nothing else would have been as conducive to the preservation of the body. In the same way, when we need drink, a certain dryness arises in the throat, moving its nerves and through them the interior of the brain; and this motion affects the mind with the sensation of thirst, because nothing in this whole business is more useful for us to know than that we need drink for the preservation of our health; and so with the rest. The theodicy of sensation is an optimality argument. Given that one brain motion produces one sensation, the best institution is the one that produces the sensation most often useful to a healthy body. God could have made the brain's motion appear to the mind as what it is, a motion; he chose instead a sensation that directs action, pain "in the foot," thirst. The sensation is a sign (signum), not a picture, and its location in the foot is part of its signifying function. The design is known from experience ("experience testifies"), not from God's purposes, which keeps the passage consistent with the ban on final causes: the usefulness of sensations is observed, not deduced.

(AT 88,19–89,7) From all this it is quite manifest that, notwithstanding God's immense goodness, the nature of man as composed of mind and body cannot but sometimes be deceptive. For if some cause, not in the foot but in any other of the parts through which the nerves extend from the foot to the brain, or even in the brain itself, excites the same motion that is usually excited when the foot is badly affected, pain will be felt as if in the foot, and the sense will naturally be deceived; for since the same motion in the brain can only ever bring the same sensation to the mind, and it usually arises much more often from a cause that hurts the foot than from one existing elsewhere, it is reasonable that it should always show the mind a pain in the foot rather than in another part. And if sometimes dryness of the throat arises, not as usual because drink is useful for the health of the body, but from some contrary cause, as happens in the dropsical, it is far better that it should deceive then than if, on the contrary, it always deceived when the body was in good condition; and so with the rest. The dropsy problem is solved by a rule of trade-off. A fixed correlation between brain states and sensations, optimized for the usual case, must misfire in unusual ones; error is the price of a system tuned for the healthy body. The error is real, as the previous paragraphs insisted, but it is the unavoidable consequence of the best possible institution for a composite of an indivisible mind and a divisible body. The solution differs from the Fourth Meditation's: there error was traced to my misuse of freedom, here to the structure of a finite composite, but in both God is cleared because the alternative would be worse or impossible.

(AT 89,8–90,16) This consideration helps a great deal, not only for noticing all the errors to which my nature is subject but also for easily correcting or avoiding them. For since I know that all the senses indicate the truth much more often than falsehood regarding what concerns the body's welfare, and I can almost always use several of them to examine the same thing, and also memory, which connects present things with previous ones, and the intellect, which has now seen all the causes of error, I should no longer fear that what the senses show me every day is false; the hyperbolic doubts of the past days should be rejected as laughable, especially the chief one about sleep, which I could not distinguish from waking. For I now notice that there is a great difference between them, in that dreams are never joined by memory with all the other actions of life, as the things that happen to someone awake are. If, while I was awake, someone suddenly appeared to me and then immediately disappeared, as happens in dreams, so that I saw neither where he came from nor where he went, I would rightly judge him to be a ghost or a phantom formed in my brain rather than a real man. But when things occur of which I distinctly notice where, from where and when they come to me, and I connect their perception without any interruption with the whole rest of my life, I am quite certain that they occur not in sleep but to someone awake. And I should not doubt their truth in the least if, after calling on all the senses, memory and the intellect to examine them, nothing is reported by any of them that conflicts with the others. For from the fact that God is not a deceiver, it follows that I am not deceived in such things. But since the need for action does not always allow the time for so careful an examination, it must be admitted that human life is often subject to errors about particular things, and the weakness of our nature must be acknowledged. The Meditations end with the restoration of everyday knowledge under a criterion of coherence. Senses checking senses, memory linking present to past, and the intellect that knows the causes of error together certify particular perceptions; the guarantee is the correctability principle (AT 80,11–19), now applied. The dream doubt, the First Meditation's most powerful, is answered by continuity: dreams do not connect with the rest of life by memory. Hobbes objects that someone dreaming can dream that his dream coheres with a long series of past events. Descartes answers that a dreamer cannot really connect what he dreams with the ideas of past things, though he can dream that he does; he will recognize his error on waking. An atheist can infer he is awake from memory of his past life, but cannot know that this sign suffices for certainty unless he knows he was created by a God who does not deceive (Third Objections, AT 195,16–196,4; Third Replies, AT 196,5–13). The final sentence returns to the distinction the whole work relies on, between the search for truth and the needs of action, and states its cost. The hyperbolic doubt could be indulged because nothing practical was at stake (AT 22,18–22); the certainty recovered at the end is available only where there is time for examination, so practical life remains exposed to error. The last word is an acknowledgment of the infirmity of our nature, not a claim of mastery. The Meditations close where the Synopsis said their aim lay: in showing that the reasons by which we know our mind and God are firmer and clearer than any by which we know the existence of bodies (AT 15,26–16,8).

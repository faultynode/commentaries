# Freud, *Massenpsychologie und Ich-Analyse* (1921): A Commentary

Citations give the page of *Gesammelte Werke* XIII (London: Imago, 1940), in parentheses. Freud quotes Le Bon from Rudolf Eisler's German translation (2nd ed., 1912); those quotations are cited by their GW page, not by Eisler's. Quotations from Freud are given in German and followed by an English rendering. *Masse* is rendered "mass" throughout: Strachey's "group" follows McDougall and hides the continuity with Le Bon's *foule*, and Freud uses one word for a street crowd, a church, an army, and a nation. *Massenseele* is "mass mind," *Gefühlsbindung* "emotional tie," *Führer* "leader," *Ich* "ego," *Ichideal* "ego ideal," *zielgehemmt* "aim-inhibited"; Libido is kept.

The text invites five recurrent misreadings. First, Freud does not endorse Le Bon: he takes Le Bon's description as clinical material and replaces Le Bon's explanation, so that the "collective soul" becomes a set of ties within individuals, never a super-individual entity. Second, libido here does not mean genital sexuality acting directly; the ties that hold a mass are aim-inhibited, and Freud's insistence on the sexual name is a claim about their origin, not their present aim. Third, identification is not imitation or empathy; empathy is derived from identification, not the reverse. Fourth, the leader is not an external hypnotic cause working on passive subjects; the leader works because members put one and the same object in the place of their ego ideal, and the book finally explains hypnosis by the mass rather than the mass by hypnosis. Fifth, the "ego ideal" of 1921 is the precursor of the superego of 1923, not the same concept: here it still carries reality testing, a function Freud later returns to the ego.

## I. Introduction

Freud opens by dissolving the opposition between individual and social psychology. Individual psychology follows how one person seeks to satisfy his drives, but it can almost never abstract from that person's relations to others: "Im Seelenleben des Einzelnen kommt ganz regelmäßig der Andere als Vorbild, als Objekt, als Helfer und als Gegner in Betracht" ("In the individual's mental life the other figures, quite regularly, as model, as object, as helper and as opponent") (73). Individual psychology is therefore social psychology from the start, in an extended but legitimate sense. The four roles are a table of contents for the book. The other as model becomes identification (VII); as object, object-cathexis and being in love (VIII); as helper, the anaclitic origin of social ties (VI); as opponent, ambivalence and rivalry (VI, IX). Psychoanalysis is not being applied to a new field. Freud claims that the field never lay outside it.

The relations analysis has studied so far, to parents, siblings, the beloved, the teacher, and the physician, can all count as social phenomena. Their real counterpart is not mass life but the narcissistic processes, in which drive satisfaction escapes the influence of others or renounces them. The opposition between social and narcissistic acts (Bleuler would say "autistic") falls entirely within individual psychology and cannot be used to separate it from mass psychology (73–74). Freud thus moves the dividing line: it now runs between object-directed and narcissistic libido, not between one person and many. The whole argument of chapter VI rests on this displacement, and on the economics of the 1914 essay on narcissism. The physician on the list is no accident: transference is the social relation analysis knows best.

In these intimate relations a person meets the influence of one or a few people, each of great importance to him. Mass psychology customarily sets these relations aside and isolates a different object: the simultaneous influence of a large number of people with whom one is linked by something, though otherwise they may be strangers. Its individual is the member of a tribe, people, caste, estate, or institution, or of a crowd organized at a given moment for a given purpose. Having torn apart a natural connection ("nach dieser Zerreißung eines natürlichen Zusammenhanges"), the discipline found it natural to attribute the resulting phenomena to a special, irreducible social drive, the "herd instinct" or "group mind" (74). Freud objects that number alone cannot plausibly awaken a drive otherwise never active. Two alternatives remain: "daß der soziale Trieb kein ursprünglicher und unzerlegbarer sein mag, und daß die Anfänge seiner Bildung in einem engeren Kreis, wie etwa in dem der Familie, gefunden werden können" ("that the social drive may be neither original nor indecomposable, and that the beginnings of its formation may be found in a narrower circle, such as the family") (74). These two theses set the program: decomposition (libido, identification) and genesis (the family, the Oedipus complex, and finally the primal horde of chapter X). The targets are Trotter's herd instinct (IX) and McDougall's group mind (III). The principle that quantity cannot generate a new quality also rules out Le Bon's emergentist analogy of cells forming an organism (77).

Mass psychology is young, but its problems are already vast and poorly delimited. Classifying the forms of mass formation and describing their phenomena has produced a large literature. Freud's small book will treat only a few questions in which depth psychology has a special interest (75). The restriction is substantive. Freud declines the morphology of masses here and again in chapter VI; he wants their libidinal structure. The second half of the title states the real yield: the book's lasting results, the ego ideal and the theory of identification, belong to the analysis of the ego, for which mass psychology supplies the occasion.

## II. Le Bon's Description of the Mass Mind

Rather than open with a definition, Freud begins from the field of phenomena and selects some striking facts as starting points. He takes both from Le Bon's *Psychologie des foules* (1895), a book "rightly famous" (76). Starting from someone else's description lets the libidinal hypothesis enter later as an explanation of facts Freud did not select to suit it.

Freud restates the problem. Suppose individual psychology had completely explained a person's dispositions, drives, motives, and intentions, up to his actions and his relations to those close to him. It would still face a new task: explaining why this now intelligible individual feels, thinks, and acts quite differently under one condition, namely "die Einreihung in eine Menschenmenge, welche die Eigenschaft einer ‚psychologischen Masse' erworben hat" ("his inclusion in a human multitude that has acquired the character of a 'psychological mass'") (76). Three questions follow: what a mass is, how it gains the power to influence individual mental life so decisively, and what mental change it forces on the individual. The qualifier "psychological" carries weight. A multitude is not yet a mass; it must *acquire* that character, and chapter VI will say what it acquires: ties (109).

Answering the three questions is the task of a theoretical mass psychology. The best entry is the third, because the observed change in individual reactions supplies the material, and description must precede explanation (77). The book follows this order. Chapters II and III describe the change; IV and V propose its cause, libido, and with it an answer to the first question. Freud proceeds as analysis always does: from the symptom to the structure that produces it.

Le Bon's first thesis: whatever the individuals composing a psychological mass, however alike or unlike in occupation, character, or intelligence, their mere transformation into a mass gives them a collective soul (*Kollektivseele*), by which they feel, think, and act otherwise than each would alone. The psychological mass is a provisional being made of heterogeneous elements, bound for a moment, exactly as cells combine into an organism with properties none of them had (77). Le Bon's claim is emergentist: the mass is a new being. Freud quotes it to set up the opposition already announced in chapter I. The cell analogy returns transformed when Trotter treats mass formation as a biological continuation of multicellularity (94), and Freud rejects it in chapter IX.

Freud interrupts with his decisive question. If individuals in a mass form a unity, "so muß es wohl etwas geben, was sie aneinander bindet, und dies Bindemittel könnte gerade das sein, was für die Masse charakteristisch ist" ("there must be something that binds them to one another, and this binding agent could be precisely what characterizes the mass") (77). Le Bon leaves this unanswered and passes straight to the change in the individual, though he describes it in terms consistent with depth psychology. The question of the tie replaces the question of the collective soul. Its answer arrives in stages: libido (IV), the double direction of the tie (V), identification through a shared object in the place of the ego ideal (VII–VIII).

Le Bon locates the causes of the change in the unconscious. The difference between the mass member and the isolated individual is easy to state; its causes are hard to find. Modern psychology has shown that unconscious phenomena predominate, in intellectual functions as in organic life. Conscious acts derive from an unconscious substrate created mainly by heredity, which holds the countless ancestral traces that make up the "race soul." Behind the motives we admit lie secret ones we do not admit, and behind those, still more secret ones we do not even know (77–78). The editors note that where the German translation has the finest analysis reaching only a few "conscious" motives, Le Bon's French reads *inconscients*. Le Bon's unconscious is hereditary and racial, a reservoir of ancestry. Freud will grant it a place and refuse it the decisive role.

In the mass, Le Bon says, the individual acquisitions of its members are erased, and their particularity with them; the racial unconscious comes forward and the heterogeneous sinks into the homogeneous. Freud restates this in his own terms: "der psychische Oberbau, der sich bei den Einzelnen so verschiedenartig entwickelt hat, wird abgetragen, entkräftet und das bei allen gleichartige unbewußte Fundament wird bloßgelegt (wirksam gemacht)" ("the psychical superstructure, developed so differently in each individual, is dismantled and weakened, and the unconscious foundation, alike in all, is laid bare (made effective)") (78). He replaces Le Bon's ethnological image with a topographical one: what distinguishes individuals is late and acquired, what they share is basic and unconscious. The parenthesis matters. The foundation is not merely exposed to view but set in operation.

This would yield an average character. But Le Bon finds that mass members also show new properties, and he names three causes. The first: through sheer number, the individual in a mass gains a feeling of invincible power, which lets him yield to drives he would have curbed alone; and since the mass is anonymous and so irresponsible, the sense of responsibility that always restrains individuals vanishes (78–79).

Freud declines to stress new properties. It is enough to say that the individual in a mass finds himself in conditions that allow him "die Verdrängungen seiner unbewußten Triebregungen abzuwerfen" ("to throw off the repressions of his unconscious drive impulses"). The apparently new traits express the unconscious, "in dem ja alles Böse der Menschenseele in der Anlage enthalten ist" ("which contains, as a disposition, all that is evil in the human soul"). The disappearance of conscience poses no difficulty, since analysis long ago held that "der Kern des sogenannten Gewissens sei ‚soziale Angst'" ("the core of so-called conscience is 'social anxiety'") (79). A footnote marks the real difference. Le Bon's unconscious holds above all the deepest features of the race soul, which individual analysis leaves aside. Freud grants that the core of the ego, "the id, as I later called it," which holds the "archaic heritage," is unconscious, but he separates from it the repressed unconscious, which arose from part of that heritage; Le Bon lacks the concept of the repressed (79 n.). Le Bon's unconscious is descriptive and hereditary, Freud's dynamic. The mass therefore creates nothing; it lifts repression. This release model is provisional. From chapter IV on, Freud explains the change by a new tie rather than by the mere removal of an inhibition. Conscience as "social anxiety," fear of the community, also anticipates the mass as substitute authority (92) and the ego ideal (VIII, XI). The footnote's reference to the id postdates *Das Ich und das Es* (1923) and records the book's later revision.

The second cause is contagion. It is easy to establish and inexplicable, and must be counted among phenomena of a hypnotic kind. In a crowd every feeling and act is contagious, to the point that the individual readily sacrifices his personal interest to the collective one: a capacity contrary to his nature, of which a person is capable only as part of a mass (79).

Freud notes that he will later found an important conjecture on this last sentence (80). The sentence is the one about self-sacrifice possible only in a mass. The conjecture is cashed out in chapter IV, where self-sacrifice and the striving for closeness serve as the marks by which aim-diverted love remains recognizable as love (98), and in chapter VI, where the restriction of narcissism in the mass proves a libidinal tie (112).

The third and most important cause is suggestibility, of which contagion is only an effect. Le Bon appeals to physiology: a person can be put by various procedures into a state in which, having lost his conscious personality, he obeys every suggestion of whoever deprived him of it, even to acts contrary to his character. An individual immersed for some time in an active mass soon falls, through emanations or some unknown cause, into a special state close to the fascination of the hypnotized subject. Conscious personality has vanished, will and discernment are absent, and all feelings and thoughts are oriented in the direction set by the hypnotist. As in hypnosis, some faculties are abolished while others are heightened, and the impulse to carry out a suggested act is still more irresistible in the mass, because a suggestion that is the same for all grows by reciprocity (80–81).

Le Bon sums up: the chief marks of the individual in a mass are the vanishing of the conscious personality, the predominance of the unconscious personality, the orientation of thoughts and feelings in one direction by suggestion and contagion, and the tendency to carry out suggested ideas at once. "Das Individuum ist nicht mehr es selbst, es ist ein willenloser Automat geworden" ("The individual is no longer himself; he has become an automaton without a will") (81).

Freud quoted at length to show that Le Bon declares the mass member's state hypnotic, "nicht etwa ihn bloß mit einem solchen vergleicht" ("not merely that he compares it to one") (81). He does not dispute this but splits Le Bon's mechanism. Contagion and heightened suggestibility cannot be of the same kind if contagion is itself an expression of suggestibility, and Le Bon does not keep their effects apart. The best reading refers contagion to the effect of members on one another, while the suggestion phenomena equated with hypnosis point to another source. Which one? Le Bon never mentions a central piece of his own analogy: "die Person, welche für die Masse den Hypnotiseur ersetzt" ("the person who takes the hypnotist's place for the mass") (81). He distinguishes from this fascinating influence, left in the dark, the contagious effect members exert on each other, which reinforces the original suggestion. Freud here separates a horizontal vector (member to member) from a vertical one (hypnotist to subject). These become the two ties of chapter V. He also takes Le Bon's identification of mass and hypnosis literally, and will turn it around: hypnosis is a mass of two (VIII), and the hypnotic relation is explained from the primal horde (X).

Le Bon adds that mere membership in an organized mass makes a person descend several rungs on the ladder of civilization. In isolation he may have been cultivated; in the mass he is a barbarian, a creature of drives, with the spontaneity, violence, and ferocity, but also the enthusiasm and heroism, of primitive beings. He dwells on the lowering of intellectual performance, which Freud illustrates with Schiller's distich: each, seen singly, is passably clever; in corpore, they make one blockhead (81–82 and n.). Freud lets the claim stand because the regression to the primitive links the mass to *Totem und Tabu* and to the primal horde of chapter X. The regression runs in two directions, toward ferocity and toward heroism, and Freud's theory must explain both.

Leaving the individual, Freud turns to Le Bon's picture of the mass mind and asserts that no trait in it would be hard for a psychoanalyst to derive and place. Le Bon himself shows the way by pointing to its agreement with the mental life of primitives and children (82). This sets up the series that organizes the rest of the chapter: mass, primitive, child, neurotic, dreamer, hypnotized subject. Each of Le Bon's traits will be matched to a known mode of functioning of the unconscious.

The mass is impulsive, changeable, and irritable, led almost exclusively by the unconscious. Its impulses may be noble or cruel, heroic or cowardly, but they are so imperious that neither personal interest nor self-preservation prevails. Nothing in it is premeditated; it desires passionately but never for long and cannot sustain a will. It tolerates no delay between desire and fulfilment. It feels omnipotent: for the individual in the mass the notion of the impossible disappears (82). Freud's notes add that Le Bon uses "unconscious" correctly in the descriptive sense, which does not mean only the repressed, and refer the feeling of omnipotence to the omnipotence of thoughts in *Totem und Tabu* III (82 nn.). The intolerance of delay is the pleasure principle before the reality principle has interposed thought between wish and act (the 1911 "Formulierungen"). The descriptive-unconscious note softens the earlier footnote: Le Bon's term is legitimate as description, deficient as theory.

The mass is extraordinarily open to influence and credulous. It lacks criticism; for it the improbable does not exist. It thinks in images that call one another up by association, as in a person's free fantasying, and no reasonable agency measures them against reality. Its feelings are simple and excessive, and it knows neither doubt nor uncertainty (82–83). Freud's note on this last point draws on dream interpretation: the analyst disregards doubt and uncertainty in the dream report and treats every manifest element as equally certain, because doubt derives from the censorship and the primary dream-thoughts do not know doubt as a critical function (83 n.). The note turns a pejorative into a structural claim: the mass lacks doubt because the critical agency is out of action. The missing "reasonable agency" (*verständige Instanz*) points ahead to chapter VIII, where Freud assigns reality testing to the ego ideal and explains its suspension by putting an object in that ideal's place.

The mass goes at once to extremes: a suspicion voiced becomes unshakable certainty, a germ of antipathy becomes savage hatred (83). The note finds the same escalation of every emotion in the child's affectivity and in dreams, where the isolation of single impulses in the unconscious lets a slight annoyance of the day appear as a death wish against its cause. Freud cites Hanns Sachs: having seen in the dream, under the magnifying glass of analysis, a monster, we should not be surprised to find it again in consciousness as an infusorian (83 n.). Extremity is the unconscious impulse acting alone, without the integration that would temper it against others. Sachs's image also cautions: the dream magnifies what waking life keeps small, and the mass, like the dream, removes the reduction.

Being inclined to every extreme, the mass is excited only by excessive stimuli. Whoever wants to move it needs no logical weighing of arguments; he must paint in the strongest images, exaggerate, and repeat the same thing again and again (83). Demagogic technique addresses the image-thinking just described, not judgment.

Because the mass has no doubt about what is true or false and is conscious of its great strength, it is as intolerant as it is credulous toward authority. It respects force and is only mildly moved by kindness, which it takes for weakness. It demands strength, even violence, from its heroes; it wants to be ruled and suppressed and to fear its master. At bottom wholly conservative, it abhors innovation and progress and reveres tradition without limit (83–84). Freud adds nothing yet. The wish to be ruled, here a bare datum of description, becomes in chapter X the mass's "thirst for obedience," derived from the members' relation to the primal father.

To judge the morality of masses one must remember that when mass members come together all individual inhibitions fall away. The cruel, brutal, destructive instincts that slumber in each person as relics of primal times are awakened to free satisfaction. Yet under the influence of suggestion masses are also capable of great renunciation, unselfishness, and devotion to an ideal. Personal advantage, almost the sole motive of the isolated individual, rarely prevails in masses, and one can speak of the individual being moralized by the mass. The mass's intellect always stands well below the individual's, but its ethical conduct may rise as far above that level as it may sink below it (84). Le Bon's description is morally ambivalent, and Freud's explanation must cover both poles with one mechanism. It will: when the members put the same object in the place of their ego ideal (VIII), they lose individual conscience and gain a shared ideal, so that crime and sacrifice issue from the same structure.

Other traits confirm the identification of the mass mind with the primitive mind. In masses the most contradictory ideas coexist without conflict arising from their logical opposition, as analysis has long shown for the unconscious mental life of individuals, children, and neurotics (84). The long note supplies the norm against which the mass is a regression. In small children ambivalent attitudes toward those closest to them coexist for a long time without either disturbing the other's expression; when conflict finally comes, the child often settles it by changing objects, displacing one of the impulses onto a substitute. In adult neurosis a suppressed impulse may survive for years in fantasies that contradict a dominant striving, until an increase in its cathexis brings on conflict with the ego. Development as a whole brings a growing integration of the personality, a gathering of drive impulses and aims that grew up independently, analogous to the gathering of the sexual drives under genital organization. The unification of the ego can suffer the same disturbances as that of the libido, as in natural scientists who remain believers in the Bible (84–85 n.). The note states the book's second theme in miniature: the ego is a synthesis, and the mass undoes it. Displacement of hostility onto a substitute object anticipates the outsider who absorbs the mass's aggression (107, 111).

The mass is also subject to the truly magical power of words, which can raise the most terrible storms in the mass mind and calm them. Against certain words and formulas reason and argument are powerless; when they are pronounced with reverence, faces turn respectful and heads bow, and many treat them as natural or supernatural powers. Freud need only recall the taboo on names among primitives and the magical powers attached to names and words, discussed in *Totem und Tabu* (85 and n.). The word acts as a thing, not as a sign to be weighed. This places the mass's language beside the omnipotence of thoughts just mentioned.

Finally, masses have never known thirst for truth. They demand illusions they cannot give up. The unreal always takes precedence for them over the real and affects them almost as strongly; they tend visibly to make no distinction between the two (85).

Freud names this precisely. The dominance of fantasy and of illusion sustained by unfulfilled wishes is what analysis found decisive in the neuroses: for neurotics "nicht die gemeine objektive, sondern die psychische Realität" ("not ordinary objective reality but psychical reality") counts. A hysterical symptom rests on fantasy rather than on the repetition of real experience, and obsessional guilt rests on an evil intention never carried out. "Ja, wie im Traum und in der Hypnose, tritt in der Seelentätigkeit der Masse die Realitätsprüfung zurück gegen die Stärke der affektiv besetzten Wunschregungen" ("Indeed, as in dreams and in hypnosis, in the mental activity of the mass reality testing recedes before the strength of affectively cathected wishes") (85–86). The series closes on its decisive term: what mass, neurosis, dream, and hypnosis share is the retreat of reality testing. Placing hypnosis in the series prepares chapter VIII, where the hypnotist occupies the ego ideal that performs reality testing. "Illusion," a belief carried by a wish, prepares chapter V's "illusion" of the leader's equal love, and *Die Zukunft einer Illusion* (1927).

What Le Bon says about leaders is less complete and lets the underlying law show through less clearly. As soon as living beings gather in some number, herd of animals or human crowd, they place themselves instinctively under the authority of a chief. The mass is an obedient herd that cannot live without a master; it thirsts so for obedience that it submits instinctively to anyone who appoints himself its master (86). Le Bon already supplies the herd image; chapter IX will correct it by calling man a horde animal rather than a herd animal, a creature led by a chief rather than merely gregarious.

The mass's need meets the leader halfway, but he must answer it with personal qualities. He must himself be fascinated by a strong belief in an idea in order to awaken belief in the mass, and he must have a strong, imposing will, which the will-less mass takes over. Le Bon discusses kinds of leaders and their means; on the whole the leaders gain importance through the ideas they are fanatical about (86). Le Bon's leader is himself captured by an idea. Chapter X will draw the opposite portrait: the primal father, model of the leader, loves no one but himself and is absolutely narcissistic and independent. Freud's leader holds the mass by needing nothing from it; Le Bon's holds it by a borrowed fanaticism.

Le Bon attributes to these ideas and leaders a mysterious, irresistible power he calls "prestige," a kind of domination exerted over us by an individual, a work, or an idea. It paralyses our capacity for criticism and fills us with astonishment and respect, arousing a feeling similar to the fascination of hypnosis (86). The hypnotic vocabulary returns on the vertical axis that Le Bon's account of the mass left dark (81). Chapter IV will say outright that prestige is suggestion under another name (96).

Le Bon distinguishes acquired or artificial prestige from personal prestige. The first is conferred on persons by name, wealth, and reputation, and on views and works of art by tradition; since it always reaches back to the past, it will do little to explain this enigmatic influence. Personal prestige belongs to few people, makes them leaders, and secures obedience as if by a magnetic spell. Yet all prestige depends on success and is lost by failure (86–87). Acquired prestige presupposes the power it should explain. The "magnetic spell" recalls the mesmerist ancestry of hypnosis, and chapter X will trace the leader's uncanny force to the primal father, before whom the gaze of the hypnotist and the mana of the chief first appeared.

Freud's verdict: one does not get the impression that Le Bon has brought the role of leaders and the emphasis on prestige into proper harmony with his brilliant description of the mass mind (87). Le Bon's two halves, the mass mind and the leader, stand side by side unconnected. Freud's theory will join them by making the leader constitutive of the mass rather than an addition to it.

## III. Other Assessments of Collective Mental Life

Freud used Le Bon as an introduction because his emphasis on unconscious mental life coincides with psychoanalysis. But none of Le Bon's assertions is new. Everything disparaging he says about the mass mind had been said before him, as decidedly and as hostilely, by thinkers, statesmen, and poets since the earliest literature. His two most important propositions, the collective inhibition of intellectual performance and the heightening of affectivity in the mass, had been formulated shortly before by Sighele. Only the appeal to the unconscious and the comparison with primitives remain his own, and these too had been touched on before (88). Freud reduces Le Bon to two theses, which become the "two propositions" he takes as the fundamental fact of mass psychology in chapter IV (95). He also marks the tone of the tradition as hostile, which tells the reader to separate its observations from its verdicts.

Moreover, the description has been contested. The phenomena are correctly observed, but opposite effects of mass formation can also be recognized, from which a much higher estimate of the mass mind follows (89).

Le Bon himself conceded that the morality of a mass can be higher than that of its members, and that only collectivities are capable of great unselfishness and devotion: while personal advantage is almost the sole motive of the isolated individual, it rarely prevails in masses (89). Le Bon's concession undercuts his own disparaging picture and opens the space for the opposing assessments.

Others argue that society alone prescribes the norms of morality to the individual, who as a rule falls somewhat short of them; or that in exceptional states a collectivity produces enthusiasm, which has made possible the grandest collective achievements (89). The first argument makes morality social in origin. Freud will absorb it in his own terms: the ego ideal is formed after social models, and each individual takes part in many mass minds (XI).

On intellect, it remains true that the great decisions of thought, the momentous discoveries and solutions, are possible only for the individual working in solitude. Yet the mass mind too is capable of creations of genius, as language itself shows, and folk song and folklore. It also remains open how much the individual thinker or poet owes to the stimulus of the mass in which he lives, and whether he is more than the completer of mental work in which others took part at the same time (89). Freud leaves the question undecided. He returns to it in the Postscript (XII B), where the epic poet who invents the heroic myth takes the first step out of mass psychology.

Faced with such complete contradictions, mass psychology might seem doomed to come to nothing. But there is an easy way out. Very different formations have probably been lumped together as "masses" and need to be separated. Sighele, Le Bon, and others describe short-lived masses, quickly assembled from dissimilar individuals by a passing interest; the revolutionary masses, especially of the French Revolution, plainly shaped their accounts. The opposite claims derive from stable masses or associations in which people spend their lives and which are embodied in society's institutions. The first kind sits on the second "wie die kurzen, aber hohen Wellen den langen Dünungen der See" ("like the short but high waves on the long swells of the sea") (89–90). The contradiction is resolved by a typology. The image also ranks the terms: institutions are the ground, crowds a disturbance on its surface. This justifies chapter V's choice of church and army as paradigms.

McDougall, in *The Group Mind* (1920), starts from the same contradiction and resolves it by organization. In the simplest case a group has no organization worth mentioning; he calls it a crowd. He concedes that a crowd hardly assembles without at least the beginnings of organization, and that basic facts of collective psychology are especially visible in such simple groups. For a chance gathering to become a group in the psychological sense, its members must have something in common: a shared interest in an object, a similar emotional inclination in some situation, "and (I would insert: consequently)" a certain capacity to influence one another. The greater this mental homogeneity, the more easily a psychological group forms and the more strikingly a "group mind" appears (90–91). Freud's inserted "infolgedessen" changes the causal order. Reciprocal influence, that is suggestion, does not stand beside the common emotional bond; it follows from it. This is the thesis of chapter IV in a parenthesis.

The most remarkable and most important phenomenon of mass formation is the heightening of each member's affectivity. People's emotions rarely rise so high under other conditions, McDougall says, and the participants find it pleasurable to abandon themselves without limit to their passions, merge in the mass, and lose the sense of their individual boundaries. McDougall explains this being carried away by the "principle of direct induction of emotion by way of the primitive sympathetic response," which is the familiar contagion of feeling: perceived signs of an affect automatically arouse the same affect in the perceiver, the more strongly the more people show it at once. Criticism falls silent and the individual slides into the affect, raising in turn the excitement of those who acted on him, so that the charge mounts by mutual induction. "Es ist unverkennbar etwas wie ein Zwang dabei wirksam, es den anderen gleichzutun, im Einklang mit den Vielen zu bleiben" ("Something like a compulsion is unmistakably at work here, to do as the others do, to remain in harmony with the many"). The cruder and simpler emotions spread most easily (91). Two details matter for what follows. The loss of boundaries is enjoyed, which points to a libidinal gain. And the compulsion to stay in harmony with the many is what Freud will reinterpret as doing something "for love of them" (100).

Other influences from the mass favour this mechanism. The mass gives the individual the impression of unlimited power and invincible danger. For the moment it has taken the place of the whole of human society, the bearer of authority, whose punishments one feared and for whose sake one imposed so many inhibitions on oneself. Contradicting it is dangerous; following the example all around is safe, even if it means "howling with the wolves." In obedience to the new authority one may put one's former "conscience" out of action and yield to the lure of the pleasure gained by lifting inhibitions. So it is not surprising to see individuals in a mass do or approve things they would have shunned, and one may hope thereby to dispel some of the darkness "die man mit dem Rätselwort der ‚Suggestion' zu decken pflegt" ("that is usually covered by the enigmatic word 'suggestion'") (91–92). The paragraph blends McDougall's report with Freud's gloss, and the gloss carries the thought. Conscience is not abolished in the mass; it is transferred to a new authority. This links the earlier "social anxiety" (79) to the later thesis that the mass exchanges the ego ideal for a common object (VIII, XI).

McDougall does not contest the collective inhibition of intelligence. The lesser intelligences drag the greater down to their level. The greater are hampered because heightened affect is unfavourable to correct mental work, because individuals are intimidated by the mass and their thinking is not free, and because each member's sense of responsibility for his performance is reduced (92). These are rationalist explanations, and chapter IV dismisses them as insufficient (95).

McDougall's overall judgment on the simple, "unorganized" group is no kinder than Le Bon's. Such a group is excessively excitable, impulsive, passionate, fickle, inconsistent, irresolute and yet ready for extreme acts, open only to the coarser passions and simpler feelings, extremely suggestible, careless in deliberation, hasty in judgment, receptive only to the simplest and most imperfect arguments, easily led and shaken, without self-consciousness, self-respect, or sense of responsibility, but ready to be carried by its consciousness of strength into any outrage. It behaves like an unruly child or an untutored passionate savage in a strange situation, and in the worst cases more like a pack of wild animals than like human beings (92–93). The comparison with an *unsupervised* savage names exactly what the mass lacks: a supervising agency. Freud will locate it in the ego ideal.

Since McDougall contrasts the behaviour of highly organized groups with this picture, one wants to know what such organization consists of and what produces it. McDougall lists five "principal conditions" for raising the mental life of a group to a higher level (93).

The first and basic condition is some continuity in the group's existence. It may be material, when the same persons remain in the group for a long time, or formal, when the group develops fixed positions assigned to persons who succeed one another (93). Formal continuity is the institution, which outlasts its members. Church and army possess it, and it is one reason Freud chooses them in chapter V.

The second condition is that each member has formed some idea of the nature, function, achievements, and claims of the group, so that he can develop an emotional relation to the group as a whole (93). This is the only condition that is explicitly affective. It makes "the whole" an object of feeling. Freud will ask what fills that place, a leader or a leading idea (VI).

The third condition is that the group be brought into relation with other groups similar to it yet different in many respects, for instance by rivalry (93). Rivalry with neighbours as a source of cohesion anticipates chapter VI's analysis of hostility between closely related groups and of the outsider as target.

The fourth condition is that the group possess traditions, customs, and institutions, especially those regulating the relations of its members to one another (93). Tradition was the basis of Le Bon's acquired prestige; McDougall makes it a stabilizer rather than a mystery.

The fifth condition is an internal articulation expressed in the specialization and differentiation of each member's work (94). The army's hierarchy of units, discussed in chapter V, is the clearest case.

According to McDougall, meeting these conditions removes the psychological disadvantages of mass formation. One protects against the collective lowering of intelligence by withdrawing intellectual tasks from the mass and reserving them to individuals within it (94). Organization does not raise the mass's intellect; it routes around it. The basic fact stands.

Freud redescribes McDougall's "organization." The task is to give the mass precisely those properties that characterized the individual and were extinguished in him by mass formation. Outside the primitive mass the individual had his continuity, his self-consciousness, his traditions and habits, his particular work and position, and he kept himself apart from others with whom he competed. He lost this particularity on entering the unorganized mass. If the aim is to furnish the mass with the attributes of the individual, one is reminded of Trotter's remark that the tendency to form masses is a biological continuation of the multicellularity of all higher organisms (94). A footnote, answering Hans Kelsen's review (*Imago*, 1922), denies that such an endowment of the "mass mind" with organization hypostatizes it, that is, grants it independence from mental processes in individuals (94 n.). The redescription matches McDougall's five conditions to five attributes of the person. Organization means that the mass borrows the form of an ego. Mass and ego thus become mirror problems, which is why the book's title joins them. The Kelsen note guards against the reading Le Bon invited: the mass mind is not a subject over and above its members. Trotter's biological analogy is admitted here only as a remark and is rejected as an explanation in chapter IX.

## IV. Suggestion and Libido

Freud restates the basic fact. Within a mass the individual undergoes an often profound change in his mental activity: his affectivity is greatly heightened and his intellectual performance markedly reduced, both in the direction of becoming like the other members. This can be achieved only by lifting the drive inhibitions peculiar to each and by renouncing the particular shapes of his inclinations. Higher "organization" can partly prevent these often undesirable effects, but it does not contradict the two propositions of affective heightening and inhibition of thought in the primitive mass. What is needed is a psychological explanation of the change (95). Two words reframe the problem. *Angleichung*, becoming alike, describes the change as an assimilation, which identification will explain (VII). *Verzicht*, renunciation, adds to the lifting of inhibitions an act of giving up, which requires a motive.

Rational factors such as the intimidation just mentioned, the action of the drive for self-preservation, plainly do not cover the phenomena. What the authors on sociology and mass psychology otherwise offer is always the same, under changing names: "das Zauberwort der Suggestion" ("the magic word of suggestion") (95). The phrase is pointed. The theory explains the mass by the very kind of word, magical and unexamined, that the mass itself obeys (85).

Tarde called it imitation, but an author (Brugeilles, 1913) rightly objects that imitation falls under suggestion and follows from it. Le Bon traced everything strange in social phenomena to two factors, mutual suggestion among individuals and the prestige of leaders; but prestige shows itself only in the power to produce suggestion. McDougall's principle of "primary induction of emotion" seemed for a moment to make suggestion superfluous, but on reflection it says no more than the familiar claims about imitation or contagion, only with stress on affect. That we tend to fall into the affect whose signs we see in another is beyond doubt. But how often do we resist it, reject the affect, even react in the opposite way? Why then do we regularly give in to this contagion in a mass? Once again one will have to say that the suggestive influence of the mass compels us to obey the tendency to imitate. And McDougall too, like the others, says that masses are notably suggestible (96). Freud's argument is that every proposed mechanism presupposes a disposition to yield that it does not explain. Contagion is not automatic, since outside the mass we often resist it. The question is what in the mass disarms resistance, and the answer, love, will be given at the end of the chapter.

This prepares the claim that suggestion, more precisely suggestibility, is an irreducible "Urphänomen," a basic fact of human mental life. Bernheim held this view, and Freud witnessed his astonishing feats in 1889. Even then he felt a dull opposition to "diese Tyrannei der Suggestion" ("this tyranny of suggestion"). When a patient who proved unyielding was shouted at, "Vous vous contre-suggestionnez!", Freud told himself this was a plain injustice and an act of violence; the man surely had a right to countersuggestions if one was trying to subdue him with suggestions. His resistance later turned into a revolt against the idea that suggestion, which explained everything, should itself be exempt from explanation. He recalled the old riddle: Christopher carried Christ, Christ carried the whole world; where did Christopher put his foot? (96–97). Two objections meet here, one ethical (suggestion as domination over a subject who has a right to resist) and one epistemic (an explanation that explains everything but rests on nothing). The riddle states the second exactly: what carries the carrier? The memory is also autobiographical. Freud translated Bernheim and left hypnotic suggestion for analysis in the 1890s; the book now pays an old debt by explaining suggestion instead of using it.

Returning after some thirty years to the enigma of suggestion, Freud finds nothing changed, with one exception that itself shows the influence of psychoanalysis. (The reference is most likely to Ferenczi's "Introjektion und Übertragung," 1909, which traced suggestibility to the parental complex.) Efforts have gone into fixing the concept by convention, as in McDougall's 1920 note on suggestion, and these are not superfluous, since the word is drifting toward meaning any influence whatever, as English "to suggest" already does. But on the essence of suggestion, "über die Bedingungen, unter denen sich Beeinflussungen ohne zureichende logische Begründung herstellen" ("the conditions under which influences arise without sufficient logical grounds"), no clarification has come. Freud would review the literature of thirty years to prove this, but a detailed study is being prepared nearby; a 1924 addition notes that it never appeared (97–98 and n.). Freud's definition is negative: suggestion is influence in the absence of sufficient reasons. The question is what positive force fills the gap that reasons leave.

Instead, Freud will try to clarify mass psychology with the concept of libido, which proved so useful in the study of the psychoneuroses (98). The method of the neuroses transfers to the mass, as chapter II's parallels already suggested.

Libido is a term from the theory of affects. "Wir heißen so die als quantitative Größe betrachtete — wenn auch derzeit nicht meßbare — Energie solcher Triebe, welche mit all dem zu tun haben, was man als Liebe zusammenfassen kann" ("We call by this name the energy, regarded as a quantity, though not at present measurable, of those drives that have to do with everything that may be comprised under the word 'love'") (98). Its core is what is commonly called love and what poets sing of, sexual love aiming at sexual union. But psychoanalysis does not separate from it anything else that shares the name: self-love, parental and filial love, friendship, love of humanity, devotion to concrete objects and to abstract ideas. The justification is that analysis has shown all these to be expressions of the same drive impulses that push toward sexual union between the sexes. In other relations they are diverted from that aim or held up in reaching it, yet they always keep enough of their original nature to remain recognizable, in self-sacrifice and the striving for closeness (98). The distinction between "diverted" (*abgedrängt*) and "held up" (*aufgehalten*) anticipates the concept of aim-inhibited drives (XII C). The two marks of identity, self-sacrifice and the striving for closeness, are exactly the traits Le Bon found in the mass, where a person sacrifices his interest as he could not alone (79). This is the "important conjecture" promised on page 80: the mass's self-sacrifice is the sign of love.

Language, with the word "love" in all its uses, has made a fully justified synthesis, and science can do no better than adopt it. Psychoanalysis provoked a storm of indignation by doing so, as if guilty of sacrilegious innovation. Yet the "extended" conception of love is nothing original. Plato's Eros coincides completely with the libido of psychoanalysis in its origin, its function, and its relation to sexual love, as Nachmansohn and Pfister have shown; and when the Apostle Paul praises love above all else in the letter to the Corinthians, he surely meant it in the same extended sense. One learns only that people do not always take their great thinkers seriously, even when they claim to admire them (98–99). Freud legitimates a technical concept by ordinary language and by the philosophical tradition at once. After *Jenseits des Lustprinzips* Eros has become the name for the life drives as such, which the end of this chapter will invoke. The Pauline reference is pointed: the love of 1 Corinthians 13 is the founding love of the church, the first artificial mass of chapter V.

Psychoanalysis calls these love drives sexual drives *a potiori*, from their principal member, and from their origin. Most "educated" people took this as an insult and avenged themselves with the charge of "pansexualism." Those who find sexuality shameful and degrading are free to use the more genteel terms Eros and erotic. Freud could have done so from the start and spared himself much opposition, but he would not, "denn ich vermeide gern Konzessionen an die Schwachmütigkeit. Man kann nicht wissen, wohin man auf diesem Wege gerät; man gibt zuerst in Worten nach und dann allmählich auch in der Sache" ("for I prefer to avoid concessions to faintheartedness. One cannot tell where that path leads; one gives way first in words and then gradually in substance"). Eros, meant to soften the offence, is only the translation of the German *Liebe*, and whoever can wait need make no concessions (99–100). The polemical target is the desexualization of libido, above all by Jung. The terminological stand is a theoretical one: naming the love drives by their origin keeps the genetic claim that social ties derive from the sexual drive rather than running parallel to it.

Freud states his hypothesis: "daß Liebesbeziehungen (indifferent ausgedrückt: Gefühlsbindungen) auch das Wesen der Massenseele ausmachen" ("that love relationships (to use a neutral expression: emotional ties) also constitute the essence of the mass mind") (100). The authors never mention them; what would correspond to them is hidden "hinter dem Schirm, der spanischen Wand, der Suggestion" ("behind the screen, the folding screen, of suggestion"). Two passing thoughts support the expectation. First, a mass is evidently held together by some power, and to what power could this be better ascribed than to Eros, "der alles in der Welt zusammenhält" ("which holds everything in the world together")? Second, when the individual gives up his particularity in the mass and lets others suggest things to him, one has the impression he does so because he needs to be in agreement with them rather than opposed to them, perhaps "ihnen zuliebe" ("for love of them") (100). The two supports answer the first two questions of chapter II: what binds the mass (Eros), and why the individual changes in it (to be at one with the others). The neutral "emotional tie" lets Freud speak without sexual connotation while keeping the thesis. "Suggestion" is now a screen concept, a cover for what it hides, much as a screen memory conceals the memory behind it. And *ihnen zuliebe* turns McDougall's compulsion to stay in harmony with the many (91) into a gift of love.

## V. Two Artificial Masses: Church and Army

The morphology of masses distinguishes many kinds and opposite tendencies of development: fleeting and highly durable masses, homogeneous and heterogeneous ones, natural masses and artificial ones that need external compulsion to hold together, primitive and articulated, highly organized ones. For reasons "in welche die Einsicht noch verhüllt ist" ("into which our insight is still veiled"), Freud stresses a distinction the authors neglect: between leaderless masses and masses with leaders. And contrary to usual practice he begins not with a relatively simple formation but with highly organized, durable, artificial masses, the most interesting being the church, the community of believers, and the army (101). The method reverses the usual order: the complex case first, because it displays the structure plainly, as pathology displays the normal. The veiled reasons are those of chapter X. If the primal horde is the original mass and the leader is original to it, the mass with a leader is the primary form and the leaderless mass the derivative.

Church and army are artificial masses: a certain external compulsion is applied to keep them from dissolving and to prevent changes in their structure. As a rule one is not asked, or not given the choice, whether to join; attempts to leave are usually pursued, severely punished, or tied to definite conditions. A note adds that the attributes "stable" and "artificial" seem to coincide in masses, or at least to be closely connected. Why such associations need such safeguards is of no present interest. What matters is that in these highly organized masses, protected from disintegration in this way, certain relations can be seen with great clarity that are far more concealed elsewhere (101–102 and n.). The note concedes a limit: durable masses do not sustain themselves by libido alone. The artificial mass serves as a magnifying lens, not as evidence that compulsion explains cohesion.

In the church, with the Catholic Church as model, and in the army, however different, the same pretence (illusion) holds: "daß ein Oberhaupt da ist — in der katholischen Kirche Christus, in der Armee der Feldherr, — das alle Einzelnen der Masse mit der gleichen Liebe liebt" ("that there is a head, Christ in the Catholic Church, the commander-in-chief in the army, who loves all the individuals of the mass with an equal love"). "An dieser Illusion hängt alles" ("Everything depends on this illusion"): were it dropped, both church and army would dissolve at once, so far as external compulsion allowed (102). Christ's equal love is stated expressly: "Was ihr getan habt einem unter diesen meinen geringsten Brüdern, das habt ihr mir getan" (Matthew 25:40). He stands to the faithful as a kind elder brother and is a father substitute for them. All demands on individuals derive from this love. A democratic strain runs through the church because all are equal before Christ and have an equal share of his love; the faithful call themselves brothers in Christ, brothers through the love he has for them. "Es ist nicht zu bezweifeln, daß die Bindung jedes Einzelnen an Christus auch die Ursache ihrer Bindung untereinander ist" ("There is no doubt that the tie of each individual to Christ is also the cause of their tie to one another") (102). The same holds for the army: the commander is the father who loves all his soldiers equally, and therefore they are comrades. The army differs structurally in being built as a hierarchy of such masses, each captain the commander and father of his company, each non-commissioned officer of his section. The church has a similar hierarchy, but it plays no comparable economic role, since Christ can be credited with more knowledge of and care for individuals than a human commander (102–103). Three moves carry the chapter. Fraternity is derived: the horizontal tie is caused by the vertical one. The cohesion rests on an "illusion," a belief carried by a wish (85), which looks ahead to *Die Zukunft einer Illusion*. And the hierarchy is explained economically: a human commander cannot distribute equal love to thousands, so the army multiplies fathers. Equality of love is the seed of the demand for equality and justice that chapter IX derives from the rivalry among siblings. Christ as "elder brother" and father substitute points to *Totem und Tabu*, where the Christian son-religion replaces the father, and to the hero of the Postscript (XII B).

One will object that this account of the army's libidinal structure omits the ideas of fatherland, national glory, and the like, so important for its cohesion. Freud answers that this is a different and less simple case of mass tie, and the examples of great commanders, Caesar, Wallenstein, Napoleon, show such ideas are not indispensable. The possible replacement of the leader by a leading idea will be discussed briefly later. Neglecting the libidinal factor in the army, even where it is not the only one at work, is not just a theoretical flaw but a practical danger. Prussian militarism, as unpsychological as German science, may have learned this in the war. The war neuroses that ate away the German army were largely recognized as the individual's protest against the role imposed on him, and according to Ernst Simmel the loveless treatment of the common soldier by his superiors stood first among the causes. Had this libidinal claim been better understood, the fantastic promises of the American President's Fourteen Points would not have been believed so readily, and the great instrument would not have broken in the hands of the German war-makers (103). The theory meets a historical test in the collapse of 1918. The war neuroses, which Freud had treated in the 1919 introduction to *Psychoanalysis and the War Neuroses* as a conflict between a peacetime and a warrior ego, appear here as withdrawal of love from leaders who gave none. Wilson's promises then capture the libido set free, as a rival object.

In both artificial masses each individual is libidinally bound on one side to the leader (Christ, the commander) and on the other to the other members. How these two ties relate, whether they are of the same kind and value and how they should be described psychologically, is reserved for later. But Freud already ventures a mild reproach: the authors have not appreciated the leader's significance for mass psychology, while the choice of his first object of study has put him in a better position. He believes he is on the right path to explaining the main phenomenon of mass psychology, "die Unfreiheit des Einzelnen in der Masse" ("the individual's lack of freedom in the mass"). If each individual is tied so extensively in two directions, the observed change and restriction of his personality will be easy to derive (104). Lack of freedom is now the explanandum, and its explanation is economic: a personality that invests so much libido in two directions has less left for itself, as with the impoverishment of the ego in being in love (VIII). The question left open receives its answer in chapters VII and VIII: the two ties differ in kind, identification among members and an object in place of the ego ideal toward the leader (summarized in XII A).

The phenomenon of panic, best studied in military masses, gives a further hint that the essence of a mass lies in its libidinal ties. Panic arises when such a mass disintegrates. No order from a superior is heeded any longer, and each looks after himself without regard for the others. The mutual ties have ceased and a huge, senseless anxiety is released. The objection lies near that the reverse is true: the anxiety grew so large that it overrode all regard and all ties. McDougall even uses panic, though non-military, as the model case of affect heightened by contagion. But this rational explanation misses the point. What needs explaining is why the anxiety became so huge. The size of the danger cannot be blamed, since the same army that now panics may have withstood equal or greater dangers flawlessly, and it belongs to the essence of panic to stand in no proportion to the threat and to break out on the slightest occasion. When the individual in panic sets about caring for himself alone, he shows that he has grasped that the affective ties which until then reduced the danger for him have ceased. Facing the danger alone, he may well rate it higher. Thus "daß die panische Angst die Lockerung in der libidinösen Struktur der Masse voraussetzt und in berechtigter Weise auf sie reagiert" ("panic anxiety presupposes the loosening of the libidinal structure of the mass and is a justified reaction to it"), not the other way round (104–105). Freud reverses the causal direction and also judges the anxiety appropriate. The danger has not grown; the protection has gone. The tie functioned as a shield, and its loss is a real increase of exposure.

This does not deny that anxiety in a mass grows enormously by induction. McDougall's account is entirely right where the danger is really great and no strong emotional ties exist, as when fire breaks out in a theatre. The instructive case is the army that panics when the danger has not exceeded what it has often endured. The word "panic" is not sharply defined: sometimes it means any mass fear, sometimes an individual's fear beyond all measure, often fear unjustified by its occasion. Taking it as mass fear, a far-reaching analogy holds. Individual anxiety arises either from the size of a danger or from the abandonment of emotional ties (libidinal cathexes); the latter is neurotic anxiety (Lecture XXV). Likewise panic arises from an increase of the danger facing all or from the ceasing of the ties that hold the mass together, and the latter is analogous to neurotic anxiety. Freud cites Béla von Felszeghy's "rich, somewhat fanciful" essay on panic (105–106). The analogy rests on the theory of anxiety of 1917, in which neurotic anxiety is libido transformed when its object is lost. Panic is the collective form of that transformation. *Hemmung, Symptom und Angst* (1926) later revises the theory, making anxiety a signal of danger; the analysis of panic survives the revision because the loss of the tie is itself the danger.

If, like McDougall, one describes panic as one of the clearest achievements of the "group mind," one arrives at a paradox: in one of its most striking manifestations the mass mind abolishes itself. Panic undoubtedly means the disintegration of the mass; it ends all the consideration the members otherwise show one another (106). McDougall's choice of example refutes his concept. What he cites as the group mind at its most intense is the group mind's dissolution.

The typical occasion of panic resembles the scene in Nestroy's parody of Hebbel's *Judith*, where a soldier cries "Der Feldherr hat den Kopf verloren" ("The general has lost his head"), and all the Assyrians flee. Loss of the leader in any sense, losing faith in him, brings panic while the danger stays the same. With the tie to the leader the members' mutual ties also vanish, as a rule. The mass bursts apart like a Bologna flask with its tip broken off (106). Nestroy's pun covers both senses at once: Holofernes literally beheaded, and the commander losing his nerve. "In any sense" makes the leader's psychical function the point, not his body. The confirmation is of chapter V's main claim: when the vertical tie goes, the horizontal ones go with it. The Bologna flask, a thick-walled glass vessel that shatters when its tip is scratched, is a precise image: a structure held by internal tension collapses entirely from one small break.

The disintegration of a religious mass is harder to observe. Freud recently came across an English novel of Catholic origin, recommended by the Bishop of London, titled *When It Was Dark* (Guy Thorne, 1903), which depicts such a possibility skilfully and, he thinks, plausibly. Enemies of Christ and Christianity contrive the discovery in Jerusalem of a tomb whose inscription has Joseph of Arimathea confess that, out of piety, he secretly removed Christ's body on the third day and buried it there. The resurrection and Christ's divinity are thereby disposed of. The archaeological discovery shakes European culture and brings an extraordinary increase in violence and crime, which subsides only when the forgers' plot is exposed (106–107). Where observation fails, Freud uses fiction as a thought experiment. The forged tomb removes precisely the illusion on which "everything depends" (102).

What emerges in this imagined dissolution of the religious mass is not anxiety, for which there is no occasion, but ruthless and hostile impulses toward other people, which until then could not express themselves thanks to Christ's equal love. A note refers to Paul Federn's account of similar phenomena after the fall of paternal state authority, *Die vaterlose Gesellschaft* (1919). Even during Christ's reign those outside the community of believers, who do not love him and whom he does not love, stand outside the tie. Therefore "muß eine Religion, auch wenn sie sich die Religion der Liebe heißt, hart und lieblos gegen diejenigen sein, die ihr nicht angehören" ("a religion, even if it calls itself the religion of love, must be hard and loveless toward those who do not belong to it"). Every religion is such a religion of love for all it embraces, and cruelty and intolerance toward outsiders lie close to every one. Believers should not be reproached too severely; unbelievers and the indifferent have it psychologically much easier here. If this intolerance is less violent and cruel today than in earlier centuries, this hardly shows a softening of morals; the cause is rather the undeniable weakening of religious feeling and of the libidinal ties depending on it. If another mass tie takes the place of the religious one, as the socialist tie now seems to, the same intolerance toward outsiders will follow as in the age of religious wars; and if differences of scientific opinion ever gained similar importance for masses, the same result would recur (107–108 and n.). The two dissolutions release different things: the army anxiety, the church aggression. The love of Christ was holding hostility in check, which presupposes the ambivalence chapter VI will establish. The boundary of love is also the line along which hate is directed outward. Federn's "fatherless society" names the political case after 1918, the fall of the Habsburg father. The extension to socialism and to science, psychoanalysis's own movement included, makes the structure independent of content. *Das Unbehagen in der Kultur* (V) states the corollary: a number of people can be bound in love as long as others remain to receive their aggression.

## VI. Further Problems and Lines of Work

Two artificial masses have been examined and found governed by two kinds of emotional tie, of which the tie to the leader seems, at least for these masses, more decisive than the tie among members (109). The qualification "at least for them" limits the result to masses with leaders. For the leaderless mass the priority of the vertical tie remains to be shown.

Much remains for the morphology of masses. It would start from the point that a mere multitude is not yet a mass so long as these ties have not formed in it, while conceding that in any multitude the tendency to form a psychological mass appears very easily. It would study the various more or less durable masses that arise spontaneously, and the conditions of their formation and decay. Above all it would examine the difference between masses with and without leaders: whether masses with leaders are the more original and complete; whether in the others the leader can be replaced by an idea, an abstraction, for which religious masses with their head who cannot be shown provide the transition; whether a common tendency, a wish in which many can share, can serve as the same substitute. The abstraction might in turn be embodied more or less completely in a kind of secondary leader, and the relation between idea and leader would yield interesting variations. Leader or leading idea could also become negative: hatred of a particular person or institution could unify just as positive attachment does and call forth similar emotional ties. And one would ask whether the leader is really indispensable to a mass (109–110). Freud sketches a research program he will not carry out. Each item has a later answer: masses with a leader are original (X); Christ, invisible yet personal, bridges person and idea; the negative leader works because hatred binds as love does, a point grounded in this chapter's account of narcissism and in chapter IX's rivalry. The secondary leader who embodies an idea anticipates the role of the prophet in *Der Mann Moses*.

These questions, some of which the literature may have treated, will not divert attention from the basic psychological problems that the structure of a mass presents. A consideration now offers the shortest route to proving that libidinal ties characterize a mass (110).

Freud recalls how people generally relate to one another emotionally. In Schopenhauer's famous parable of the freezing porcupines, none can bear too intimate an approach by another (110). The quoted parable: on a cold winter's day porcupines huddle together for warmth, feel each other's quills and draw apart, and are driven back and forth between the two evils until they find a moderate distance at which they can best endure it (110 n.). The baseline of human relations is not sociability but regulated distance, a compromise between need and repulsion. The mass is what suspends this distance, and its suspension requires an explanation.

Psychoanalysis attests that almost every intimate emotional relation of some duration between two people, marriage, friendship, the relation of parents and children, contains a sediment of aversive and hostile feeling, which escapes perception only through repression. A note excepts, perhaps alone, the mother's relation to her son: founded on narcissism, it is not disturbed by later rivalry and is reinforced by a beginning of sexual object choice (110 n.). The hostility is more open when partners in business quarrel and subordinates grumble against superiors. The same happens in larger units. When two families are joined by marriage, each thinks itself better or finer than the other. Of two neighbouring towns each becomes the other's jealous rival; every little canton looks down on the next. Closely related peoples repel each other: the South German cannot stand the North German, the Englishman speaks ill of the Scot, the Spaniard despises the Portuguese. That greater differences produce an aversion hard to overcome, the Gaul's against the German, the Aryan's against the Semite, the white's against the coloured, has ceased to surprise us (110–111). The gradient is the striking fact: hostility is sharpest where difference is smallest. The single exception in the note confirms the rule from the other side. The one relation free of ambivalence is narcissistic, the son loved as part of the mother's self, so that unambivalent love appears possible only where the other is not quite other.

Where hostility is directed at otherwise loved persons, analysis speaks of ambivalence and explains it, surely too rationally, by the many conflicts of interest that arise precisely in close relations. In the undisguised aversions toward strangers close at hand one may recognize the expression of self-love, a narcissism that strives for self-assertion and behaves as though any divergence from its own particular developments implied a criticism of them and a demand to change them. Why so great a sensitivity should fasten on just these details of differentiation is unknown. But it is unmistakable that people's behaviour here shows "eine Haßbereitschaft, eine Aggressivität ..., deren Herkunft unbekannt ist, und der man einen elementaren Charakter zusprechen möchte" ("a readiness to hate, an aggressiveness whose origin is unknown and to which one would like to ascribe an elementary character") (111). A note refers to *Jenseits des Lustprinzips* (1920), where Freud tried to link the polarity of love and hate to the opposition of life and death drives, treating the sexual drives as the purest representatives of the life drives (111–112 n.). This is the phenomenon Freud elsewhere calls the narcissism of minor differences (*Das Tabu der Virginität*, 1918; *Das Unbehagen in der Kultur*, 1930), though the phrase does not occur here. Freud names two sources of the hostility and does not merge them. One is narcissistic self-assertion, which experiences difference as reproach. The other is an elementary aggressiveness of unknown origin. The note shows him reaching toward the death drive to ground the second, a step *Das Unbehagen* will take openly.

Yet all this intolerance disappears, temporarily or permanently, through mass formation and within the mass. As long as the mass formation lasts, and as far as it extends, individuals behave as if they were uniform, tolerate each other's particularity, put themselves on a level with the other, and feel no repulsion. On psychoanalytic theory such a restriction of narcissism can be produced by one factor only, a libidinal tie to other persons: "Die Selbstliebe findet nur an der Fremdliebe, Liebe zu Objekten, eine Schranke" ("Self-love finds a limit only in love for others, love for objects") (112). One will at once ask whether a community of interest alone, without any libidinal contribution, must lead to tolerance of and consideration for others. The answer is that no lasting restriction of narcissism comes about that way, since such tolerance lasts no longer than the immediate advantage drawn from the other's cooperation. The practical weight of the question is smaller than it seems, however, because experience shows that in cooperation libidinal ties regularly form between comrades and prolong and fix the relation beyond what is advantageous. Social relations repeat what analysis found in the development of individual libido: "Die Libido lehnt sich an die Befriedigung der großen Lebensbedürfnisse an und wählt die daran beteiligten Personen zu ihren ersten Objekten" ("Libido leans on the satisfaction of the great vital needs and chooses as its first objects the persons who take part in it"), as shown in the 1914 essay on narcissism. And as in the individual, so in the development of humanity as a whole only love has acted as a cultural factor in the sense of a turn from egoism to altruism: sexual love for woman, with all the obligations that follow from it to spare what was dear to her, and the desexualized, sublimated homosexual love for other men that attached itself to common work (112–113). The proof rests on the economics of the narcissism essay: narcissistic and object libido are communicating vessels, so a measurable fall in narcissism shows a rise in object investment. Anaclisis supplies the genesis: the social tie grows on utility as sexuality grows on nourishment, which gives the "helper" of the first page (73) its place. Utility, McDougall's and the rationalists' explanation, is not denied but demoted to the support on which love leans. The construction of culture as a bond among men working together, with women as objects whose wishes are spared, is Freud's own, and *Das Unbehagen* (IV) will make it explicit by casting women as opponents of culture. The sublimated homosexual tie among men recalls the close of the narcissism essay, where the ego ideal has a social side fed by homosexual libido.

If, then, the mass shows restrictions of narcissistic self-love that do not operate outside it, this compels the conclusion "daß das Wesen der Massenbildung in neuartigen libidinösen Bindungen der Massenmitglieder aneinander besteht" ("that the essence of mass formation consists in new kinds of libidinal ties among the members of the mass") (113). The proof works from an effect, reduced narcissism, to its only possible cause. The emphasis falls on *neuartig*, "of a new kind": these are not the sexual ties analysis knows best, and the next paragraph asks what they are.

What kind of ties are they? Psychoanalytic neurosis theory has so far dealt almost exclusively with the binding of love drives to objects that still pursue direct sexual aims. In the mass such aims are evidently out of the question. Here one has love drives that have been diverted from their original aims, without acting any less energetically. Within ordinary sexual object-cathexis analysis has already noticed phenomena that correspond to such a diversion of the drive from its sexual aim. They were described as degrees of being in love, and recognized to involve a certain impairment of the ego. These phenomena will now receive closer attention, in the well-founded hope of finding relations transferable to the ties in masses. But Freud also wants to know whether this kind of object-cathexis is the only way of being emotionally tied to another person. Analysis shows that there are other mechanisms of emotional tie, the so-called identifications, insufficiently known and hard to present, whose investigation will keep the argument away from mass psychology for some time (113–114). The two mechanisms that will explain the two ties of chapter V are now named: aim-inhibited object love, studied through being in love (VIII), and identification (VII). Freud takes identification first because it is the earlier and more basic form of tie. The "impairment of the ego" in being in love prepares the impoverishment of the ego by idealization in chapter VIII. The detour announced here is the second half of the title: the problem of the mass is to be solved by the analysis of the ego.

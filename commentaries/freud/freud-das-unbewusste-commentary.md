# Freud, *Das Unbewußte* (1915): Commentary

Page references are to *Gesammelte Werke* X (1946), 264–303. Ucs., Pcs. and Cs. render Freud's system abbreviations Ubw, Vbw and Bw; "cathexis" renders *Besetzung* (with "hypercathexis" for *Überbesetzung* and "anticathexis" for *Gegenbesetzung*); "drive" renders *Trieb*; "idea" renders *Vorstellung* in general, and "thing-presentation" and "word-presentation" render *Sachvorstellung* and *Wortvorstellung*. Quotations are given in the original, followed by an English rendering.

Six misreadings beset this text, and the commentary guards against them throughout. The unconscious is not the repressed: the repressed is a subset of it. The descriptive sense of "unconscious" (not now conscious) is not the systematic sense (belonging to the system Ucs.), and Freud's latency arguments establish only the first. The Ucs. is not a second, hidden subject or a "subconsciousness": Freud explicitly refuses that model. Psychical topography is not cerebral localization. "Unconscious affect" is not symmetrical with "unconscious idea": only ideas persist as formations in the Ucs. Finally, the word-presentation thesis of section VII does not say that words make an idea conscious; it says that linkage to words makes an idea capable of consciousness and thereby defines the system Pcs.

Freud opens by recalling the result of the preceding paper, "Die Verdrängung." Repression does not consist "darin, eine den Trieb repräsentierende Vorstellung aufzuheben, zu vernichten, sondern sie vom Bewußtwerden abzuhalten" ("in cancelling or annihilating an idea that represents a drive, but in keeping it from becoming conscious") (264). The repressed idea remains active, and some of its effects reach consciousness. He then fixes the relation of extension that the whole essay presupposes: "Das Unbewußte hat den weiteren Umfang; das Verdrängte ist ein Teil des Unbewußten" ("The unconscious has the wider compass; the repressed is a part of the unconscious") (264). This sentence blocks the commonest misreading at the outset, and it leaves room for what section VI will find and *Das Ich und das Es* (1923) will make structural: unconscious portions of the very agency that represses.

The second paragraph poses the epistemic question: how can we know the unconscious at all? Only as something conscious, after it has undergone "eine Umsetzung oder Übersetzung in Bewußtes" ("a transposition or translation into something conscious") (264). Analysis shows daily that such translation is possible, but only if the patient overcomes resistances, and these are the very forces that once made the content repressed by expelling it from consciousness. Two points follow. Access to the unconscious is always mediated, never direct; and the clinic is its privileged site because repression returns there as resistance, so that the force which conceals is also the force one can observe. The word "translation" is not idle: section VII will give it a literal sense, translation into words (300).

## I. The Justification of the Unconscious

Freud argues that the hypothesis of the unconscious is both necessary and legitimate. It is necessary because the data of consciousness are full of gaps. In the healthy and the sick alike, conscious acts occur that presuppose other acts to which consciousness bears no witness: parapraxes, dreams, symptoms and obsessions, but also ordinary ideas whose origin we do not know and results of thinking whose elaboration stayed hidden from us. If we insist that every mental act must be known through consciousness, these acts remain disconnected and unintelligible; if we interpolate the inferred unconscious acts, they fall into a demonstrable connection. "Gewinn an Sinn und Zusammenhang ist aber ein vollberechtigtes Motiv, das uns über die unmittelbare Erfahrung hinaus führen darf" ("Gain in sense and coherence is a fully justified motive for going beyond immediate experience") (265). A second criterion is pragmatic: if action based on the hypothesis successfully alters conscious processes, that success is "einen unanfechtbaren Beweis" ("an incontestable proof") of the existence of what was assumed. The demand that everything psychical be known to consciousness is therefore "eine unhaltbare Anmaßung" ("an untenable presumption"). The argument is abductive and runs on two criteria at once, meaning and efficacy. Ricoeur's diagnosis of Freud's mixed discourse, hermeneutic and energetic, finds its first evidence here. The everyday examples (unbidden ideas, thoughts arrived at without awareness of their working-out) also anticipate section VI's discovery that much that is not repressed never becomes conscious (291).

Freud adds a further argument. At any moment consciousness holds only a small content, so most of what we call conscious knowledge must spend long periods in latency, that is, in a state of psychical unconsciousness. Given our latent memories, denying the unconscious becomes incomprehensible. The opponent replies that latent memories are no longer psychical but correspond to residues of somatic processes from which the psychical can re-emerge. Freud counters that a latent memory is plainly a residue of a psychical process; but the decisive point is that the objection rests on an unstated equation of the conscious with the mental. That equation is either a *petitio principii* that forbids asking whether everything psychical must be conscious, or a terminological convention. As a convention it is irrefutable; the only question is whether it is useful, and Freud answers that it is not. "Sie zerreißt die psychischen Kontinuitäten, stürzt uns in die unlösbaren Schwierigkeiten des psychophysischen Parallelismus" ("It disrupts psychical continuities and plunges us into the insoluble difficulties of psychophysical parallelism") (266); it overrates consciousness without justification, and it forces us to leave psychology prematurely without compensation from other fields. Freud moves the dispute from fact to the utility of a concept, as he had treated basic concepts as revisable conventions at the start of "Triebe und Triebschicksale." The target of the equation is the Brentanian position, whose lectures Freud attended in Vienna: Brentano had argued against unconscious mental phenomena. Note the limit of this argument: latency establishes only the descriptive unconscious, which section II will separate sharply from the systematic one.

Freud concedes that the question whether undeniable latent states should be called unconscious-mental or physical threatens to become a verbal dispute, so he turns to what is known about them with certainty. Physically, they are wholly inaccessible: no physiological idea or chemical process gives any notion of their nature. Psychically, they are in the closest contact with conscious processes. With some work they can be transposed into conscious processes or replaced by them, and they can be described in all the categories we apply to conscious acts: ideas, strivings, decisions. Of some of them we must say "sie unterscheiden sich von den bewußten eben nur durch den Wegfall des Bewußtseins" ("they differ from conscious ones only in the absence of consciousness") (267). The criterion of the psychical is therefore categorial description, not the substrate or the presence of awareness. "Some" is chosen carefully: the repressed will turn out to differ from conscious acts in far more than the absence of awareness (269, 270–71).

Freud explains the stubborn rejection of the psychical character of latent acts by the narrowness of the evidence that opponents consider. Most of the relevant phenomena have been studied only within psychoanalysis. Whoever ignores pathological facts, treats the parapraxes of normal people as accidents, and accepts the old saying that dreams are froth ("Träume seien Schäume") need only neglect a few puzzles of the psychology of consciousness to spare himself the hypothesis of unconscious mental activity (267). Hypnotic experiments, and posthypnotic suggestion in particular, had already demonstrated the existence and mode of action of the mental unconscious before psychoanalysis. The posthypnotic case had been Freud's chief exhibit in his English paper of 1912, "A Note on the Unconscious in Psycho-Analysis": an idea demonstrably present and effective, executed at the appointed time, while its origin remains unknown to the subject.

The hypothesis is also legitimate, because it departs in no way from a mode of thinking we already accept. Consciousness gives each of us knowledge only of our own mental states; that another person has consciousness is an inference by analogy from his observable utterances and actions. Freud immediately corrects this: it is psychologically more accurate to say that we attribute our own constitution, and so our consciousness, to every other person without reflection, and that this identification is the precondition of understanding him. The inference, or identification, was once extended by the ego to animals, plants, inanimate things and the world as a whole, and it served well as long as the resemblance to the individual ego was overwhelming, becoming less reliable as the other grew more remote. Critique now hesitates over animal consciousness, refuses plant consciousness, and consigns the consciousness of inanimate things to mysticism. Yet even for the nearest human other, the assumption of consciousness "ruht ... auf einem Schluß und kann nicht die unmittelbare Sicherheit unseres eigenen Bewußtseins teilen" ("rests on an inference and cannot share the immediate certainty of our own consciousness") (268). Freud's parenthetical correction, that understanding others is an unreflective transfer rather than an argument, converges with the phenomenological rejection of the analogical-inference theory of empathy (Husserl against Lipps in Hua XIII, and the analogizing apperception of the Fifth Cartesian Meditation). The term "identification" is also loaded: it will carry the theory of mourning, melancholia and group formation.

Psychoanalysis demands only that this procedure be turned against one's own person, though we have no constitutional inclination to do so. Then the acts and utterances I notice in myself but cannot connect with the rest of my mental life must be judged "als ob sie einer anderen Person angehörten" ("as if they belonged to another person") and explained by a mental life ascribed to that person (268). Experience confirms that we readily interpret in others, that is, insert into a mental connection, the very acts to which we refuse psychical status in ourselves. Some particular obstacle deflects our inquiry from ourselves and blocks correct knowledge. Self-knowledge here acquires the form of knowledge of the other: what I cannot integrate stands to me as another's mind stands to me, known only by inference. The obstacle is resistance, though Freud does not yet name it. Lacan's formula that the unconscious is the discourse of the Other has a methodological precursor here, but Freud at once withdraws the "other person" model.

Turned against oneself in spite of inner reluctance, the inference would correctly yield a second consciousness united with mine. Freud raises three objections. First, a consciousness of which its own bearer knows nothing is something other than another person's consciousness, and it is doubtful whether a consciousness lacking its most important characteristic deserves discussion: whoever rejected an unconscious mind will not accept "ein unbewußtes Bewußtsein" ("an unconscious consciousness") in exchange (269). Second, the latent processes we infer are highly independent of one another, as if unaware of one another; we would need a third, fourth, perhaps endless series of consciousnesses, all unknown to us and to each other. Third, and weightiest, some latent processes have characteristics that seem alien, even incredible, and directly contrary to the known properties of consciousness. The inference must therefore be amended: it proves not a second consciousness in us but "die Existenz von psychischen Akten, welche des Bewußtseins entbehren" ("the existence of psychical acts that lack consciousness"). Freud rejects the term "subconsciousness" as incorrect and misleading, and redescribes cases of "double conscience" as a splitting of mental activities into two groups, toward which one and the same consciousness turns alternately. The targets are Janet's subconscious and the theories of co-consciousness. The third objection carries the most weight because it anticipates section V: the Ucs. is defined by qualitative otherness (primary process, absence of negation, timelessness), and that otherness is what rules out modelling it as a subject.

Psychoanalysis therefore has no alternative but to declare mental processes to be in themselves unconscious, and to compare their perception by consciousness with the perception of the external world by the sense organs. Freud places this hypothesis in two lineages. It extends primitive animism, which showed us copies of our consciousness everywhere, and it continues Kant's correction of our view of external perception. As Kant warned us not to overlook the subjective conditioning of perception and not to identify perception with the unknowable thing perceived, so psychoanalysis warns us not to substitute the perception of consciousness for the unconscious process that is its object. "Wie das Physische, so braucht auch das Psychische nicht in Wirklichkeit so zu sein, wie es uns erscheint" ("Like the physical, the psychical need not be in reality as it appears to us") (270). Freud expects, with some satisfaction, that inner perception will be easier to correct than outer, the inner object being less unknowable than the external world. The thesis inverts the Cartesian order: the psychical is unconscious in itself, and consciousness becomes a sense organ for psychical qualities, as chapter VII of the *Traumdeutung* had put it. The Kantian analogy is apt, since Kant himself restricted inner sense to appearances. The double lineage is also precise: psychoanalysis continues animism in ascribing mind beyond awareness, and corrects it in denying that this mind is a copy of consciousness. This is the thesis Michel Henry attacks in *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*: by locating the essence of the psychical outside appearing and conceiving consciousness as representational perception, Freud inherits the very ontology of representation that makes life's self-manifestation unthinkable.

## II. The Ambiguity of the Unconscious and the Topographical Point of View

Freud now states a fact both important and troublesome: unconsciousness is only one mark of the psychical and quite insufficient to characterize it. Psychical acts of very different standing share it. The unconscious includes acts that are merely latent, temporarily unconscious, and otherwise no different from conscious ones, and also processes such as the repressed, which, were they to become conscious, "sich von den übrigen bewußten aufs grellste abheben müßten" ("would stand out most glaringly from the other conscious ones") (271). All misunderstanding would end if we classified acts without regard to consciousness, by their relation to drives and aims, their composition, and their membership in the hierarchy of psychical systems. That is impracticable, so we cannot avoid using "conscious" and "unconscious" sometimes descriptively and sometimes systematically, where they signify membership in a system and possession of certain properties. Arbitrary names that avoid any reference to consciousness would not help: we would first have to account for how the systems are distinguished, and consciousness cannot be bypassed, since it is "den Ausgangspunkt aller unserer Untersuchungen" ("the starting point of all our investigations"). Freud settles for writing Cs. and Ucs. when the words are used systematically. Consciousness is thus epistemically primary and theoretically secondary, and Freud cannot yet free his classification from it. He will call for that emancipation at 291, and *Das Ich und das Es* will redraw the whole apparatus because the ambiguity proves unmanageable.

Positively stated, a psychical act generally passes through two phases with a kind of testing, the censorship, between them. In the first phase it is unconscious and belongs to the Ucs.; if the censorship rejects it, it may not pass to the second phase, is called repressed, and must remain unconscious. If it passes, it enters the second system, which Freud here calls Cs. But its relation to consciousness is still not fixed: it is not yet conscious but "bewußtseinsfähig" ("capable of consciousness"), in Breuer's term, meaning that under certain conditions it can become an object of consciousness without particular resistance. In view of this capacity Freud also calls the system the preconscious. Should a further censorship also govern the becoming-conscious of the preconscious, the systems Pcs. and Cs. will have to be separated more strictly; for now it suffices that Pcs. shares the properties of Cs. and that the strict censorship operates at the passage from Ucs. to Pcs. (or Cs.) (272). Freud's naming wavers between Cs. and Pcs., and he will acknowledge the wavering at 288. The conditional about a second censorship is not idle: section VI confirms it (290–92).

With these two or three systems, psychoanalysis has moved a step further from descriptive psychology of consciousness and given itself new questions and new content. It had differed from psychology chiefly by its dynamic conception of mental processes, that is, by treating them as the outcome of conflicting forces. Now it adds psychical topography and aims to say of any mental act within which system, or between which systems, it takes place. This aim has earned it the name depth psychology. Freud announces that a further point of view will enrich it; the economic point of view arrives at 280. Metapsychology is assembled one point of view at a time, and each is introduced only when an explanatory gap requires it.

Taking topography seriously raises a question. When an idea passes from Ucs. to Cs. (or Pcs.), should we assume a fresh fixation, "gleichsam eine zweite Niederschrift" ("as it were a second registration"), located in a new psychical place, alongside which the original unconscious registration persists? Or does the passage consist in a change of state in the same material at the same place (273)? The question may seem abstruse, but it must be put if we want a definite idea of psychical depth. It is difficult because it touches the relation of the mental apparatus to anatomy. That mental activity is bound to the brain as to no other organ is an unshakable result; the unequal value of the brain's parts and their special relations to particular body parts and mental functions take us somewhat further. But every attempt to localize ideas in nerve cells and have excitations travel along fibres has failed, and a theory placing Cs. in the cortex and the unconscious in subcortical regions would fail likewise. "Unsere psychische Topik hat vorläufig nichts mit der Anatomie zu tun; sie bezieht sich auf Regionen des seelischen Apparats, wo immer sie im Körper gelegen sein mögen, und nicht auf anatomische Örtlichkeiten" ("Our psychical topography has for the present nothing to do with anatomy; it refers to regions of the mental apparatus, wherever they may be situated in the body, and not to anatomical localities"). Freud speaks here as the author of the aphasia monograph (1891), which had criticized localizationism, and of the abandoned *Entwurf* (1895). The notion of successive registrations goes back to the letter to Fliess of 6 December 1896, and Derrida's "Freud et la scène de l'écriture" builds on exactly this vocabulary of inscription.

Psychoanalysis is therefore free to proceed according to its own needs, and its assumptions should claim only the value of illustrations. Of the two possibilities, the first, that the conscious phase of an idea is a new registration in another place, is cruder but handier; the second, a merely functional change of state, is more probable from the outset but less vivid and harder to handle. The topographical hypothesis entails a spatial separation of Ucs. and Cs. and the possibility that an idea is present at two places in the apparatus at once, advancing regularly from one to the other when the censorship does not stop it, possibly without losing its first registration. This looks strange but can draw on impressions from analytic practice (274). Freud weighs hypotheses by heuristic yield as well as probability, consistent with his treatment of the topography as a model rather than a map.

The practice in question: if one tells a patient an idea he once repressed, which the analyst has guessed, nothing changes at first in his mental state. The repression is not lifted, its consequences are not undone; one obtains instead a renewed rejection. Yet the patient now has the same idea in two forms in different places: the conscious memory of the auditory trace of what he was told, and, as we know for certain, the unconscious memory of the experience in its earlier form. The repression is lifted only when the conscious idea, after the resistances are overcome, connects with the unconscious memory trace; success comes only by making that trace itself conscious. This seems to show that conscious and unconscious ideas are distinct, topographically separate registrations of the same content. But on reflection the identity between the communication and the repressed memory is only apparent: "Das Gehörthaben und das Erlebthaben sind zwei nach ihrer psychologischen Natur ganz verschiedene Dinge, auch wenn sie den nämlichen Inhalt haben" ("Having heard something and having experienced it are, in their psychological nature, two quite different things, even if they have the same content") (275). The technical consequence is the one drawn in "Über 'wilde' Psychoanalyse" (1910): informing the patient is not interpretation. The theoretical consequence waits until section VII. What the patient receives is a word-presentation unconnected to the thing-presentation, and section VII will make the connection of the two the condition of a lifted repression (300).

Freud therefore cannot yet decide between the two possibilities. Factors may later tip the balance; or "unsere Fragestellung unzureichend war" ("our way of putting the question was inadequate"), and the difference between an unconscious and a conscious idea must be determined quite otherwise (275). The second suspicion is the right one. Section VII rejects both alternatives in the same words in which this paragraph frames them (300), so the open question becomes the thread that runs through the essay.

## III. Unconscious Feelings

Having confined the discussion to ideas, Freud asks a new question whose answer must clarify his theoretical views. There are conscious and unconscious ideas; are there also unconscious drive impulses, feelings and sensations, or is it nonsense to form such compounds (275)? The question tests whether "unconscious" is a predicate of representations only. The answer separates the psychical contents to which the opposition applies from those to which it applies only by extension.

The opposition of conscious and unconscious does not apply to the drive. "Ein Trieb kann nie Objekt des Bewußtseins werden, nur die Vorstellung, die ihn repräsentiert" ("A drive can never become an object of consciousness, only the idea that represents it") (275). Nor can the drive be represented in the unconscious other than by an idea. If it did not attach to an idea or appear as an affective state, we could know nothing of it. To speak of an unconscious or repressed drive impulse is therefore a harmless looseness of expression meaning a drive impulse "deren Vorstellungsrepräsentanz unbewußt ist" ("whose ideational representative is unconscious") (276). The drive is a limit concept, as "Triebe und Triebschicksale" had defined it, on the frontier between the somatic and the psychical. It is known only through its two representatives, idea and affect, and so stands outside the conscious/unconscious opposition altogether. The compound *Vorstellungsrepräsentanz* (the representative that consists in an idea) became a crux of Lacan's Seminar XI, where he rendered it "le représentant de la représentation" and made it the signifier that primal repression holds down.

One might expect the question about unconscious sensations, feelings and affects to be answered as easily. It belongs to the essence of a feeling that it is felt, that is, known to consciousness, so unconsciousness would seem excluded. Yet analytic practice speaks routinely of unconscious love, hate and rage, and even finds unavoidable the odd combination "unconscious sense of guilt" (*unbewußtes Schuldbewußtsein*) and the paradoxical "unconscious anxiety." Does this usage mean more than "unconscious drive" does (276)? Freud grants the phenomenological premise without argument: "Zum Wesen eines Gefühls gehört es doch, daß es verspürt, also dem Bewußtsein bekannt wird" ("It surely belongs to the essence of a feeling that it is felt, that is, known to consciousness"). Clinical speech presses against this essence. Henry makes this concession the lever of his critique: affect is the self-manifestation that representation-based theory cannot capture.

Here the situation really is different. First, an affect may be perceived but misconstrued: because its proper representative has been repressed, it has been forced to connect with another idea, and consciousness takes it for the expression of that idea. When we restore the correct connection we call the original affect "unconscious," "obwohl ihr Affekt niemals unbewußt war, nur ihre Vorstellung der Verdrängung erlegen ist" ("although its affect was never unconscious; only its idea had succumbed to repression") (276). This is the "false connection" of the *Studies on Hysteria*. More generally, talk of unconscious affect refers to the vicissitudes of the quantitative factor of the drive impulse under repression, which are threefold: the affect persists wholly or in part as it is; it is transformed into a qualitatively different quantity of affect, above all anxiety; or it is suppressed, its development prevented altogether. Suppressing the development of affect is the real aim of repression, whose work remains unfinished if it fails. When repression succeeds in inhibiting affect, we call "unconscious" the affects we reinstate when we undo the repression. The usage is consistent, but there is a significant difference: the unconscious idea persists after repression "als reale Bildung im System Ubw" ("as an actual formation in the system Ucs."), whereas to the unconscious affect there corresponds there only a potential beginning that was not allowed to develop. "Streng genommen ... gibt es also keine unbewußten Affekte, wie es unbewußte Vorstellungen gibt" ("Strictly speaking, then, there are no unconscious affects as there are unconscious ideas") (277). There may well be affective formations in the Ucs. that become conscious like others. The whole difference arises because ideas are cathexes, ultimately of memory traces, while affects and feelings correspond to processes of discharge whose final manifestations are perceived as sensations. The asymmetry is structural: an idea is something stored (a trace plus its cathexis), an affect is something that happens (a discharge). The Ucs. is therefore an order of ideas, not of affects, and later objections to Freud, from André Green's work on affect to Henry's, start from this decision. Freud nonetheless keeps the phrase "unconscious sense of guilt" and puts it at the centre of *Das Ich und das Es*.

That repression can prevent a drive impulse from turning into affective expression shows that the system Cs. normally governs affectivity as well as access to motility, and it enhances the importance of repression, which keeps contents not only from consciousness but also from the development of affect and the setting off of muscular activity. Conversely, we call a person's mental state normal so long as Cs. governs affectivity and motility. The two forms of discharge differ, however, in how firmly the system controls them. Control over voluntary motility is firmly grounded, regularly withstands neurosis and breaks down only in psychosis; control over the development of affect is weaker. Even in normal life one can see "ein beständiges Ringen der beiden Systeme Bw und Ubw um den Primat in der Affektivität" ("a constant struggle between the systems Cs. and Ucs. for primacy in affectivity") (278), with spheres of influence marked off and mixtures of forces produced. A footnote defines the two kinds of discharge: affectivity is essentially motor (secretory, vasomotor) discharge that alters the subject's own body without reference to the external world, while motility consists in actions designed to alter the external world. The system Cs. is thus defined here by its control of the paths to discharge, not by awareness: it guards access to action. The criterion of psychosis follows from this, as does the clinical point that affect is the first ground the Ucs. contests. The distinction between internal and external alteration goes back to the *Entwurf*.

The importance of Cs. (Pcs.) for access to affect and action also explains the role of the substitute idea in the formation of illness. Affect may develop directly from the Ucs., in which case it always has the character of anxiety, the currency for which all "repressed" affects are exchanged. Often, however, the drive impulse must wait until it has found a substitute idea in Cs.; affect can then develop from this conscious substitute, whose nature determines its quality. Freud corrects his previous paper accordingly. Repression was said to separate the affect from its idea, each then going its own way; this is descriptively undeniable, but in reality an affect as a rule does not arise "bis nicht der Durchbruch zu einer neuen Vertretung im System Bw gelungen ist" ("until a breakthrough to a new representation in the system Cs. has succeeded") (278–79). Qualified affect needs a conscious representation; anxiety is what unrepresented drive becomes. This is the 1915 transformation theory of anxiety, which *Hemmung, Symptom und Angst* (1926) will revise by making anxiety the cause of repression rather than its product.

## IV. Topography and Dynamics of Repression

Freud has established that repression is essentially a process affecting ideas at the boundary between Ucs. and Pcs. (Cs.), and he now tries to describe it more closely. It must consist in a withdrawal of cathexis; the question is in which system the withdrawal occurs and to which system the withdrawn cathexis belongs (279). The question reformulates repression in economic terms: not exclusion from a place but redistribution of a quantity.

The repressed idea remains capable of action in the Ucs., so it must have kept its cathexis; what is withdrawn must be something else. In repression proper, the "after-pressure" (*Nachdrängen*) that acts on a preconscious or already conscious idea, repression can only consist in withdrawing from the idea the preconscious cathexis that belongs to the system Pcs. The idea then remains uncathected, or receives cathexis from the Ucs., or keeps the unconscious cathexis it already had: withdrawal of the preconscious cathexis, retention of the unconscious one, or replacement of the former by the latter. Freud notices that he has assumed, almost unintentionally, that passage from Ucs. to the next system occurs not through a new registration but through a change of state, a change in cathexis. "Die funktionale Annahme hat hier die topische mit leichter Mühe aus dem Felde geschlagen" ("Here the functional hypothesis has easily routed the topographical one") (279). The open question of section II is settled provisionally by the logic of explanation rather than by argument. The settlement will not last: section VII rejects both hypotheses (300).

This withdrawal of libido does not suffice to explain another feature of repression. There is no reason why the idea, having kept its cathexis or received one from the Ucs., should not renew its attempt to enter the Pcs. by virtue of that cathexis. The withdrawal would then have to be repeated, the same game would continue without end, and the result would not be repression. The mechanism also fails for primal repression, where an unconscious idea has never received preconscious cathexis, so none can be withdrawn from it (280). Withdrawal explains neither the stability of repression nor its origin.

Another process is needed, one that maintains repression in the first case and establishes and perpetuates it in the second. Freud finds it only in an anticathexis by which the system Pcs. protects itself against the pressure of the unconscious idea. Clinical examples will show how this anticathexis, which takes place in the Pcs., manifests itself. "Sie ist es, welche den Daueraufwand einer Urverdrängung repräsentiert, aber auch deren Dauerhaftigkeit verbürgt" ("It is what represents the permanent expenditure of a primal repression, but also what guarantees its permanence") (280). Anticathexis is the sole mechanism of primal repression; in repression proper, withdrawal of preconscious cathexis is added, and it is quite possible that the withdrawn cathexis is the one used for the anticathexis. Repression thus costs a continuous expenditure, which grounds both the clinical theory of resistance and the neurotic's impoverishment of energy. Primal repression is defined only negatively, by an anticathexis with no prior withdrawal, and this leaves its origin obscure. Laplanche later fastened on this obscurity and answered it with the theory of the enigmatic message from the adult other.

Freud notes that he has gradually brought a third point of view to bear in the presentation of psychical phenomena, besides the dynamic and the topographical: the economic, which follows the fortunes of quantities of excitation and seeks at least a relative estimate of them. The approach that is the culmination of psychoanalytic research deserves a special name: "Ich schlage vor, daß es eine metapsychologische Darstellung genannt werden soll, wenn es uns gelingt, einen psychischen Vorgang nach seinen dynamischen, topischen und ökonomischen Beziehungen zu beschreiben" ("I propose that we speak of a metapsychological presentation when we succeed in describing a psychical process in its dynamic, topographical and economic relations") (281). Given the present state of knowledge, this will succeed only in isolated places. This is the canonical definition of metapsychology, a word Freud had used privately since the Fliess letters and publicly in the *Psychopathologie des Alltagslebens* (1901), where he proposed to transform metaphysics into metapsychology. The economic point of view entered the essay because of an explanatory failure (the endless repetition, the unexplained primal repression), not by fiat.

Freud now attempts, tentatively, a metapsychological description of repression in the three familiar transference neuroses. He may replace "cathexis" with "libido," since what is at stake are the vicissitudes of sexual drives (281). The substitution is local and justified by the subject matter; "cathexis" remains the general term for any quantity invested in an idea.

In anxiety hysteria a first phase is often overlooked, perhaps even skipped, but careful observation reveals it: anxiety appears without the subject perceiving what he is afraid of. One must assume that a love impulse in the Ucs. demanded transposition into the Pcs., but that the cathexis directed toward it from the Pcs. withdrew as in an attempt at flight, and the unconscious libidinal cathexis of the rejected idea was discharged as anxiety. When the process is repeated, a first step is taken to master the unwelcome anxiety. The fleeing cathexis turns to a substitute idea, associatively connected with the rejected idea but removed from repression by its distance from it (the substitute by displacement), and this permits a rationalization of the still uninhibitable anxiety. The substitute idea now plays the part of an anticathexis for Cs. (Pcs.), securing it against the emergence of the repressed idea; at the same time it is, or behaves as, the point from which the now quite uninhibitable anxiety is released. The child with an animal phobia feels anxiety under two conditions: when the repressed love impulse is intensified, and when the feared animal is perceived. In the first case the substitute idea functions as a point of transmission from Ucs. to Cs., in the second as an independent source of anxiety. As the dominance of Cs. extends, the first mode of excitation gives way to the second; in the end the child may behave as if he had no inclination toward his father at all and really feared the animal. But this animal anxiety, fed from an unconscious drive source, proves stubborn and excessive against all influences from Cs., "und dadurch ihre Herkunft aus dem System Ubw verrät" ("and thereby betrays its origin in the system Ucs.") (282). The case behind this is Little Hans (1909). The substitute is doubly determined: it is the defensive system's chosen bulwark and the drive's point of entry, one formation serving both. Freud is already applying the principle he will state at 289: origin decides fate.

In the second phase the anticathexis from Cs. has led to a substitute formation, and the same mechanism is soon applied again. Repression is not finished and takes on a further task: to inhibit the anxiety arising from the substitute. The whole associated environment of the substitute idea is cathected with special intensity so that it becomes highly sensitive to excitation. Excitation at any point of this outwork (*Vorbau*), being connected with the substitute, gives rise to a slight development of anxiety, which is now used "als Signal" ("as a signal") to inhibit further anxiety by a renewed flight of cathexis (282). The farther from the feared substitute the sensitive, vigilant anticathexes are placed, the more precisely the mechanism can isolate the substitute and keep new excitations from it. These precautions protect only against excitations that reach the substitute from outside, through perception, never against the drive excitation that reaches it through its connection with the repressed idea. They work only once the substitute has taken over the representation of the repressed, and they can never work reliably: with each rise in drive excitation the protective wall must be moved farther out. The whole construction, built analogously in other neuroses, is called a phobia. The flight from conscious cathexis of the substitute is expressed in the avoidances, renunciations and prohibitions by which anxiety hysteria is recognized. The third phase repeats the work of the second on a larger scale: Cs. now protects itself against activation of the substitute by anticathecting its surroundings, as it earlier protected itself against the repressed idea by cathecting the substitute. Formerly Cs. had only one small point of entry for the repressed impulse, the substitute; in the end the entire phobic outwork corresponds to "einer solchen Enklave des unbewußten Einflusses" ("an enclave of unconscious influence") (283). The defence has achieved a projection of the drive danger outward: the ego behaves as if the danger of anxiety came from a perception rather than a drive impulse, and can therefore react to this external danger with phobic avoidance. Repression succeeds in one respect: the release of anxiety can be contained to some extent, but only at heavy cost to personal freedom. Flight from drive demands is generally useless, and the outcome of phobic flight remains unsatisfying (284). Two later doctrines appear here in embryo. Signal anxiety, which 1926 will make the ego's normal instrument, is already present as a small anxiety used to forestall a large one. And the logic of phobia is laid bare: the ego converts an inner danger into an outer one because only a perception can be fled. The military metaphors (outwork, enclave, wall) state the economic point that defence spends ever more to guard a growing perimeter.

Much of what holds for anxiety hysteria holds for the other two neuroses, so Freud can restrict himself to the differences and to the role of anticathexis. In conversion hysteria the drive cathexis of the repressed idea is converted into the innervation of the symptom. How far, and under what conditions, the unconscious idea is drained by this discharge, so that it can give up its pressure on Cs., is a question better reserved for a special study of hysteria. The role of the anticathexis from Cs. (Pcs.) is clear and shows itself in symptom formation: the anticathexis selects which fragment of the drive representative is allowed to concentrate the whole of its cathexis. The fragment chosen as symptom satisfies the condition that it expresses the wishful aim of the drive impulse as much as the defensive or punitive striving of Cs.; it is therefore hypercathected and held from both sides, like the substitute idea in anxiety hysteria. It follows that the expenditure of repression by Cs. need not be as great as the cathectic energy of the symptom, "denn die Stärke der Verdrängung wird durch die aufgewendete Gegenbesetzung gemessen" ("for the strength of repression is measured by the anticathexis expended"), and the symptom rests not only on the anticathexis but also on the drive cathexis from the Ucs. condensed in it (284). The symptom is a compromise with two sources of energy, and this explains economically why conversion is relatively cheap: the drive pays for part of its own containment.

For obsessional neurosis Freud adds to the remarks of the previous paper only that the anticathexis of Cs. is most conspicuous here. Organized as a reaction formation, it effects the first repression, and it is also the point at which the repressed idea later breaks through. He conjectures that the predominance of anticathexis and the absence of discharge explain why repression appears far less successful in anxiety hysteria and obsessional neurosis than in conversion hysteria (285). The comparison yields a rule: the more a repression relies on anticathexis and the less it allows discharge, the less it succeeds. The breakthrough at the very site of the reaction formation, where scrupulosity becomes the vehicle of the forbidden, is the obsessional version of the doubly determined substitute.

## V. The Special Characteristics of the System Ucs.

The distinction between the two systems gains new significance once we notice that the processes of one of them, the Ucs., show characteristics not found in the next higher system (285). This is the payoff of the "weightiest" objection of section I (269): the Ucs. differs from consciousness not merely by lacking awareness but by its mode of functioning. The topography of places becomes a typology of processes.

The nucleus of the Ucs. consists of drive representatives that seek to discharge their cathexis: "Der Kern des Ubw besteht aus Triebrepräsentanzen, die ihre Besetzung abführen wollen, also aus Wunschregungen" ("The nucleus of the Ucs. consists of drive representatives that seek to discharge their cathexis, that is, of wishful impulses") (285). These impulses are coordinated with one another, exist side by side without influencing each other, and do not contradict each other. When two wishful impulses whose aims must seem incompatible to us are activated together, they do not diminish or cancel each other but combine into an intermediate aim, a compromise. The Ucs. is characterized here in logical terms: its contents coexist without exclusion, and incompatibility becomes composition. This is the condition of the overdetermination familiar from the *Traumdeutung*, where one element serves several wishes at once.

In this system there is no negation, no doubt, no degree of certainty. All these are introduced only by the work of the censorship between Ucs. and Pcs. "Die Negation ist ein Ersatz der Verdrängung von höherer Stufe" ("Negation is a substitute, at a higher level, for repression") (285). In the Ucs. there are only contents cathected with greater or lesser strength. Modal and logical determinations (negation, doubt, certainty) thus belong to censorship, not to the representations themselves; in the Ucs., intensity takes the place of truth value. The sentence on negation is the seed of "Die Verneinung" (1925), where the negating judgment becomes the intellectual acceptance of the repressed while repression persists, a text Hyppolite and Lacan later read together.

The intensities of cathexis are far more mobile in the Ucs. Through displacement an idea can hand over its entire quota of cathexis to another; through condensation it can take over the whole cathexis of several others. Freud has proposed regarding these two processes as marks of the so-called psychical primary process. In the Pcs. the secondary process prevails. Where a primary process is allowed to run its course on elements of the Pcs., it appears "komisch" ("comic") and provokes laughter (286). A footnote refers to chapter VII of the *Traumdeutung*, built on ideas Breuer developed in the *Studies on Hysteria*. The primary process is defined economically, by the free mobility of quantities, not by any semantic content. The remark on the comic points back to the joke book (1905), where the comic and the joke arise when primary-process operations intrude into preconscious material under conditions that make the intrusion pleasurable.

The processes of the Ucs. are timeless: they are not ordered in time, are not altered by its passage, and have no relation to time at all. The relation to time is also bound to the work of the system Cs. (286). This is why infantile wishes persist undiminished in adult life and why analytic work, not the lapse of years, is required to change them. Phenomenology treats temporality as the form of consciousness itself (Hua X); Freud agrees from the other side, ascribing time to Cs. and therefore denying it to the Ucs. *Jenseits des Lustprinzips* (1920) will link Kant's thesis that time is a form of intuition to the functioning of the system Cs.

Nor do unconscious processes take account of reality. They are subject to the pleasure principle; their fate depends only on how strong they are and on whether they meet the requirements of pleasure-unpleasure regulation (286). The opposition of pleasure principle and reality principle had been set out in "Formulierungen über die zwei Prinzipien des psychischen Geschehens" (1911). Here it is distributed across the two systems: the reality principle is not a separate force but a property of the higher system.

Freud summarizes: "Widerspruchslosigkeit, Primärvorgang (Beweglichkeit der Besetzungen), Zeitlosigkeit und Ersetzung der äußeren Realität durch die psychische" ("exemption from mutual contradiction, primary process (mobility of cathexes), timelessness, and replacement of external by psychical reality") are the characteristics we may expect to find in processes belonging to the Ucs. (286). A footnote reserves mention of another important privilege of the Ucs. for a different context. The list is the essay's positive definition of the system, and every item is privative relative to Cs.: no contradiction, no binding, no time, no external reality. The reserved privilege has never been identified with certainty; it may belong to the lost metapsychological papers.

Unconscious processes become recognizable only under the conditions of dreaming and of the neuroses, that is, when processes of the higher Pcs. system are set back to an earlier stage by regression. In themselves they are unknowable, and also incapable of existence ("existenzunfähig"), because the Ucs. is overlaid very early by the Pcs., which has seized access to consciousness and to motility (286). The Ucs. discharges into bodily innervation in the development of affect, but even this path is contested by the Pcs. On its own, under normal conditions, the Ucs. could bring about no purposive muscular action except those already organized as reflexes (287). The claim that unconscious processes are unable to exist in themselves qualifies the thesis of 270 that the psychical is unconscious in itself. The pure Ucs. is a theoretical limit never met as such; it is always encountered through regression, in a higher system set back to a lower mode. Freud's Kantian analogy returns in a sharper form: the Ucs. is not merely unknown in itself but unable to exist apart from its overlay.

The full significance of these characteristics would emerge only by contrasting them with those of the Pcs. That would lead too far, so Freud proposes another postponement: the comparison will follow the treatment of the higher system (287). That treatment belongs to the planned paper on consciousness, one of the seven metapsychological essays Freud wrote in 1915 but never published. The essay is thus deliberately incomplete; the Pcs. is defined here only as far as the description of the Ucs. requires.

Only the most urgent points are given now. The processes of the Pcs., whether already conscious or only capable of consciousness, show an inhibition of the tendency of cathected ideas to discharge. When the process passes from one idea to another, the first retains part of its cathexis and only a small portion is displaced. Displacements and condensations as in the primary process are excluded or greatly restricted. This led Breuer to assume two states of cathectic energy in mental life, "einen tonisch gebundenen und einen frei beweglichen, der Abfuhr zustrebenden" ("one tonically bound, and one freely mobile and pressing toward discharge") (287). Freud believes this distinction is so far our deepest insight into the nature of nervous energy and sees no way around it. Pursuing the discussion would be an urgent need of metapsychology, but perhaps too daring a venture at this point. The secondary process is defined by binding: energy held at an idea rather than passed on. *Jenseits des Lustprinzips* will make binding the central function of the apparatus and ask what precedes the pleasure principle.

The Pcs. also establishes communication among ideational contents so that they can influence one another; it gives them a temporal order; it institutes one or several censorships; and it is responsible for reality testing and the reality principle. Conscious memory, too, seems to depend wholly on the Pcs. It must be sharply distinguished from the memory traces in which the experiences of the Ucs. are fixed, and it probably corresponds to a special registration, of the kind "wie wir sie für das Verhältnis der bewußten zur unbewußten Vorstellung annehmen wollten, aber bereits verworfen haben" ("such as we wanted to assume for the relation between the conscious and the unconscious idea, but have already rejected") (288). In this context Freud will also end the wavering in his name for the higher system, which he has been calling now Pcs., now Cs. Two things stand out. Every function that makes an idea part of a coherent mental life (mutual influence, order, testing) belongs to the higher system, so the Ucs. is a field of isolated wishes. And the double-registration hypothesis, rejected for the passage from unconscious to conscious idea, is kept in reserve for memory. The opposition between memory traces and consciousness will return in *Jenseits des Lustprinzips* and in the "Notiz über den 'Wunderblock'" (1925).

Freud warns against generalizing too quickly from this distribution of functions. He is describing the mature human being, in whom the Ucs., strictly speaking, functions only as a preliminary stage of the higher organization. What content and relations the system has during individual development, and what significance it has in animals, must be investigated independently rather than deduced from this description. Even in humans we must be prepared for pathological conditions under which the two systems change their content and characteristics, or even exchange them (288). The caution limits the scope of the systemic description: it is a cross-section of a developed apparatus, not a genetic account, and it allows that the same content can change systems. The last possibility points ahead to section VII, where schizophrenia applies the primary process to preconscious material.

## VI. Communication between the Two Systems

It would be wrong to imagine the Ucs. at rest while the Pcs. does all the psychical work, or to treat it as something finished with, a rudimentary organ, a residue of development. It would equally be wrong to suppose that the two systems communicate only through repression, the Pcs. hurling into the abyss of the Ucs. whatever disturbs it. "Das Ubw ist vielmehr lebend, entwicklungsfähig und unterhält eine Anzahl von anderen Beziehungen zum Vbw, darunter auch die der Kooperation" ("The Ucs. is, on the contrary, alive and capable of development, and maintains a number of other relations with the Pcs., among them that of cooperation") (288–89). In sum, the Ucs. continues into its so-called derivatives, is accessible to the impressions of life, constantly influences the Pcs. and is itself subject to influences from the Pcs. Freud rejects the storage model of the unconscious. The concept that replaces it is the derivative (*Abkömmling*): a formation that issues from the Ucs. and carries its descent into higher systems.

The study of derivatives will thoroughly disappoint any expectation of a schematically clean separation between the two systems. This will cause dissatisfaction and will likely be used to cast doubt on the value of dividing psychical processes in this way. Freud replies that his only task is to translate the results of observation into theory, and he declines any obligation to reach, at the first attempt, a smooth theory recommended by its simplicity. He defends the theory's complications as long as they fit the observations, and hopes that through them he will finally arrive at a state of affairs that is simple in itself yet does justice to the complications of reality (289). The methodological stance is consistent with the treatment of concepts as conventions (266) and of the topography as illustration (274): observation constrains theory, and elegance is not a criterion.

Among the derivatives of unconscious drive impulses there are some that unite opposite determinations. On the one hand they are highly organized, free of contradiction, have made use of every acquisition of the system Cs., and could hardly be distinguished from its formations. On the other hand they are unconscious and incapable of becoming conscious. "Sie gehören also qualitativ zum System Vbw, faktisch aber zum Ubw. Ihre Herkunft bleibt das für ihr Schicksal Entscheidende" ("Qualitatively, then, they belong to the system Pcs., but factually to the Ucs. Their origin remains decisive for their fate") (289). Freud illustrates this with a racial analogy typical of his period: people of mixed descent who broadly resemble whites but betray their ancestry by some conspicuous feature and are therefore excluded from society and its privileges. The structural point is that exclusion follows descent, not qualities. Such formations are the fantasies of normal people and of neurotics, which analysis has recognized as preliminary stages of dream and symptom formation. Despite their high organization they remain repressed and cannot become conscious. They come close to consciousness and remain undisturbed so long as their cathexis is not intense, but are thrown back as soon as it exceeds a certain level. Substitute formations are similarly highly organized derivatives of the Ucs., but they break through to consciousness thanks to a favourable relation, for instance by coinciding with a preconscious anticathexis (290). The thesis undermines the classification by properties that section II had called the ideal (271): system membership is fixed by genealogy, not by quality. A formation can have every property of the Pcs. and still be treated as unconscious. The point about substitutes recalls the phobic and conversion symptoms of section IV, which pass precisely because the defence itself selects them. The theory of fantasy draws on "Der Dichter und das Phantasieren" (1908) and "Hysterische Phantasien und ihre Beziehung zur Bisexualität" (1908).

When the conditions of becoming conscious are examined elsewhere, some of these difficulties will be resolved. For now Freud sets against the view that has ascended from the Ucs. a view that starts from consciousness. To consciousness the whole sum of psychical processes presents itself as the realm of the preconscious. A very large part of this preconscious derives from the unconscious, has the character of its derivatives, and is subject to a censorship before it can become conscious. Another part of the Pcs. can become conscious without censorship. This contradicts an earlier assumption: in considering repression, the censorship decisive for becoming conscious was placed between Ucs. and Pcs.; now a censorship between Pcs. and Cs. suggests itself. Freud declines to treat this as a difficulty and assumes instead that "jedem Übergang von einem System zum nächst höheren, also jedem Fortschritt zu einer höheren Stufe psychischer Organisation eine neue Zensur entspreche" ("to every transition from one system to the next higher one, that is, to every advance to a higher stage of psychical organization, there corresponds a new censorship") (290). This disposes of the assumption of a continuous renewal of registrations (291). Multiplying censorships is the alternative to multiplying registrations: what marks each step upward is a new test, not a new copy. The conditional of section II (272) is now confirmed.

The source of these difficulties is that consciousness, the only character of psychical processes immediately given to us, is in no way suited to distinguishing the systems. Apart from the fact that the conscious is not always conscious but sometimes latent, observation has shown that much that shares the properties of the Pcs. does not become conscious, and we shall learn that becoming conscious is restricted by certain directions of attention. Consciousness therefore has no simple relation either to the systems or to repression. The truth is that not only the psychically repressed remains foreign to consciousness, but also some of the impulses that dominate our ego, the strongest functional opposite of the repressed. To the extent that we work toward a metapsychological view of mental life, "müssen wir lernen, uns von der Bedeutung des Symptoms ‚Bewußtheit' zu emanzipieren" ("we must learn to emancipate ourselves from the importance of the symptom 'being conscious'") (291). Consciousness is demoted to a symptom, a sign that may or may not appear, and no longer defines anything. The unconscious part of the ego is the decisive discovery: what repression should most sharply oppose (the repressing agency) is itself partly unconscious. *Das Ich und das Es* opens by repeating this argument and draws the structural conclusion.

As long as we cling to that symptom, our generalizations are regularly broken by exceptions. Derivatives of the Ucs. become conscious as substitute formations and symptoms, usually after great distortion relative to the unconscious, but often retaining many characteristics that call for repression. Many preconscious formations remain unconscious that, we would think, could by their nature very well become conscious; the stronger attraction of the Ucs. probably asserts itself in them. We are thus directed to seek the more significant difference "nicht zwischen dem Bewußten und dem Vorbewußten, sondern zwischen dem Vorbewußten und dem Unbewußten" ("not between the conscious and the preconscious but between the preconscious and the unconscious") (291). The Ucs. is turned back at the frontier of the Pcs. by the censorship; its derivatives can bypass this censorship, become highly organized, and grow in the Pcs. to a certain intensity of cathexis; once they exceed it and try to force themselves on consciousness, they are recognized as derivatives of the Ucs. and repressed anew at the new censorship between Pcs. and Cs. The first censorship operates against the Ucs. itself, the second against its preconscious derivatives. One might think the censorship had shifted forward somewhat in the course of individual development (292). The "attraction" of the Ucs. recalls the account in "Die Verdrängung" of repression as both repulsion from above and attraction from what was primally repressed. The two censorships also explain the course of free association: derivatives can be produced in abundance until they become recognizable as such.

In analytic treatment we obtain irrefutable proof of the second censorship, between Pcs. and Cs. We ask the patient to form derivatives of the Ucs. abundantly, oblige him to overcome the censorship's objections to these preconscious formations becoming conscious, and by defeating this censorship open the way to lifting the repression that is the work of the first. Freud adds that the existence of the censorship between Pcs. and Cs. reminds us that becoming conscious is no mere act of perception but probably also "eine Überbesetzung, ein weiterer Fortschritt der psychischen Organisation" ("a hypercathexis, a further advance in psychical organization") (292). The fundamental rule receives its metapsychological rationale: technique attacks the second censorship as the route to the first. The definition of becoming conscious as hypercathexis amends the perceptual model of 270, where consciousness was a sense organ, and prepares the decisive claim of section VII that the Pcs. arises through hypercathexis by words (300).

Turning to the relations of the Ucs. with the other systems, Freud aims less to establish anything new than to avoid passing over what is most obvious. At the roots of drive activity the systems communicate extensively. Some of the processes excited there pass through the Ucs. as through a preparatory stage and reach their highest psychical development in Cs.; others are retained as Ucs. The Ucs. is also affected by experiences arising from external perception. "Alle Wege von der Wahrnehmung zum Ubw bleiben in der Norm frei; erst die vom Ubw weiter führenden Wege unterliegen der Sperrung durch die Verdrängung" ("Normally all paths from perception to the Ucs. remain open; only the paths leading onward from the Ucs. are subject to blocking by repression") (292). The Ucs. is not fed by drives alone: perception inscribes directly into it. Repression is a one-way barrier that blocks exit, not entry. This explains how day residues and present perceptions become material for unconscious wishes.

It is very remarkable that the Ucs. of one person can react to the Ucs. of another, bypassing the Cs. The fact deserves closer study, particularly as to whether preconscious activity can be ruled out in it, but as a description it is incontestable (293). Freud had given the technical version in the "Ratschläge für den Arzt bei der psychoanalytischen Behandlung" (1912): the analyst should turn his own unconscious like a receiving organ toward the patient's transmitting unconscious. The remark grounds evenly suspended attention and the later theory of countertransference, and it prefigures Freud's cautious interest in thought transference.

The content of the Pcs. (or Cs.) derives partly from drive life, through the mediation of the Ucs., and partly from perception. It is doubtful how far the processes of this system can act directly on the Ucs.; the study of pathological cases often shows an almost incredible independence and imperviousness of the Ucs. "Ein völliges Auseinandergehen der Strebungen, ein absoluter Zerfall der beiden Systeme ist überhaupt die Charakteristik des Krankseins" ("A complete divergence of their tendencies, an absolute dissociation of the two systems, is what in general characterizes illness") (293). Yet psychoanalytic treatment is built on influencing the Ucs. from the Cs., and shows that this is possible, though laborious. The derivatives of the Ucs. that mediate between the systems open the way. Spontaneous change of the Ucs. from the Cs. is presumably a difficult and slow process. Illness is defined as disconnection of the systems, cure as their reconnection through intermediate formations. The formula of the *Neue Folge* (1933), "Wo Es war, soll Ich werden," states the same programme in the vocabulary of the second topography.

Cooperation between a preconscious impulse and an unconscious one, even an intensely repressed one, can come about when the situation allows the unconscious impulse to act in the same direction as one of the dominant tendencies. The repression is lifted for this case, and the repressed activity is admitted as a reinforcement of the activity the ego intends. For this one constellation the unconscious becomes "ichgerecht" ("ego-syntonic"), though its repression is otherwise unchanged. The contribution of the Ucs. is unmistakable: the reinforced tendencies behave differently from normal ones, enable especially perfect achievements, and show a resistance to contradiction similar to that of obsessional symptoms (293). The paragraph gives a model of overdetermined motivation in ordinary life: exceptional performance and exceptional stubbornness have the same source. Freud describes the Ucs. as allied with the ego, not only opposed to it, and the passage anticipates the idealizations and fanaticisms analysed in *Massenpsychologie und Ich-Analyse* (1921).

The content of the Ucs. may be compared to an aboriginal population of the mind (*psychische Urbevölkerung*). If inherited mental formations, something analogous to animal instinct, exist in human beings, they form the nucleus of the Ucs. To this is later added what was discarded as unusable during childhood development, which need not differ in nature from what is inherited. A sharp and final division between the contents of the two systems is generally established only at puberty (294). The phylogenetic hypothesis belongs to the same year as the unpublished "Übersicht der Übertragungsneurosen" and to the line from *Totem und Tabu* to the primal fantasies of the Wolf Man case. The reference to puberty rests on the diphasic onset of sexuality in the *Drei Abhandlungen*. Laplanche's critique of Freud's "biologism" targets exactly this recourse to an inherited nucleus as an answer to the question of the unconscious's origin, the question that primal repression had left open (280).

## VII. Identifying the Unconscious

What has been assembled so far is roughly what can be said of the Ucs. as long as one draws only on knowledge of dream life and the transference neuroses. It is not much; in places it seems unclear and confusing; above all it lacks the possibility of fitting the Ucs. into an already known context. Only the analysis of one of the affections Freud calls narcissistic psychoneuroses promises conceptions that will bring the enigmatic Ucs. closer and make it, as it were, tangible (294). The title word *Agnoszierung* is a legal and administrative term for identifying a person or body, and the section aims at exactly that: to say what an unconscious idea is, not merely that it exists. The method changes accordingly. The narcissistic neuroses serve as a magnifying glass, since what the transference neuroses conceal they display.

Since Abraham's paper of 1908, which its conscientious author traced back to Freud's suggestion, analysts have tried to characterize Kraepelin's dementia praecox (Bleuler's schizophrenia) by its relation to the opposition between ego and object. Nothing in the transference neuroses (anxiety and conversion hysteria, obsessional neurosis) had brought this opposition to the fore. It was known that frustration in relation to the object sets off the neurosis, that the neurosis involves renouncing the real object, and that libido withdrawn from the real object turns back to a fantasied object and then to a repressed one (introversion). But in these neuroses object-cathexis as such is held onto with great energy, and closer study of repression has forced the assumption that object-cathexis persists in the Ucs. "trotz der Verdrängung — vielmehr infolge derselben" ("despite repression, or rather in consequence of it") (295). The capacity for transference, which treatment exploits in these affections, presupposes intact object-cathexis. The transference neuroses are defined by retained object-cathexis, so their unconscious is an unconscious of objects. The material comes from "Zur Einführung des Narzißmus" (1914), printed earlier in the same volume.

In schizophrenia, by contrast, Freud has been led to assume that after repression the withdrawn libido seeks no new object but withdraws into the ego, so that object-cathexes are abandoned and a primitive objectless state of narcissism is restored. The patients' incapacity for transference, so far as the illness extends, their consequent inaccessibility to treatment, their characteristic rejection of the external world, the signs of hypercathexis of their own ego, and the final outcome in complete apathy all fit this assumption well. As for the relation between the two systems, every observer has noticed that in schizophrenia much is expressed consciously that in the transference neuroses can be shown to exist in the Ucs. only through analysis. But it at first proved impossible to connect the ego-object relation intelligibly with the relations of consciousness (295). The puzzle is precise: schizophrenics say openly what neurotics repress, yet they are less, not more, accessible to analysis. Section VII is built to solve exactly this paradox.

The solution comes by an unexpected route. In schizophrenics, especially in the instructive early stages, one observes a number of changes in speech, some of which deserve examination from a particular point of view. The patient's mode of expression often becomes an object of special care; it becomes "gewählt" and "geziert" ("stilted" and "affected"). Sentences undergo a peculiar disorganization of structure that makes them unintelligible, so that we take the patient's remarks for nonsense. Their content often brings into the foreground a relation to bodily organs or innervations. In addition, in schizophrenic symptoms that resemble hysterical or obsessional substitute formations, the relation between the substitute and the repressed shows peculiarities that would surprise us in those two neuroses (295–96). Freud turns a clinical symptom, disordered speech, into the key to a theory of representation. Three features are singled out (style, syntax, reference to the body), and the comparison with neurotic substitutes is announced as the test case.

Victor Tausk of Vienna had made available some observations of incipient schizophrenia, with the advantage that the patient herself was still willing to explain what she said. Freud will use two of these examples to show the view he intends to defend, and does not doubt that any observer could easily produce such material in abundance (296). The evidential value lies in the patient's own explanation, which functions as an analysis carried out by the patient herself. Tausk's best-known study, "Über die Entstehung des 'Beeinflussungsapparates' in der Schizophrenie" (1919), develops the same observations of bodily reference.

One of Tausk's patients, a girl brought to the clinic after a quarrel with her lover, complains: "Die Augen sind nicht richtig, sie sind verdreht" ("The eyes aren't right, they're twisted") (296). She herself explains this in orderly speech through a series of reproaches against her lover: she cannot understand him; he looks different every time; he is a hypocrite, an "Augenverdreher" ("eye-twister"); "er hat ihr die Augen verdreht" ("he has twisted her eyes"); now she has twisted eyes, they are no longer her eyes, she sees the world with different eyes. Everything turns on German idiom. *Jemandem die Augen verdrehen* means to turn someone's head, to infatuate and deceive; an *Augenverdreher* is a hypocrite who rolls his eyes in feigned piety. The patient takes a figure of speech about her relation to her lover and treats it as a literal statement about her own organs.

The patient's comments on her unintelligible speech have the value of an analysis, since they contain its equivalent in generally intelligible language; at the same time they shed light on the meaning and the genesis of schizophrenic word-formation. With Tausk, Freud emphasizes that the relation to the organ, the eye, has set itself up as the representative of the whole content. "Die schizophrene Rede hat hier einen hypochondrischen Zug, sie ist Organsprache geworden" ("Schizophrenic speech here has a hypochondriacal trait; it has become organ-speech") (297). "Organ-speech" ties the linguistic symptom to libido theory. In the narcissism paper hypochondria was explained as libido withdrawn from objects and invested in an organ; organ-speech is the same process carried out on words.

A second statement from the same patient: "Sie steht in der Kirche, plötzlich gibt es ihr einen Ruck, sie muß sich anders stellen, als stellte sie jemand, als würde sie gestellt" ("She was standing in church; suddenly she felt a jolt; she had to change her position, as though someone were putting her into a position, as though she were being placed") (297). The verbs in *stellen* ("to place, to put") carry the whole statement. The patient reports a bodily sensation of being moved by an outside agent; the explanation shows how a word generated it.

The analysis consists of a new series of reproaches against the lover, who is vulgar and has made her, who was refined by upbringing, vulgar too. He made her like himself by making her believe he was superior to her; she became as he is because she believed she would be better if she were like him. "Er hat sich verstellt, sie ist jetzt so wie er (Identifizierung!), er hat sie verstellt" ("He has disguised himself; she is now like him (identification!); he has displaced her") (297). *Sich verstellen* means to dissemble or pretend; *jemanden verstellen* means to shift or disfigure someone. The single verb joins the lover's deceit, her identification with him and her bodily experience of being moved. Freud's interjection "identification!" links the case to the mechanism he is developing in "Trauer und Melancholie," written in the same months.

Tausk observes that the movement of "placing herself differently" depicts the word *verstellen* and the identification with the lover. Freud again stresses that the element prevailing in the whole train of thought is one whose content is a bodily innervation, or rather the sensation of it. A hysteric, he adds, would in the first case have rolled her eyes convulsively and in the second actually executed the jolt, instead of feeling the impulse or the sensation; in neither case would she have had any conscious thought about it, nor would she have been able to express one afterward (297). The contrast is the key comparison of the section. The hysteric converts into the body and has no words; the schizophrenic has the words consciously and only the sensation of the bodily act. The hysteric's repressed thought is inaccessible to consciousness, while the schizophrenic's lies on the surface, but in a form that has lost its link to the thing.

These two observations attest to what Freud has called hypochondriacal or organ-speech. But they also point, more importantly, to another state of affairs, which can be demonstrated at will in the examples collected in Bleuler's monograph and stated in a definite formula. "Bei der Schizophrenie werden die Worte demselben Prozeß unterworfen, der aus den latenten Traumgedanken die Traumbilder macht, den wir den psychischen Primärvorgang geheißen haben" ("In schizophrenia words are subjected to the same process that makes dream images out of latent dream thoughts, the process we have called the psychical primary process") (297). They are condensed and transfer their cathexes wholly to one another by displacement; the process can go so far that a single word, suited to it by multiple connections, represents a whole chain of thought (298). The work of Bleuler, Jung and their students has supplied ample material for this. A footnote adds that dream-work occasionally treats words like things and then produces very similar "schizophrenic" speeches or neologisms. Words are treated as things: the primary process, normally confined to the Ucs., operates on preconscious material. This is the pathological exchange of characteristics between systems that Freud allowed for at 288. Lacan's dictum that the unconscious is structured like a language draws on such passages, but Freud's own claim runs the other way: for him the normal unconscious lacks words, and primary-process operations on words are the schizophrenic exception.

Before drawing a conclusion, Freud considers the subtle but disconcerting differences between schizophrenic substitute formation and the hysterical and obsessional kinds. A patient he is currently treating has let himself be withdrawn from all interests in life by the poor condition of the skin of his face. He claims to have blackheads and deep holes in his face that everyone notices. Analysis shows that he is playing out his castration complex on his skin. At first he dealt with his blackheads without remorse, and squeezing them gave him great satisfaction because, as he put it, something spurted out. Then he began to believe that wherever he had removed a blackhead a deep cavity had formed, and he reproached himself bitterly for having ruined his skin forever by "beständiges Herumarbeiten mit der Hand" ("constantly working at it with his hand") (298). Squeezing out the blackheads is clearly a substitute for masturbation; the cavity that then appears through his fault is the female genital, that is, the fulfilment of the castration threat provoked by masturbation (or of the fantasy representing it). Despite its hypochondriacal character this substitute formation resembles a hysterical conversion, yet one feels that something else must be going on, that one could not credit a hysteric with it, even before being able to say where the difference lies. A hysteric would hardly take a tiny pit like a skin pore as a symbol of the vagina, which he otherwise compares with every object that encloses a hollow space; and the multiplicity of pits would deter him from using them as a substitute for the female genital (299). The same holds for a young patient Tausk had reported to the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society years earlier. He otherwise behaved entirely like an obsessional neurotic, spending hours on his toilet and the like, but was remarkable in that he could communicate the meaning of his inhibitions without any resistance. When pulling on his stockings, for instance, he was disturbed by the idea that he must pull apart the stitches of the knitting, that is, the holes, and every hole was for him a symbol of the female genital opening. This too cannot be expected of an obsessional neurotic. One such patient, observed by Rudolf Reitler, who suffered from the same lingering over pulling on his stockings, found the explanation only after overcoming his resistances: the foot was a penis symbol, pulling on the stocking an act of masturbation, and he had to keep pulling the stocking on and off, partly to complete the image of masturbation, partly to undo it. The two comparisons isolate two variables. In the neurotic cases the symbol rests on resemblance between things (foot and penis share a shape) and is reached only through resistance. In the schizophrenic cases the symbol rests on something else and is available without resistance. The next paragraph names that something else.

Asking what gives the schizophrenic substitute formation and symptom their strange character, we finally grasp that it is "das Überwiegen der Wortbeziehung über die Sachbeziehung" ("the predominance of the relation to the word over the relation to the thing") (299). There is very little similarity in the things themselves between squeezing a blackhead and ejaculating from the penis, and still less between countless shallow skin pores and the vagina. But in the first case something spurts out both times, and for the second the cynical saying applies literally: "Loch ist Loch" ("a hole is a hole"). "Die Gleichheit des sprachlichen Ausdruckes, nicht die Ähnlichkeit der bezeichneten Dinge, hat den Ersatz vorgeschrieben" ("The sameness of the verbal expression, not the similarity of the things designated, has dictated the substitution"). Where word and thing do not coincide, schizophrenic substitute formation departs from that of the transference neuroses (300). This is the discovery the section has been preparing: substitution in schizophrenia follows verbal identity, substitution in the neuroses follows likeness between things. The two relations come apart only where language and things diverge, and that divergence makes them distinguishable at all.

Freud combines this insight with the hypothesis that object-cathexes are abandoned in schizophrenia, and must modify it: the cathexis of the word-presentations of objects is retained. What could be called the conscious presentation of the object now splits into the word-presentation and the thing-presentation, which consists in the cathexis, if not of direct memory images of the thing, then of remoter memory traces derived from them. "Mit einem Male glauben wir nun zu wissen, wodurch sich eine bewußte Vorstellung von einer unbewußten unterscheidet" ("All at once we think we know how a conscious idea differs from an unconscious one"). The two are not, as we had thought, different registrations of the same content in different psychical places, nor different functional states of cathexis in the same place: "die bewußte Vorstellung umfaßt die Sachvorstellung plus der zugehörigen Wortvorstellung, die unbewußte ist die Sachvorstellung allein" ("the conscious idea comprises the thing-presentation plus the corresponding word-presentation, the unconscious idea is the thing-presentation alone") (300). The system Ucs. contains the thing-cathexes of objects, the first and true object-cathexes; the system Pcs. arises when this thing-presentation is hypercathected by being linked with the word-presentations corresponding to it. Such hypercathexes, one may suppose, bring about a higher psychical organization and make it possible for the primary process to be replaced by the secondary process that governs the Pcs. We can now state precisely what repression in the transference neuroses denies to the rejected idea: "die Übersetzung in Worte, welche mit dem Objekt verknüpft bleiben sollen" ("translation into words that are to remain attached to the object"). The idea not put into words, or the psychical act not hypercathected, remains in the Ucs. in a state of repression. This paragraph answers the question left open in section II (275) by rejecting both of its alternatives, and it gives a literal sense to the "translation" of the second paragraph (264): the unconscious becomes conscious by being translated into words. It also explains the clinical fact of 274–75. The analyst's communication gives the patient words, but not words linked to the thing-presentation, so the repression stands until the two are connected. And it accounts for the schizophrenic paradox of 295: the schizophrenic keeps words cut loose from abandoned thing-cathexes and handles them by the primary process, so that he can say everything and attach it to nothing. The terminology revises the aphasia monograph of 1891, where the "object-presentation" was the associative complex and the "word-presentation" was attached to it at one end. Lacan inverted the hierarchy by placing the signifier in the unconscious; Henry objected that both terms remain representations, so that Freud never reaches the affective life that drives them.

Freud points out how early psychoanalysis possessed the insight that now makes one of the most striking features of schizophrenia intelligible. The last pages of the *Traumdeutung* (1900) argued that thought processes, that is, acts of cathexis more remote from perceptions, are in themselves without quality and unconscious, and acquire the capacity to become conscious only through connection with the residues of word-perceptions (300–301). Word-presentations, for their part, derive from sense perception just as thing-presentations do, so one might ask why object-presentations cannot become conscious through their own perceptual residues. Probably thinking proceeds in systems so remote from the original perceptual residues that they have retained none of their qualities and need reinforcement by new qualities in order to become conscious. Moreover, linkage with words can give quality even to cathexes that could bring none from perception because they correspond merely to relations between object-presentations. "Solche erst durch Worte faßbar gewordene Relationen sind ein Hauptbestandteil unserer Denkvorgänge" ("Such relations, which become graspable only through words, form a major component of our thought processes") (301). Linkage with word-presentations does not yet coincide with becoming conscious; it only provides the possibility of it, and therefore characterizes no system other than the Pcs. Freud notes that he is leaving his proper theme for the problems of the preconscious and conscious, which are best treated separately. Two claims stand out. Words supply quality, and hence consciousness-capability, to what has none of its own, above all to relations; abstract thought is verbal because relations have no perceptual residue. And the distinction between capacity for consciousness and actual consciousness is kept: words define the Pcs., not the Cs. *Das Ich und das Es* (ch. II) restates the thesis in nearly the same terms. The deferral again points to the lost paper on consciousness.

In schizophrenia, which Freud touches on here only as far as the general identification of the Ucs. requires, one must doubt whether the process called repression has anything at all in common with repression in the transference neuroses. The formula that repression is a process between the system Ucs. and the Pcs. (or Cs.), resulting in exclusion from consciousness, needs modification in any case if it is to include dementia praecox and the other narcissistic affections. But the ego's attempt at flight, expressed in the withdrawal of conscious cathexis, remains common to both. The most superficial reflection shows how much more radically and profoundly this flight of the ego is carried out in the narcissistic neuroses (301–2). The concept of repression is stretched to its limit, and what survives the stretching is the ego's flight. The ego thus emerges as the agent of defence, preparing the later generalization of repression into defence (*Abwehr*) in 1926.

If this flight in schizophrenia consists in withdrawing drive cathexis from the points that represent the unconscious object-presentation, it may seem strange that the part of the same object-presentation belonging to the Pcs., its word-presentations, should instead receive a more intense cathexis. One would rather expect the word-presentation, as the preconscious part, to bear the first impact of repression and to become entirely incapable of cathexis once repression has advanced to the unconscious thing-presentations. Freud resolves the difficulty: the cathexis of the word-presentation does not belong to the act of repression but represents the first of the attempts at restoration or cure that so conspicuously dominate the clinical picture of schizophrenia. These efforts aim to regain the lost objects, and they may well take the path to the object by way of its verbal part, but then they must content themselves "mit den Worten an Stelle der Dinge" ("with words in place of things") (302). Mental activity in general moves in two opposite directions: either from the drives through the Ucs. to conscious thought, or, prompted from outside, through Cs. and Pcs. to the unconscious cathexes of the ego and of objects. This second path must remain passable despite repression and lies open, for some distance, to the neurosis's efforts to regain its objects. "Wenn wir abstrakt denken, sind wir in Gefahr, die Beziehungen der Worte zu den unbewußten Sachvorstellungen zu vernachlässigen, und es ist nicht zu leugnen, daß unser Philosophieren dann eine unerwünschte Ähnlichkeit in Ausdruck und Inhalt mit der Arbeitsweise der Schizophrenen gewinnt" ("When we think abstractly, we are in danger of neglecting the relations of words to unconscious thing-presentations, and it cannot be denied that our philosophizing then acquires an unwelcome resemblance, in expression and content, to the way schizophrenics operate") (303). Conversely, one may try to characterize the schizophrenic mode of thought by saying that it treats concrete things as though they were abstract. The theory of restitution comes from the Schreber case (1911), where delusion was read as an attempt at recovery; here it receives its linguistic form. The barb at philosophy repeats the claim of *Totem und Tabu* that a paranoid delusion is a caricature of a philosophical system, now grounded in the word-thing theory: abstraction risks keeping words whose link to things has been cut. The closing chiasmus is exact. The philosopher treats abstractions as if they were things, while the schizophrenic treats things as if they were words.

If we have really identified the Ucs. and correctly determined the difference between an unconscious and a preconscious idea, our investigations will be led back to this insight from many other directions (303). The essay closes with a criterion of confirmation by convergence and offers its central thesis as a hypothesis to be tested elsewhere. The arc of the whole is now visible. Section I demoted consciousness from criterion of the psychical to its sense organ; section VI demoted it further to a mere symptom; section VII brings it back in a new form, as the capacity conferred by words. The question whether the conscious and the unconscious idea are two registrations or two states was answered by a third option that Freud could not have reached from the topography alone: the difference lies in what the idea is composed of.

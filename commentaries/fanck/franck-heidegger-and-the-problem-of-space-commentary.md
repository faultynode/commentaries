# **Didier Franck, *Heidegger and the Problem of Space* (1986)**

## **Introduction**

Franck opens not with his own voice but with a citation from Heidegger's *Nietzsche* lectures, a passage about what "catastrophe" means in the work of great thinkers. The claim Heidegger makes there is counterintuitive and Franck wants its full force: the catastrophe of a great thinker does not lie in failure, in ceasing to advance, but precisely in *continuing* — in letting oneself be carried forward by the nearest, most immediate effect of one's own thought, which is never anything but a bad effect. What is ruinous is to press ever further ahead instead of holding back, remaining behind at the source, at the proper beginning. The history of Western philosophy, Heidegger suggests, will one day have to be appropriated from exactly this vantage point, and remarkable insights would follow from doing so.

Having set down this borrowed thesis, Franck turns it back upon Heidegger himself with a series of rhetorical questions. How could Heidegger have named this threat — the shadow, the collapsed hearth and center, that accompanies and perhaps even precedes all thinking — without himself having undergone its searing trial? How could he not have had a singular, even eminent, experience of catastrophe, if he underwent it precisely *there where everything turns*, at the very site of the turning, which is to say at the site of catastrophe proper: at the point where *Being and Time* breaks off? The move here is deliberate and programmatic. Franck is converting a general Heideggerian thesis about the danger inherent in thinking into a specific hypothesis about *Sein und Zeit*'s famous incompletion. The interruption of the book, he proposes in his closing question, may not be an accident but the index of an *essential difficulty*. With this, the governing problem of the entire study is announced: the breaking-off of *Being and Time* is to be read not as a biographical or editorial contingency but as a philosophically meaningful event.

Franck now distinguishes two ways of taking the interruption, and clearly subordinates one to the other. To recognize the interruption, to search out its motives, to risk an interpretation of it — this is, he grants, to aim at understanding why a *book* remained unfinished. Necessary as that task is, he insists that the most important thing does not lie there. The point is not bibliographical. *Sein und Zeit* is not merely one of the major pieces in the archive of philosophy; it is, in Franck's formulation, the *determining conjunction of its destiny*, finally offered up to question and to repetition. (The word "repetition," *Wiederholung*, is loaded: it is Heidegger's own term for the retrieval of a buried question from the tradition, so Franck is signaling that the book is itself the site where the tradition becomes available for such retrieval.)

He then enlarges the stakes to the maximum. It is the whole of metaphysics — taken in the monumental continuity of its history — that turns around being and time; or more precisely, metaphysics receives its very *space of play* (its *espace de jeu*, its room for maneuver) from the understanding of being *as presence*. This is the decisive Heideggerian diagnosis Franck is invoking: the entire metaphysical tradition has tacitly understood the being of beings as presence, and "presence" is a temporal determination. Therefore the fracture that immobilized the Heideggerian project carries a significance that bears on metaphysics as a whole — and equally on every effort to delimit it, to overcome it, or to leave it behind. The interruption, Franck says, *circumscribes the proximate site of thinking*: it marks out the place where thought now finds itself. The unfinished seam of the book is thus elevated into a clue about the boundary and the future of philosophy itself.

Having argued that the interruption matters, Franck reconstructs precisely *what* was interrupted by laying out the published plan of the work as Heidegger gave it in §8 of *Sein und Zeit*. The elaboration of the question of being was to proceed in two parts. The **first part** was to consist in the interpretation of *Dasein* on the basis of temporality (*Zeitlichkeit*), together with the explication of time as the *transcendental horizon* of the question of being. The **second part** was then to follow with a turn back toward and upon the history of ontology, in order to submit it to a *phenomenological destruction* guided by the temporal problematic.

Franck then specifies the internal subdivisions of each part, because the location of the break depends on them. The destruction of ontology (second part) was to bear on three of its most decisive moments, in this order: (1) the Kantian doctrine of schematism; (2) the ontological foundations of the Cartesian *cogito sum*; (3) the Aristotelian analysis of time. The hermeneutic of *Dasein* and the bringing-to-light of time as the horizon of all understanding of being (first part) was likewise to be carried out in three divisions: (1) a preliminary fundamental analysis of *Dasein*; (2) *Dasein* and temporality (*Zeitlichkeit*); (3) Time and Being (*Zeit und Sein*). And then the crucial fact: it was *at the threshold of this last division* — the third division of the first part, "Time and Being" — that the enterprise was suspended. Franck is being exact on purpose. The published book contains divisions one and two of part one; it stops right where division three would begin. Everything that follows in the Introduction turns on the significance of that exact cut.

Franck draws the consequence. Only the first half (or nearly) of the planned program was actually published. He then poses the natural objection and dismantles it. Does the incompletion mean that the *phenomenological destruction of the tradition* presented insurmountable obstacles that forbade its execution? No — and Franck shows why by pointing out how much of that destructive work Heidegger had in fact already accomplished or could accomplish. The destruction is, in a certain manner, already *underway within the analytic of Dasein itself*: the critique of the Cartesian ontology of the world and the existential genesis of the vulgar concept of time, both carried out in the published portions, are more than mere sketches of it. Moreover, *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics* furnishes an elucidation of the transcendental schematism within the horizon of temporality — that is, it delivers the very piece (the Kant chapter) that the second part promised. So the destructive program demonstrably *could* be done; Heidegger did substantial parts of it elsewhere.

The conclusion is therefore sharp and orients the whole investigation: the reasons that kept *Sein und Zeit* in the state of a fragment are *not* to be sought in the field opened by the title of its second part (the Destruction), but in the field named by "**Time and Being**" — the unpublished third division of part one. Franck has now localized the wound. The book did not fail for want of being able to criticize the tradition; it failed at the positive, culminating moment where temporality was to be turned toward being itself.

Having identified the missing division as the locus of the difficulty, Franck asks the disciplined scholarly question: what do we actually know, and what can we know, on the basis of *Sein und Zeit* itself, about the section "Time and Being"? He assembles the textual evidence — the explicit forward-references Heidegger makes within the published book to this unwritten division. There are three. Heidegger refers to it a first time by indicating that he will treat there the reasons why the phenomenon of *world* has always been missed; a second time by announcing that he will return there to the copula and to the *logos* (λόγος); and a third time to say that it "will have to show that, and how, the intentionality of 'consciousness' is grounded in the ekstatic temporality of *Dasein*."

But Franck immediately marks the insufficiency of this evidence. That the critiques of Husserlian intentionality, of the "logical" interpretation of being, and of the traditional ontologies of the world were all supposed to find their place in this division does two things — and fails to do a third. On the one hand, this list does not suffice to *characterize the content* of the division; on the other hand, and above all, it does not exhibit its *guiding axis* — the single directing thread that would have organized those critiques into one movement. So the forward-references tell us topics but not the spine. Franck needs that spine, and the next paragraph supplies it.

Franck now reconstructs the missing axis by situating the third division within the overall *movement* of the questioning, using the published plan as an anticipated tracing of that movement. The reasoning is structural: once the analytic of *Dasein* is completed and the destruction of ontology is set aside as not being the obstacle, then, looking back at the blueprint, it becomes clear what "Time and Being" was meant to accomplish. It was to *verify time as the fundamental horizon of every understanding of being*, and to treat of the *transcendence and the temporality of being itself*.

To make the target vivid, Franck points to the closing words of *Being and Time* — and notes, with attention to Heidegger's own typographical emphases ("the ordered play of underlining"), that these final questions mark in advance the theme of the missing division before becoming, provisionally and then definitively, the book's last words: Does a path lead from originary time to the *meaning of being*? Does time itself manifest itself as the *horizon of being*? Franck then states the decisive distinction that the whole study will exploit. Having established that **temporality (*Zeitlichkeit*)** is the meaning of being of *Dasein* — the meaning of being of that being to whose being it belongs to understand being — Heidegger proposed, in the missing division, to disengage the **temporal-ity (*Temporalität*)** of *being itself*, in order to give "the concrete answer to the question of the meaning of being." The two words must be heard as distinct: *Zeitlichkeit* is the temporality that *Dasein* is; *Temporalität* would be the temporal determination of *being as such*. The unwritten division was to be the passage from the first to the second. That passage is the axis Franck was looking for.

Franck corroborates this reconstruction with an external text, treating it as Heidegger's own renewed elaboration of the missing division: the 1927 lecture course *The Basic Problems of Phenomenology*. He notes its structure. The course proceeds through a phenomenological-critical discussion of four theses on being, which Franck lists: (1) the **Kantian** thesis that being is not a real predicate; (2) the **medieval** thesis, of Aristotelian origin, that divides being into *essentia* and *existentia*; (3) the thesis of **modern ontology** that splits being into nature (*res extensa*) and mind (*res cogitans*); and (4) the thesis of **logic** that reduces being to the copula of the judgment. (These map closely onto the destructive moments and onto the topics the third division was to address — Kant, the tradition, the Cartesian world-split, and the *logos*/copula.)

Through this discussion, Franck reports, Heidegger begins by reaffirming the necessity of an existential analytic *prior* to any ontology in general — i.e., he repeats the basic gesture of *Being and Time*. Then, and precisely *because* the ontological constitution of the *Dasein* that understands being is founded on temporality, a problematic of its own arises, referred to temporality insofar as temporality is the *condition of possibility* of every understanding of being. This is exactly the move toward *Temporalität*, now confirmed by a second text. The reconstruction of paragraph 6 is thus not Franck's free conjecture; the 1927 course shows Heidegger taking the same step.

Franck lets the 1927 course define the key term in Heidegger's own words, and the citation is worth following closely because the entire problematic of the book hangs on the distinction it draws. Heidegger names the new problematic *Temporalität* and immediately warns that "temporal-ity" (*Temporalität*) does not coincide with "temporality" (*Zeitlichkeit*). *Temporalität* designates temporality *insofar as it is thematized as the condition of possibility of the understanding of being and of ontology as such*. The term is meant to indicate that, within the existential analytic, temporality presents the *horizon* from which we understand being. What is questioned in the existential analytic — namely, existence — gives itself as temporality (*Zeitlichkeit*); and this temporality, in its turn, constitutes the horizon for the understanding of being that belongs to *Dasein*. The task, Heidegger says, is to see being in its *temporal determinateness* and to unveil the problematic contained in it. And the payoff is stated explicitly: only when being offers itself to the phenomenological gaze in its temporal determinateness will we be in a position to grasp more clearly the *distinction between being and beings* and to fix the ground of the **ontological difference**.

Franck includes the full statement because it accomplishes two things he needs. First, it ratifies, in Heidegger's own technical vocabulary, the *Zeitlichkeit* / *Temporalität* distinction that organizes the reconstruction. Second, it ties that distinction to the ontological difference itself — the difference between being and beings — thereby showing that what was at stake in the missing division was nothing less than the ground of Heidegger's central concept. The unfinished seam is now revealed to sit at the very foundation.

Franck now states his thesis about the *nature* of the incompletion in its strongest form. If *Sein und Zeit* nonetheless remained without sequel — like a question awaiting a forever-deferred answer — this is *not* because, for contingent reasons, Heidegger failed to reach the *Temporalität* of being or failed to attain the goal fixed from the very exposition of the question of its meaning. Franck explicitly refuses the "he simply ran out of road" explanation. Rather, the incompletion bears the imprint of an *aporia* — an impasse — that is doubtless as originary as the question itself, indeed perhaps originary *of* the question, lying at its very source.

To dignify the claim, Franck offers an analogy: just as the gap between the first and second editions of Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* possesses an essential (not merely editorial) significance, so the interruption of *Sein und Zeit* is, *all by itself*, a question whose rank cannot be inferior to that of the question of being. The interruption is not a defect in the asking of the question; it is itself a question of the first order. Franck adds that Heidegger never ceased to meditate on this incompletion in order to appropriate its source — preparing the transition to how Heidegger himself later read the break.

Franck observes that Heidegger's later thought is always accompanied by — and sometimes indistinguishable from — a *self-reinterpretation*, a continual rereading of his own earlier path. The leading example is the *Letter on Humanism*, which Franck cites. There Heidegger explains that a sufficient accomplishment of the "other thinking" that relinquishes subjectivity was made difficult because, at the time of the publication of *Being and Time*, the third division of the first part, "Time and Being," was *held back* (withheld). And then the phrase Franck has been building toward: "Here, the whole turns" — *hier kehrt sich das Ganze um*. The division was held back, Heidegger says, because thinking lacked the *saying* adequate to this turning and could not manage it with the help of the language of metaphysics. So Heidegger's own account is that the missing division failed for want of an adequate language — the failure was linguistic, the failure of metaphysical language at the point of the "turn."

Franck immediately interposes a methodological caution that defines his own independence as a reader. The interpretation of a thought *by itself* never takes place except within the very limits of that thought; such self-interpretation confirms the constraints of the thinking even in its effort to displace them, and it may well *impede* comprehension — that is, impede precisely the grasping of those very limits. In other words, Heidegger's self-reading is itself bounded by the horizon it is trying to step beyond, and so cannot be the final word on its own boundaries.

Therefore, Franck says, whatever proper right one must grant to the movement by which Heidegger interpreted *Sein und Zeit*, it is perhaps *not* by retracing the same path that one will succeed in elucidating — fruitfully, creatively, and faithfully — the abandonment of the project of *fundamental ontology* in favor of a *topology of being* and a *saying of appropriation* (*Ereignis*). This sentence sets Franck's whole strategy. He acknowledges that the later Heidegger moved from fundamental ontology toward a "topology of being" and the thinking of *Ereignis* (appropriation/the event), but he declines to take Heidegger's own narrative of that shift at face value. He will look for the motive of the interruption elsewhere than where Heidegger's self-interpretation locates it (in the inadequacy of metaphysical language). The reader is thereby prepared for a different diagnosis — one that will turn out to concern *space*.

Franck now closes in on the specific text that will reorient everything. The withholding of the "Time and Being" division, he reiterates, is *more and other* than the merely reserved future of a work; its motive can only have been a *sollicitation* — a shaking, a calling-into-question — of the question of being and time themselves. Where, then, should one seek the epicenter of this earthquake (*séisme*), if not in the late lecture that, after thirty-five years, takes up again the very title *Time and Being* (the 1962 conference *Zeit und Sein*)? Franck notes that among the rare references this late text makes back to *Sein und Zeit*, only one is *critical* in nature, and it amounts to an explicit disavowal.

He then quotes that disavowal. Heidegger argues that, since time as well as being are to be thought only on the basis of *Ereignis* (appropriation), the relation of *space* to appropriation must correspondingly be thought as well — and that this can succeed only if we have first recognized the *provenance of space from the propriety of place* (the *lieu*), itself sufficiently thought (with a cross-reference to "Building Dwelling Thinking"). And then the sentence that is, in effect, the seed of Franck's entire book: "The attempt in *Being and Time*, §70, to lead back the *spatiality* of *Dasein* to *temporality* is not tenable." Heidegger himself, late in life, retracts §70 — the very section in which *Sein und Zeit* had subordinated *Dasein*'s spatiality to its temporality. Franck has now found his lever. The general problem of the interruption has been narrowed to a precise, named, Heidegger-certified failure point: the reduction of spatiality to temporality.

In the concluding movement, Franck weighs the significance of Heidegger's retraction by gathering three features that together let one sense its full import. *First*, the singularity of the **tone**: never, it seems, had Heidegger's self-explication taken the form of such a flat refusal, such a "finding of no grounds" — Franck uses juridical metaphors (*fin de non-recevoir*, *constat de non-lieu*, a dismissal, a ruling that there is no case) to mark how unusually categorical this disavowal is. *Second*, the **gravity of the critique** itself: to say that an existential — *spatiality* — does not derive from temporality is grave, because *in principle every existential is a mode of temporalization*. The retraction of §70 therefore threatens a structural principle of *Being and Time*: if spatiality cannot be reduced to temporality, then the claim that temporality is the unifying meaning of all the existential structures of *Dasein* is broken at one of its joints. *Third*, the **importance of the context**: *Zeit und Sein* belongs to the ultimate steps of Heidegger's thinking, those that strive to think being on the basis of appropriation (*Ereignis*) — so the retraction is not a marginal aside but issues from the most mature stratum of the work.

Franck closes by converting this "presentiment" of significance into a research program — which is, precisely, the task of the book the reader is beginning. A presentiment, he says, is only an invitation to knowledge: here and to begin with, an incitement to understand the *phenomenological motives* for which the spatiality of *Dasein* is irreducible to its supposedly originary ontological meaning, temporality. With that, the Introduction hands off to the body of the work: the whole investigation will be the attempt to make good on this incitement — to show, phenomenologically and from within Heidegger's own analyses, *why* the spatiality of *Dasein* cannot be led back to temporality, and what that irreducibility means for fundamental ontology as a whole.

# **Chapter I — The Question of Being**

Franck opens the chapter by stating the rank of the question that organizes everything: the question of being, the question of the *meaning* of being, is the fundamental question of philosophy. It is broader, deeper, and more originary than any question that arises from within philosophy's own circle; above all, it is the *purest* question — the question restored to its dignity, its audacity, its bare freedom. Nothing, he says, attests this better than the way *Being and Time* begins.

He then recapitulates the opening moves of *Sein und Zeit*. Heidegger first establishes the necessity of an *explicit repetition* of the question of being, by reference both to the *forgetting* into which it has fallen and to our ignorance — the ignorance that *we ourselves are*. He clears away the prejudices that keep the question suppressed and that justify its omission, thereby opening the possibility of a hearing for it. Then Heidegger analyzes the *formal structure of every question in general*, in order to bring out what is specific to the question of being. A question, Franck explains, is a search pre-oriented and as it were provoked from a distance by the very thing it seeks; it presupposes a *pre-givenness* of what it aims to know, without which it could not even be understood as a question. From this Franck extracts the three structural moments Heidegger distinguishes. The first moment of any question (*Frage*) is that which it asks *about* — the **asked-about** (*Gefragte*). Second, to question is always, in one way or another, to address a question to someone or something, to interrogate a being; so the question implies that *to which or to whom* the question is put — the **interrogated** (*Befragte*). Third, the question interpellates this interrogated, concerning the asked-about, *in order to* obtain, reach, bring to light, and properly determine something — the **questioned** or what-is-asked-for (*Erfragte*). The sentence breaks off as Franck turns to apply this triad to the question of being itself.

Franck now maps the three formal moments onto the question of being and, in doing so, derives the necessity of a pre-understanding. The question of being can only arise out of a *pre-understanding* of the meaning of being. For even if we do not know what "to be" means, we nonetheless understand — vaguely — every statement that summons being, beginning with the question itself: what does "being" mean? A vague understanding, i.e. an empty, ordinary, confused understanding of being, is a *fact*, and that fact signifies that being is given *in advance* of the question devoted to it.

He then specifies the three moments. The question of being bears on being *as that on the basis of which every being is understood as such* — that which determines a being as a being and which, for this very reason, is *nothing that is*, no being itself. The being of beings is therefore the **asked-about**. But since being is always the being *of beings*, the question must be addressed to beings themselves: beings are the **interrogated**. Beings are, so to speak, hailed in order to yield up the meaning of their being, and that meaning, properly aimed at, is the **questioned**. The being of beings, beings, and the meaning of being are thus the three structural moments of the question of being.

The formal development, Franck notes, generates its own next problem. If the question of being addresses itself to beings, *which* being should be interrogated? Which being gathers the meaning of being, and how is it to be chosen without arbitrariness and reached as it is in itself? Franck poses this in Heidegger's own terms: on which being is the meaning of being to be read off; from which being is the disclosure of being to take its departure; is the point of departure arbitrary, or does some being have *precedence* in the elaboration of the question of being — and what is this exemplary being, and in what sense does it have precedence?

Franck then gives Heidegger's answer at length. If the question of being is to be explicitly posed and carried out in full transparency to itself, then its elaboration requires: clarifying the *mode of envisaging* being, of understanding and conceptually grasping its meaning; preparing the possibility of the *right choice* of the exemplary being; and elaborating the *authentic mode of access* to that being. But — and this is the decisive turn — envisaging, understanding, conceiving, choosing, and accessing are themselves *comportments that constitute questioning*, and hence they are the modes of being of a *determinate being*: namely, *that being which we, the questioners, ourselves are*. Therefore, elaborating the question of being means: *elucidating a being — the questioner — in its being*. The questioning of this question is, as a mode of being of a being, itself essentially determined by what is asked about in it, by *being*. This being that we ourselves always are, and which among its other possibilities has the possibility of questioning, Heidegger names *Dasein*. The explicit and transparent posing of the question of the meaning of being thus demands a prior and adequate explication of a being — *Dasein* — with regard to its being.

Franck draws the consequence of this election, and it is the key point of the paragraph: the choice of the *guiding thread* immediately discloses the *specificity* of the question of the meaning of being. For if questioning is the mode of being of a being, then the being whose very being is *to question being* is, from the outset, *itself concerned* by the being in question. The analysis of the structure of the question therefore does not merely lead us to privilege *Dasein* as the exemplary being; above all it brings us to recognize that, *in posing this question, Dasein poses the question of its own being*. This necessary implication of *Dasein* in the question of being — without which no rigorous ontology could constitute itself, for want precisely of a duly motivated guiding thread — is the *condition of possibility of ontological knowledge in general*.

Franck closes with a compact formula that he will exploit later: if ontology perhaps has a right only to the *question* (rather than to a finished science), this is first because *the question is the source of its right*. The reflexivity discovered here — that the being which asks about being is the being whose own being is at stake in the asking — is what gives ontology its legitimacy, and simultaneously what keeps it bound to the form of a question.

Franck now pauses on a problem of *language* that will recur throughout the book. Why did Heidegger choose the word *Dasein* — a word by which Kant, among many others, designates precisely that from which *Dasein* existentially understood is ontologically *distinct* (Kant uses *Dasein* to mean existence/presence-at-hand)? Franck splits the problem in two. (1) How, in general, can *one and the same word* belong both to the language of metaphysics and to the language of its *destruction*? Or: what determines the *metaphysical character* of language — the very character to whose account Heidegger charged the withholding of the "Time and Being" division? Franck explicitly defers these questions, but signals that they will progressively bind up with the questions raised by *the irreducibility of space to originary time* — tying this chapter to the book's thesis. (2) What justifies the recourse to the term *Dasein*; what does it mean, both positively and negatively?

He answers (2) with a 1949 self-clarification by Heidegger. The name "*Dasein*" was chosen for the essential domain in which the human being stands *as* human, in order to reach *with a single word and at one stroke* both the relation of being to the essence of the human being and the essential relation of the human being to the openness (the "there," the *Da*) of being as such. Crucially, Heidegger insists that "*Dasein*" does *not* come in place of the word "consciousness," any more than the "thing" named *Dasein* comes in place of what is represented by the name "consciousness." Rather, "*Dasein*" names what must first of all be *experienced as place* — the sentence continues onto the next page.

Franck completes the 1949 citation and draws out its spatial charge. What "*Dasein*" names, Heidegger says, is to be experienced first *as place* — and Franck includes Heidegger's own handwritten marginal gloss (from the *Gesamtausgabe* edition of *Wegmarken*): "insufficiently said: the mortally inhabited locality, the mortal region of locality" — namely, as the *locality of the truth of being*, and only then to be thought accordingly. The word *Dasein*, Franck observes, here carries a *spatial, local* signification that is *more accentuated* than in *Sein und Zeit*. (This is a quietly decisive remark: the later Heidegger reads his own earliest key term spatially, which is precisely the direction Franck's whole argument is pressing.)

He then enumerates the intrinsically interconnected necessities to which the word testifies. The word must say the *relation of being to the essence of the human being* and the *relation of that essence to the openness of being* — a relation that, on one hand, is inaccessible without the *displacement and reduction of consciousness* (because from consciousness it is impossible to reach things in their very being, i.e. in their *worldliness*), and that, on the other hand, the mere analysis of the formal structure of the question had already let appear. Franck then states two methodological consequences. First, the *abandonment* of the project of fundamental ontology as the existential analytic of *Dasein* will call for a *re-examination of the question as question*. Second — and more sweepingly — it becomes legitimate to consider the *whole* of Heidegger's path under the angle of the modifications undergone by both the *manner of questioning* and the *manner of thinking the question* itself. Franck is announcing that the trajectory from *Sein und Zeit* to the late work can be read as a transformation in the very status of questioning.

To gauge the magnitude of that transformation, Franck offers, as an example, one of its *last* states — a passage in which Heidegger reverses the priority of questioning. When we interrogate the essence of *language*, language must already have shown itself, must already have addressed a word to us. What does this mean, Franck asks, if not — and he quotes — that the proper gesture of thinking is *not* questioning but the *hearing* (the *ouïr*, the listening) of the granted word, the word-given, of that which is to come into question? Yet from time immemorial, in the history of our thinking, questioning has counted as the trait that gives thinking its measure — and this, Heidegger grants, is not accidental. A thinking is held to be all the more thoughtful the more radical its gesture, the more it goes to the *root* of all that is; the questioning of thought remains always a search for *first and ultimate grounds (fundaments)*. Why? — the citation continues into the next paragraph.

Franck completes the cited reasoning and then interprets it. The reason questioning has always counted as the measure of thinking is this: from time immemorial the *essence of essence* has been determined *as ground (fondement)*. Insofar as every essence has the character of ground, the search for essence is the *deepening and the founding of the ground*; and thinking that thinks toward essence so determined is, at bottom, a *questioning*. Franck then delivers the interpretive payoff. The *hearing of a word given in advance* is not a *fourth* structural moment to be added to the three moments of every question; rather it is the very *dimension* in which the question — now appearing as a *response* — yields up the initiative. If the question of being is in expectation of an answer toward which it strains, the *thinking of language* initially disposes itself to *listening*, and only then, perhaps and as if in response, questions. Such "questioning-in-response" is wholly other than a question resolved on the search for grounds.

He ties this back to the architecture of the book's thesis. It is *as fundamental* that ontology has a right only to the question; and the *relinquishing* of fundamental ontology is precisely what gives thinking the possibility of accomplishing its *own* gesture (hearing rather than grounding). In other words, the shift from grounding-questioning to listening-response is the same shift as the shift away from fundamental ontology — confirming the reading announced in paragraph 6\.

Franck now turns to justify the architectonic claim that the existential analytic is *fundamental* ontology, asking what the prerogative of *Dasein*, and the rank of the question that grants it that prerogative, actually mean. Being is the being *of beings*, and the totality of beings articulates itself into *domains* offered to knowledge. All scientific knowledge is by essence *regional* and presupposes, at its foundation, an *ontological determination of the ontic region* it explores. But the regional ontologies — of nature, of history, and so on — themselves presuppose, in turn, an ontology bearing on *being in general*, the true hearth of philosophy.

The question of being is therefore *absolutely first*. Franck quotes Heidegger to fix the double reach of this priority: the question of being aims not only at an *a priori condition of possibility of the sciences* that study beings as this or that (and which thus always already move within an understanding of being), but further at the *condition of possibility of the very ontologies* that precede and found the ontic sciences. And then the strong claim: *every* ontology, however rich and well-articulated the system of categories at its disposal, remains *at bottom blind* and perverted against its own ownmost intention so long as it has not first sufficiently elucidated the *meaning of being* and grasped that elucidation as its fundamental task. Regional and even general ontology, that is, are blind without the prior question of the meaning of being — which is why the analytic that poses that question is *fundamental*.

Franck now shows that the *ontological* priority of the question is accompanied by an *ontic* primacy of *Dasein*, and works out the famous threefold structure. The regional sciences are themselves *comportments of Dasein* toward beings that *Dasein* is not. This means, first, that these sciences possess the *mode of being of Dasein*, which is distinguished by *existence* — i.e. by having a relation of being to its own being, by being that being for whom, in its being, *this very being is at issue*; in short, by understanding of and openness to its being. Franck quotes the dense formula: the understanding of being is *itself a determination of being* of *Dasein*; the *ontic* distinction of *Dasein* lies in the fact that *Dasein is ontological*.

It means, second, that *Dasein* necessarily grasps the meaning of being of the beings it is *not* and to which it relates. The openness of *Dasein* to its own being is *ipso facto* openness to the being of *other* beings, and hence to being in general; the understanding of being belonging to *Dasein* concerns *co-originarily* the understanding of something like "world" and the understanding of the being of beings that become accessible *within* the world. It means, third, that the ontologies treating of beings whose mode of being is *not* existence are *founded in the ontological structure of Dasein itself* — which is why the fundamental ontology, from which all others can only derive, must be sought in the existential analytic of *Dasein*. Franck then names the resulting structure explicitly: the prerogative of *Dasein* — its right to be interrogated first and before any other being — rests on a *triple primat (primacy)*: **ontic** (it alone exists, in the strict sense), **ontological** (existing, it understands being), and **ontico-ontological** (it is the condition of possibility of all ontologies).

Franck now turns critical, raising for the first time the suspicion that the language of *Sein und Zeit* is itself still *metaphysical* — a suspicion that connects directly to the Introduction's diagnosis. By largely conserving the schema — invariant *from Aristotle to Husserl* — of a *subordination of regional ontologies to a general ontology*, does the existential analytic not *remain within the domain of ontology and of ground*, of beings as such, rather than passing into the domain of their *disclosure*, the *site of the truth of being*? Is it not *here, first of all*, Franck asks, that the language of *Sein und Zeit* must be qualified as *metaphysical* — since the hermeneutic of *Dasein* seeks to *found* the "vulgar" temporality of traditional ontology in the *ekstatic temporality* shown to be the originary meaning of existence (i.e. it still operates by grounding)?

Franck reports that Heidegger himself recognized the difficulty, and quotes the self-criticism: the very title *fundamental ontology* proves, like every other title in this case, *embarrassing*. Thought from out of metaphysics, the title says something correct — but for that very reason it *misleads*, because what is at stake is to obtain the *passage from metaphysics to the thinking of the truth of being*. So long as this thinking characterizes *itself* as fundamental ontology, it puts itself, by that very name, *on its own path* and obscures it; for the title suggests that the thinking which tries to think the *truth of being* (and not, like all ontology, the *truth of beings*) is itself still a kind of *ontology*. Yet the thinking of the truth of being, as a *step back* to the ground of metaphysics, has — from its very first step — *left behind* the domain of all ontology. Franck then asks the pointed methodological question that the chapter keeps circling: can one *accede to the truth of being under a name that refers back to metaphysics*, and *unsay* metaphysics in the very saying of it? These questions, he notes, already indicate the *burden* that the later analysis of the *mode of being of discourse* (speech, *Rede*) will have to bear.

Franck returns to the constructive thread to explain *why* the existential analytic is the seat of the philosophical problematic, and to introduce the crucial distinction of *propriety* and *impropriety* (the *eigentlich*/*uneigentlich*, authentic/inauthentic pair). The existential analytic is the seat of the philosophical problematic because *Dasein* is that *region of beings* in which all other ontic regions announce themselves and to which they necessarily refer. It is therefore fitting, having determined the exemplary being, to render it *accessible as it is in itself*; for although the ontico-ontological primacy of *Dasein* does not imply that *Dasein* is *from the outset transparent* about its own being, that transparency is nevertheless *required* for the question of being to be rigorously posed. One thing is to recognize the capital role the structure of questioning assigns to *Dasein*; *another* is to *manifest its being*.

Here Franck states one of the most important acquisitions of the existential analytic: *Dasein* is *ordinarily deported from its own being* — it exists in an *improper* understanding of itself, of others, and of the world. The being that we are is *most often exiled from its own being*, so that this own being is revealed to us *as if at a distance*. Franck quotes the paradox: *Dasein* is not only ontically close, indeed *the closest* — we are it ourselves — but *despite* or *precisely because of that*, it is *ontologically the most distant*. Understanding its being, *Dasein* always already stands within a *certain interpretation* of that being, an interpretation that, *lacking originarity*, cannot serve as the guiding thread for ontological thematization. For *Dasein* tends most often to understand itself *from out of the intraworldly beings* with which it deals and about which it is concerned, so that the *everyday understanding of the world* furnishes the model on which the understanding of *Dasein* — and of being in general — is regulated. It is thus *because of its ontico-ontological primacy* that *Dasein* is *concealed from itself*: ontically it is closest to itself, ontologically furthest, though *pre-ontologically* it is not a stranger to itself.

Franck draws an instructive parallel with Husserl to clarify the proper/improper distinction and the need for description before reduction. Just as, according to Husserl, *transcendental subjectivity* — covered over by the *psyche* (ψυχή) — surges forth in its own being *thanks to the reduction* (the psyche being only its *mundanization*), so likewise *Dasein* initially understands itself *from out of the world of things*; better, it *interprets itself as it interprets that world*, on the basis of what it encounters there. This self-understanding *in the horizon of what is other than itself* is *improper*, and it refers — if only by way of a *possible modification* — to a *proper* understanding issuing from a *properly own ipseity (selfhood)*. *Dasein* exists *properly or improperly*.

The methodological consequence follows. If the *ordinary* mode of being of *Dasein* *turns it away from its own propriety*, then one must *begin* by describing *Dasein* in its essential, constitutive structures *as it exists "most closely and most often,"* in its *average everydayness* — and only *then* bring to appearance *existence properly understood*, that is, the *existentiell modification* that makes such a *reversal (retournement)* possible, together with its *existential conditions of possibility*. The order is fixed: everydayness first, then the turn to authenticity, then the conditions that make the turn possible.

Franck announces that the preliminary analytic of *Dasein* fulfills this program by joining, *in one and the same movement*, a *reduction*, a *construction*, and a *destruction* — the three components of the phenomenological method as Heidegger (here following the 1927 *Basic Problems*) understands it. He takes them in turn.

(1) **The reduction.** If being is always the being *of beings*, then it offers itself and is graspable only *starting from beings*. The phenomenological gaze must therefore *orient itself on beings* in order to let their *being* emerge — it must *aim at beings in order to move away from them, returning to their being*. Franck quotes the definition: the fundamental component of the phenomenological method, in the sense of the *leading-back of the inquiring gaze from naively grasped beings toward being*, is what is designated *phenomenological reduction*. Franck immediately marks the contrast with Husserl: this reduction shares with Husserl's *transcendental* reduction *only the purely formal trait* of a *conversion of the gaze* — and nothing of its content. (Where Husserl's reduction leads back to transcendental subjectivity, Heidegger's leads from beings to their being.)

Franck turns to the second component. (2) **The construction.** To go *from beings to being*, one must beforehand *already stand alongside being* (one must hold oneself near being in advance). And since being *is not a being*, the *openness to being* will be essentially different from the *givenness of beings*. Franck quotes: being is not accessible *like* a being; we do not *simply find it lying before us*, but — as he will show — it must always be *brought into view in a free projection (project)*. The *projection of the pre-given being*, with regard to its being and its ontological structures, is what is called *phenomenological construction*.

Franck adds a scholarly observation about the word and then deepens its sense. This expression occurs *only once* in *Sein und Zeit*, but it is what *defines the very idea of fundamental ontology* in *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics*. There, having recalled that *only a projective understanding* is capable of reaching the being of any being — and singularly of *Dasein* — Heidegger concludes that the *express implementation* of a comprehending projection can only be a *construction*. What, then, is the specificity of *ontological* construction? Franck quotes: the *fundamental-ontological* construction has the peculiarity of having to *bring out the intrinsic possibility* of something that *reigns over all Dasein* as that which is *most known to it but indeterminate and all too evident*. This construction may be understood as an *offensive of Dasein upon itself*, undertaken in order to grasp the *originary metaphysical fact* — namely, that the *most finite* is, *in its finitude*, well known *without being thereby conceived (conceptualized)*. Franck then notes the upshot: the emphasis on *construction* falls at the same stroke on the *originary finitude of Dasein*. And since this finitude — which is *nothing other than the understanding of being* — *reposes in forgetting*, every construction will also be a *re-membering (remémoration)* — the thought completing in the next paragraph.

Franck completes the point about remembering and then sets out the third component. Every construction will also be a *remembering* — but *not* in the Hegelian sense of a *sublation (relève / Aufhebung)* of finitude in the advent of *absolute knowing*; rather as the *finite revelation of finitude itself*. (The contrast with Hegel is deliberate: where Hegel's recollection cancels and preserves finitude in absolute knowledge, Heidegger's remembering reveals finitude *as* finitude, without overcoming it.)

(3) **The destruction.** The constructing project *encounters obstacles*. *Dasein* will appear in its finitude, and as *understanding of being*, in full propriety, only on the condition that the *interpretation it gives of itself* be *limited and restricted in its pertinence*. Now the hermeneutic of everyday existence shows that *Dasein* does not only have a *disposition to fall* alongside its world, in which it is, and to interpret itself in turn *from out of that world*; *Dasein* also and *simultaneously falls into a tradition* more or less explicitly grasped. This tradition *discharges Dasein* of its own conduct, of its own *questioning* and *choosing* — and this holds *especially* for that understanding, and its possibilities of elaboration, which is rooted in the *ownmost being of Dasein*: *ontological* understanding. Franck draws the strong conclusions: this means that *Dasein is its past*; that the question of being is *essentially historical*; but also that *Dasein's everyday self-apprehension is bound up with the traditional becoming of Greek ontology*, in which being was understood — along the guiding thread of the *natural world* — as *ousia, parousia, Anwesenheit*, **presence**, *without this temporal sense ever being genuinely put into question*. The history of ontology *covers over* the possibility of fundamental-ontological construction; it constitutes a *forgetting of the first experiences of being* that lie at the origin of metaphysics. In posing the question of being, *Dasein* returns upon this *forgetting of the origins*, undergoes the test of its *ownmost potentiality-for-being*, and *destroys the sediments* that contain and hold back the power of the beginnings. The remembering *is* a destruction, because the available philosophical concepts are *no longer forged out of* what they claim to conceive, and indeed *obliterate* it. Franck closes with the citation: there belongs necessarily to the conceptual interpretation of being and its structures — to the *reductive construction* of being — a *destruction*, a critical *deconstruction* of the *received concepts* (which are at first necessarily in use), tracing them *back to the sources* from which they were drawn; only by this destruction can ontology phenomenologically assure itself fully of the *authenticity of its concepts*.

Franck now presses the destruction toward a difficulty that *Sein und Zeit* leaves unresolved, and that points beyond it. If the destruction of the history of ontology is required in order to accede to the *own being of Dasein* and to pose the question of the meaning of being, then *Dasein*, in order to carry out this destruction, must *first cease to understand itself within the exclusive horizon of what it destroys*. Franck insists there is *no circular presupposition* here, since *propriety and impropriety* are two manners of being that belong *structurally* to all *Dasein* (so *Dasein* is not trapped in the tradition; it always also has the resource of propriety).

But — and here is the genuine difficulty — there is a *graver* problem that, if one holds to *Sein und Zeit*, remains *unresolved*. If the *forgetting* of being and the *openness* to being are respectively correlative to two modes of existing, *each* of which is constitutive of the essence of *Dasein* — if *Dasein* stands *co-originarily* in *truth and in non-truth* — then *why has the question of being not always been posed, just as it has always been omitted*? Franck escalates the question into the register of *epochality*: in what sense is *Sein und Zeit* an *event*, in what sense does it *make an epoch*, and *how can one circumscribe the epoch of Sein und Zeit without thinking the epochality of being* — which would mean *leaving Sein und Zeit itself behind*? The point is sharp: the symmetry of truth and untruth in the structure of *Dasein* cannot, by itself, explain *why* forgetting has *historically* prevailed; to explain that, one would need a thinking of the *history/epochality of being* that *Sein und Zeit* does not yet provide — another way in which the book points past itself.

Franck now asks after the third term governing the method: the reduction, construction, and destruction are all *phenomenological* — but what does "phenomenological" mean, and how does Heidegger understand phenomenology? He begins from Heidegger's own first determination: the expression "phenomenology" designates in the first place a *concept of method*; it does not characterize the *quidditative content* (the *what*) of the objects of philosophical research, but its *how* (*quomodo*). And yet, Franck notes, if phenomenology is *in the first place* a concept of method, it must receive a *second signification* coordinated with the first — which one, and how?

He works through the etymology as Heidegger does. *Phenomenology* derives from the Greek *phainomenon* and *logos*. **Phenomenon** signifies *that which shows itself in itself*, and this determination is at the foundation of all the other senses the word can or could take. **Logos** is translated as *discourse* and means *dēloun* — to render *patent* that of which one discourses; Aristotle explicates this function of discourse as an *apophainesthai*, so that the *logos* is *apophantic* discourse. The relation between phenomenon and logos then appears at once: the expression "phenomenology" can be formulated in Greek as *legein ta phainomena* — *to let be seen, from itself, that which shows itself from itself*. Such is the *formal sense* of the research that gives itself the name phenomenology.

Franck draws out what this formal definition tacitly presupposes, and thereby reaches phenomenology's *material* content. Phenomenology *prescribes* a mode of *givenness and exhibition* that seems *independent* of what is to be given and shown. But this formal prescription would have *no sense whatever* if phenomena simply *offered themselves to the first glance*. Phenomenology therefore presupposes — *precisely as a methodical prescription* — that phenomena *begin by not showing themselves*; it concerns *what remains most hidden and most in withdrawal*: **being itself**.

He confirms this with a long citation. What must, in a *privileged* sense, be named "phenomenon"? What is, according to its essence, the *necessary theme* of an *express* showing? Manifestly, something that *most closely and most often does precisely not show itself*, something that — by contrast with what does show itself most closely and most often — is *concealed* (*verborgen*), but which *at the same time* belongs *essentially* to what shows itself most closely and most often, since it constitutes its *meaning and its ground*. What, in an *exceptional* sense, remains concealed, or falls back into concealment, or shows itself only in *disguise*, is *not this or that being* but — as the preceding considerations have shown — the *being of beings*. This can be so largely covered over that it is *forgotten*, and that any question about it and its meaning *goes missing*. That which therefore *demands*, in a privileged sense and from out of its *ownmost content*, *to become phenomenon* — *this* is what phenomenology has thematically "seized upon" as its object. Phenomenology is the *mode of access* to, and the *legitimating mode of determination* of, what is to become the *theme of ontology*; and therefore: *ontology is possible only as phenomenology*. Franck glosses the structure: the formal definition of phenomenology allows one, by reflecting back on *what it presupposes*, to *assign a thematic content* to the method of philosophical research. Phenomenology does not have a *formal* sense external to a *"material"* sense; it has a *single* sense in the *unity of two moments*. If a phenomenology is *needed* to characterize how phenomena are to be given and received, this is precisely because they are *from the outset covered over and dissimulated*.

Franck completes the previous citation and consolidates the identification of phenomenology with ontology, while planting two consequences he will need later. The phenomenon *begins by disappearing*; this *disappearing is the initial appearing* of the phenomenon, the appearing that phenomenology *conquers as its theme*. He quotes the formula: *being-covered* is the *counter-concept* of "phenomenon." The phenomenological method, in its philosophical use, will therefore have to do with *that which, par excellence, beings conceal and obscure*: their *being*. Phenomenology, by its *obligatory content*, *is ontology*.

Franck then appends three remarks on this "definitively *provisional*" elaboration of the concept of phenomenology — the three points of the next paragraph's argument, which I take in order.

Franck spells out the three remarks signaled in the prior paragraph, and they are programmatic for the rest of the book.

(1) *Never*, he says — provided one is careful *not to reduce method to its modern, Cartesian sense* — has the *theme* of a philosophical research been so tightly *knotted* to, indeed *resolved into*, the very *method of investigation* it demands; never has inquiry made itself so *radically methodical*, to the point of taking *the very act of seizing (the prise)* as its theme. So much so, Franck adds, that the *profoundly renewed* use of the *a priori* could *by itself* command a reading of *Sein und Zeit* — and the subsequent course of Heidegger's meditations, he says, will amply confirm this. (This flags the *a priori* as a thread Franck will pull on; it will return decisively at the book's end, where the de-temporalization of space puts the very possibility of an *a priori* in question.)

(2) Since phenomenology *presupposes the withdrawal of being*, it is the *counter-proof of the forgetting of being*: it draws its very *possibility from alētheia* — from *disclosure* (*Unverborgenheit*), since *only* unconcealment can let *concealment as such* appear — and it must therefore be understood as a *movement of recession* back toward the *dimension within which the Greek world deploys itself*. (Phenomenology is thus inseparable from the Greek experience of truth as unconcealment.)

(3) As an *experience of the forgetting of being*, phenomenology *can no longer hold sway* once "*being vanishes with the Ereignis*" — once being is *no longer to the measure of appropriation*, i.e. of what Heidegger devoted himself to thinking *after Sein und Zeit*. Franck is marking the *limit* of phenomenology: it belongs to the project that thinks being in terms of *presence/withdrawal*, and it reaches its term when the late thinking passes over to *Ereignis*. (This sets up the book's larger claim that the problem of space cannot be resolved within the phenomenological-temporal framework and pushes toward the later "topology of being.")

In the closing paragraph of the chapter, Franck gathers the results into a definition of philosophy and then opens, with a single unusual text, the question of *others* that the next chapter will pursue. Ontology and phenomenology together define philosophy *according to its object and its mode of treatment*. The *universal phenomenological ontology* whose theme is being — "*the transcendens pure and simple*" — is a *transcendental philosophy in the ekstatic sense* which, taking its guiding thread in *Dasein*, accomplishes itself in the form of an *analytic of the existentiality of existence*. Through this analytic, *Dasein* elucidates the *understanding of being* that, *as finitude*, is the *proper meaning of its very being*.

But here Franck seizes on a remarkable tension. The *transcendence of the being of Dasein*, he quotes, has this remarkable feature: that it implies the *possibility and the necessity of the most radical individuation*. Accordingly, the existential analytic develops the *understanding* that *Dasein*, in a *metaphysical isolation without recourse* — because founded on its *insubstitutable own finitude* — entertains of itself. And *yet*, Heidegger says in a *singular text* that Franck highlights, this very understanding "constitutes precisely the *first and proper openness of Dasein* to its *ownmost potentiality-for-being* as the *hearing of the voice of the friend that every Dasein carries with it*." Franck presses the paradox with a series of questions. Could the *call* to proper existence, to *guilt*, the *call of conscience*, be *issued by another*, at the risk of *breaking the individual unity of Dasein* and altering its *ipseity*? *Who* is this *Dasein* capable of *summoning* every *Dasein* to its guilty potentiality-for-being, so as to become thereby the "*conscience of others*"? Is it *the one who projects the existential analytic as his own existentiell possibility* — i.e. the *philosopher*? Is the philosopher not, then, the *friend of Dasein*, as the poet is the *friend of the house*?

Franck closes the chapter by extracting the consequence regardless of how those questions are answered: the very fact that *Dasein* *exposes, analyzes, and interprets itself in the listening to the benevolent voice of a friend* signifies that the existential analytic is indeed a *philosophy* — but *also* a *hermeneutics*, if (as he quotes) *hermēneuein* is "that *exposition* which gives something to be known insofar as it is *able to hear a message*." The chapter thus ends by binding the analytic to *hearing* (echoing paragraphs 7–8) and by quietly introducing the problem of the *other* — the friend whose voice is somehow internal to *Dasein*'s ownmost selfhood — which prepares the turn, in the next chapter ("Neutrality and Incarnation"), toward the body, sexuality, and the question of how *Dasein* is plural and embodied at all.

# **Chapter II — Neutrality and Incarnation**

Franck reopens with the question the previous chapter left poised: *who* is the *Dasein* whose prerogatives have just been secured? The answer gathers the two basic ontological characters that launch the existential analytic proper. *Dasein* is the being that we ourselves are, that *I* am, whose being is *always mine* (the "mineness," *Jemeinigkeit*), and to whose being it belongs *to relate to its being* — that is, *to exist*. Franck stresses that "existence" here, naming the *openness* of a being to its own being through the understanding it has of it, retains *nothing* of the old sense of *existentia*. Against metaphysics, which understands *existentia* as *reality (actuality)*, Heidegger labors throughout *Sein und Zeit* to carry out an "ontological differentiation of existence and reality," so as to give the words *existence* and *Dasein* a sense wholly other than the traditional one.

But — and this is the hinge of the paragraph — the phenomenological destruction, in going back to the origins of ontology so as to grant its history a *restricted* right, does not thereby cease to be *faithful* to the acquisitions of the tradition it puts to the test. What was the major theme of *past* ontology must therefore, in some fashion, be *integrated* into fundamental ontology. Under what name and what concept, then, does "reality" come to fall within the existential analytic, whose novelty demands its own language and conceptuality? To signify the being that does *not* have the mode of being of *Dasein*, Heidegger chooses the name *Vorhandenheit*. And, like every naming, this one does not go without an *interpretation* — so Franck asks what its presuppositions are, situating the answer in Heidegger's critique of the medieval thesis (which, on a horizon of understanding being as *production*, divides being into *essentia* and *existentia*).

Franck now lets Heidegger justify the choice of *Vorhandenheit* and extracts from it the problem that will, he says, *coincide with the present work as a whole*. He quotes the 1927 explanation: the analysis of the word *existentia* has already shown that *actualitas* refers to the *acting* (*Handeln*) of some indeterminate subject — or, in Heidegger's terminology, that the *being-before-the-hand* (*das Vorhandene*) is, *according to its sense*, referred to that *for which* it in some way *comes before the hand* (*vor die Hand*), that for which it is a *handle-able*, manageable being. There thus shows itself — however indeterminately — a *relation to the "subject," to Dasein*: *having before the hands*, the being-at-hand as the *product of a production*, the effected of an effectuation, corresponding to the sense of *actualitas* and of *energeia*, i.e. to the tradition of this concept.

Franck then springs the problem. This text authorizes translating *Vorhandenheit* literally as *being-before-the-hand* (in ordinary German, *vorhanden* qualifies what is *there*, subsisting). But the very term retained to interpret the Greek *ousia* and to mark the *ontological relation* between *Dasein* and the being it is *not* — does it not *presuppose that Dasein is endowed with hands, that it is incarnate (embodied)*? If the ontological relation between *Dasein* and the other being *is said by way of the hand*, must not *incarnation be a structure of Dasein*, an *existential*, and finally a *mode of temporalization*? And if that is *not* the case, then would it not be precisely in an *impossible but necessary existential incarnation* that one must seek the motives for which the *spatiality of Dasein is irreducible to its temporality*? Franck gives two reasons the question is not idle. (1) The use of the word *Vorhandenheit* *cannot be metaphorical* if it is true that *there is no metaphor except metaphysical metaphor* (a thesis he credits to Heidegger's *The Principle of Reason*): to treat the concept of *Vorhandenheit* as metaphorical from the outset would be, with no further ado, to inscribe *Sein und Zeit* within the *Platonic partition of the sensible and the intelligible* — exactly the partition the book means to overcome. (2) Heidegger *himself* attempted an existential analysis of incarnation, whose examination requires returning to the hermeneutic of *Dasein*. With this, the chapter's task is set: to follow Heidegger's own existential treatment of the body.

Franck deepens the first ontological character, *mineness* (*Jemeinigkeit*), and shows that it is *more radical* than the "I." *Dasein* is the being whose being is *always mine*. Mineness is tightly bound to *existence*, because the being of the being that *I am* can be *mine* only on the condition that *I relate to it* — that it is neither indifferent to me nor *originarily closed to sense* in the way a stone's being is closed to the stone. Mineness is the *relation of Dasein to its being* that *makes possible the pronoun "I."* And here Franck states the order of priority with precision: the *I* derives from *mineness*, not mineness from the I; the *\-self* of *myself* precedes the *me*. Mineness therefore constitutes a principle of individuation *more radical* than the saying-"I," which always already implies the *Thou*, the *He*, and so on.

Consequently, to take the *I* as the *point of departure* for an analysis of *my* being is *to miss it*; and one cannot reproach Heidegger for having neglected "the primary evidence of the *cogito*," since that evidence — abstraction made of the ontological interpretation that ordinarily accompanies it — is *phenomenologically subordinate to mineness*. Yet this primary propriety is *nothing of which I am the origin*; it is rather the *relation to being itself as the origin of the self*. Franck confirms this with a citation: the determination "always mine" means that *Dasein is thrown to me* so that *my self may be the Dasein*; *Dasein* signifies *care* not only for the human being but for the *being of beings as such*, ekstatically revealed in this care. "Always mine" therefore means *neither posited by me nor cut off into an individualized I*; *Dasein* is itself *through its essential relation to being in general* — which is the import of the proposition, frequent in *Sein und Zeit*, that the *understanding of being belongs to Dasein*.

Franck enumerates the considerable *weight and function* of mineness, in three steps that reach forward across Heidegger's whole development. *First*, mineness *founds the distinction of proper and improper existence* that runs through and structures the entire existential analytic. Whatever its mode of being, *Dasein* is always *mine* and relates to its being *as to its own possibility*; he quotes: *Dasein* is *always its possibility*, and does not "possess" it as a property in the manner of a being-at-hand. Being its possibility, *Dasein* can *lose itself or attain itself*, be *properly or improperly*, *be or not be its being*. *Second*, if *Dasein*, in its *ipseity (selfhood)*, is *relation to being itself*, then it is *being itself that is always mine*. Without this, *being* in the sense of the *asked-about* of the question would not be the very being of the interrogated being, and *no ontology would see the light*: the existential analytic *is* fundamental ontology precisely because the *always-mine being of Dasein* is *being as such*. *Third*, mineness *announces the Ereignis*: whereas in *Sein und Zeit* mineness and propriety are conceived *from out of being*, *after Sein und Zeit* being will be thought *from out of appropriation*. Franck is again threading the early work to the late "turn."

Franck introduces a *third* ontological determination — *thematically absent from Sein und Zeit* itself — whose elucidation will lead to the existential analysis of the *possibility of incarnation*: the essential *neutrality (Neutralität)* of *Dasein*. He locates it in the 1928 lecture course *The Metaphysical Foundations of Logic*, where, having identified the understanding of being with *originary transcendence*, Heidegger sets out "the problem of *Sein und Zeit*" in twelve guiding propositions articulated on this essential neutrality. Subordinated to the *single* question of being, the existential analytic is *neither an anthropology nor an ethics*. Hence the **first proposition**, which Franck quotes: for the being that is the theme of the analytic, it is *not* the title "*man*/human" but the *neutral* title "*das Dasein*" that has been chosen — designating thereby the being to which, in a determinate sense, *its own manner of being is not indifferent*.

Franck explains the stakes. Inseparable from mineness and existence — since ipseity is constituted by the relation to *being*, which moreover is *not a genus* — neutrality *withdraws Dasein from the anthropological domain*. What is the *extension* of the region "anthropology"? It encompasses everything concerning the *nature of the human being* as a spiritual, psychic, and *carnal/fleshly* being, everything that distinguishes the human from other living beings or might draw it near them — *including the difference of the sexes*. If *sexuality* and *incarnation* are indeed anthropological traits, then the *neutrality of essence* entails the **second proposition**, which Franck quotes: the specific neutrality of the title "*Dasein*" is essential because the interpretation of this being must be carried out *before any factical concretion*. This neutrality also means that *Dasein is neither of the two sexes*. But this *asexuality* is *not the indifference of empty nullity*, the impotent negativity of an indifferent ontic nothing; *Dasein* in its neutrality is *not indifferently no-one and everyone*, but the *originary positivity and power of essence*.

Franck draws out the meaning of neutrality and then discovers within it a grave threat. The *neutral Dasein* is never *this or that factically incarnate existent*, but the *possibility of every incarnate existence that belongs to itself*. So it cannot be confused with *factical ontic individuality*; yet neither is it the *indifferent concretion of a supposedly universal essence* — for here the affirmation of *neutrality* coincides with the affirmation of the *most radical individuation: ipseity*. Franck supports this with the 1929 essay *On the Essence of Ground* (directly issued from the cited course): it is *only because Dasein as such is determined by ipseity* that a *myself* can stand in relation to a *thyself*. Ipseity is the *presupposition* of the possibility of *egoity* — which never reveals itself except in the *thou* — but ipseity is *never bound to the thou*; rather it first *makes the thou possible*, being *neutral* with respect to being-I and being-thou, and *still more* with respect to *sexuality*. Every essential proposition of an ontological analytic of *Dasein* in the human being considers this being *in advance* in this neutrality.

Then the difficulty. The analyses of the existential hermeneutic — both that of *proper* existence (where *Dasein* understands itself from itself and its *being-toward-death*) and that of *improper* existence (where it understands itself within the orbit of *being-at-hand*) — are governed by this *neutrality of essence*. But the *proper ipseity*, essentially neutral, is an *existentiell modification of the "one"* (*das Man*, the impersonal "they"), the pronoun that declines the identity of the *everyday, fallen, irresolute, improper Dasein*. And this *"one" is also neutral*, but *in a second sense*. There are therefore *two neutralities*, not one and the same neutrality marked twice: the *essential* neutrality of *Dasein* is *not* that of a "no-one and everyone," i.e. not that of *das Man*. From this Franck poses the cutting question: *how*, and above all by *whom* — by *which Dasein*, in *which mode of being* — can the *neutrality of origin* be discriminated from the *neutrality in fallenness from origin*? Since *Dasein* can pose the question of being *as the question of its own being* only by *tearing itself from the grip of das Man* to be a *proper ipseity*, the *rigorous demarcation* of these two senses of neutrality *bears nothing less than the possibility of the question of being itself*. The thematic introduction of neutrality therefore *gravely threatens*, by backlash, the *general economy of phenomenological ontology* — and Franck notes we here meet again, *transformed*, the question of how *Sein und Zeit* lets itself be understood within an *existentially constructed history*.

Franck asks *what motives* this addition of neutrality obeys, and answers by tracing the 1928 transformation of the very idea of fundamental ontology. It is doubtless in *The Metaphysical Foundations of Logic* that Heidegger *reiterated for a last time*, or *reinterpreted for the first time*, the project of a fundamental ontology *as analytic of Dasein*; its recastings testify to the *progressive emergence of the ontological difference*. Fundamental ontology remains *interpretation of existence* and *analytic of the temporality of being* — but this latter, which was to take its place in the "Time and Being" division, is now *simultaneously* understood as "the *turning* by which ontology itself *explicitly turns back* into a *metaphysical ontic* in which it always implicitly stands." The temporal interpretation of being *inflects toward the ontological difference*; and to pose the *problem of metaphysics* one must "bring ontology to the *reversal* (*Umschlag*, translating *metabolē*) latent within it. There the turning is accomplished, and the reversal takes place in *metontology*."

Franck then sets out the *three intrinsically linked imperatives* that this reversal of fundamental ontology into *metontology* answers. (1) *First*, not to *absolutize* the question of being, but to also *thematize the totality of beings* — for "to think being as the being of beings and to grasp the problem of being in a radical, universal manner means at the same time *to thematize beings in totality* in the light of ontology"; from this angle, metontology *accentuates the finitude of philosophizing itself*. (2) *Second*, to *account for* — by covering the field of philosophical problems that fundamental ontology does *not* exhaust on its own — what will later be named the *onto-theo-logical constitution of metaphysics*: "fundamental ontology and metontology together form, in their unity, the concept of metaphysics," which only expresses the transformation of the *fundamental problem of philosophy* already touched on with the double concept of philosophy as *prōtē philosophia* and *theologia* — and this is nothing but the *concretion of the ontological difference*, the concretion of the *accomplishment of the understanding of being*. (3) *Third*, to *gather philosophy as the metaphysics of existence upon the ontological difference* — in short, to *think the ontological difference in the conceptuality of Sein und Zeit*. For that, *Dasein* insofar as it *understands being* must be *assimilated to the ontological difference itself*; and, Franck asks pointedly, how could it be, *without being essentially neutral*? Neutrality, that is, is what allows *Dasein* to coincide with the difference between being and beings.

Franck draws the double function of neutrality together and pivots to the body. Neutrality aims, *in one and the same movement*, to *preserve* the existential analytic from every *anthropological deviation*, and it allows one to *cut through* the residual ambiguity of certain statements of *Sein und Zeit* where *Dasein* seems to be *confused with the human being* (Franck even notes Heidegger's own marginal gloss equating "*being-human*" and "*Dasein*" at one such place). But *essence is the essence of its own fact*: even if the essence of the human *diverges from* the human, it is nonetheless the *neutral Dasein* that constitutes the *possibility of the factical existent*. And that factical existent is *always incarnate and sexed*. Therefore to *affirm the neutrality of Dasein* is, in return, to *give oneself the task of elucidating the existential possibility of the flesh*. The problem can then take this form: *by what right does Dasein hold the possibility of its own incarnation; under what existential does the flesh let itself be understood?*

Franck presents Heidegger's answer: *incarnation presents itself as a dispersion*. He quotes: *Dasein* harbors the *intrinsic possibility of factical dispersion into the flesh*, and with that, into *sexuality*; *Dasein* is, as factical, *always scattered into a flesh* and thereby *always divided into a determinate sexuality*. Franck insists on the obvious but crucial constraint: the *sexed flesh of Dasein must not be ontologically incompatible* with this being's *mode of being*. Consequently, the concepts of *dispersion, scattering, division* that describe incarnation and sexuality *cannot have an axiological (value-laden) signification*, nor can they be tributary to a *representation of the one and the many drawn from the ontology of being-at-hand*. Rather they designate a *multiplication at work in every factical Dasein*, of which the existential analytic indicates *other possibilities*, recalled at the close of the **sixth guiding proposition**. Franck lists them: (a) factical *Dasein* is *dispersed in and by the modes of its preoccupation*, and "never comports itself toward a *single* object," except "by abstraction from the other beings that always co-appear *beforehand and simultaneously*" — and this dispersion does *not* depend on a *prior plurality of objects*; on the contrary, the *multiplicity of objects is founded in the being-dispersed of Dasein*. (b) Dispersion also concerns *Dasein* in its relation *to itself*, i.e. in its *historicity*, since by *abandoning itself to world-history* it disperses into the *inconstancy of the self*. (c) "Another essential possibility of the factical dispersion of *Dasein* is its *spatiality*." Franck quotes the decisive sentence: the phenomenon of *Dasein*'s dispersion *in space* shows itself, for example, in the fact that *all languages are primarily determined by spatial significations*; this phenomenon, however, can be clarified only *once the metaphysical problem of space has been posed* — a problem that will *become visible only by passing through that of temporality* (radically: a *metontology of spatiality*). This is a key citation for Franck's whole book: Heidegger himself ties language's pervasive spatial significations to a still-unposed "metaphysical problem of space."

Franck distinguishes the *derivative* from the *originary* sense of dispersion, securing the grounding of incarnation. All these dispersive possibilities are bound up with *fallenness*, i.e. with a *derived, improper understanding of existence*. They must therefore *necessarily be rooted in an originary and proper structure of Dasein*. He quotes: to the essence of *Dasein* in general, in conformity with the *metaphysical neutrality of its concept*, there *already* belongs an *originary dissemination* (*Streuung*) which, from a determinate point of view, is a *dispersion* (*Zerstreuung*). It is this *originary, neutral dissemination* — which Heidegger more often calls *transcendental dispersion* — that *makes possible its own fallen modalization: factical dispersion*. And since *fallenness* is a concept relative to the *ontological movement* of *Dasein* insofar as it is *thrown into its being*, *transcendental dispersion takes its foundation in thrownness (being-thrown)*, which finally assures the *existential possibility of sexed incarnation*. The body, that is, is grounded not in fallenness but in the originary thrown dispersion of *Dasein*.

Franck identifies the phenomenal trait that lets flesh be thought as belonging to being-dispersed — *sexual division* — and then exposes a tension. It is by *sexuality*, *formally reduced to a multiplication*, that the flesh is *referred to the factical dispersion of Dasein* so as to be *founded on thrownness*. This interpretation *requires that sexuality itself be understood existentially*. But — as the **ninth proposition** attests — that is *not* the case. Franck quotes it: the essentially *thrown dispersion* of *Dasein*, understood *quite neutrally*, announces itself notably in this, that *Dasein is being-with* (*Mitsein*) with *Dasein*. This *being-with-with* does *not* arise on the ground of a *factical coexistence*; not only is it not explained on the basis of a supposedly more originary *generic being* of *sexually divided carnal beings*, but this common *generic impulse and generic union* have, as their *metaphysical presupposition*, the *dispersion of Dasein as such*, that is, *being-with in general*. This fundamental metaphysical character of *Dasein* can *never* be derived from *generic organization* and from the *life of beings with one another*.

Franck reads off the result and the worry. *Being-with* is a *mode of transcendental dispersion* presupposed by *every factical coexistence and every sexed incarnation*; it is therefore the *existential a priori* of every *carnal-sexual relation* between factical existents, the *a priori* of every "generic union." But — and here the *Vorhandenheit* problem of paragraph 2 returns at a higher level — *how can generic being*, which *by way of the genus pertains to being-at-hand*, find its *possibility in an existential whose mode of being is absolutely other*? Or, the same question: *can one understand the carnal-sexual relations between factical Dasein as "generic union" without surreptitiously conceiving existence within the horizon of being-at-hand* — thereby *contradicting the most sustained effort of Sein und Zeit*? Franck adds: there is more. *Being-with* is a *fundamental metaphysical determination of dispersion* that forms the existential condition of possibility of *all coexistence* — whether subjected to the *dictatorship of das Man* or having the status of an *alliance between Dasein freely resolved upon their own being*.

Franck shows that *dispersion* equivocates across the proper/improper divide in exactly the way *neutrality* did, and presses the resulting aporia. Dispersion *also* qualifies the mode of being of the *everyday Dasein fallen into das Man*: he quotes — as the *one-self* (*das Man-selbst*), the singular *Dasein* is *dispersed into das Man* and must *first find itself*; this dispersion characterizes the "subject" of the mode of being we know as *preoccupied absorption in the world* encountered nearby. And *fallen dispersion* is the mode of existence *most opposed to resolution*, since it is a moment of *curiosity*, whose temporal sense is the *fleeting, skittering present* — "that mode of the present \[which\] is the phenomenon *most contrary to the instant* (*Augenblick*, the moment-of-vision). In the former, *Dasein* is *everywhere and nowhere*; the latter carries existence into the *situation* and reveals the *proper "there."*"

From this Franck rebuilds the aporia of paragraph 6 in the register of dispersion. If *neutral transcendental dispersion*, under the figure of *being-with*, is the *a priori* both of the *coexistence dispersed in neutral das Man* and of the *coexistence founded on resolution* (which is a *counter-dispersion*), then *how* and by *whom* — *which Dasein*, in *which mode of being* — can the *neutral transcendental dispersion* be discerned from the *dispersion in neutral das Man*, the *fallen coexistence* from the *resolute coexistence*? — all the more so since, as already seen, the *two senses of neutrality cannot be dissociated and contrasted*. In other words: to *which Dasein* is one to assign this *essentially neutral dispersion* that makes *being-with* possible in *both* the proper and the improper modes, when, on one hand, "*Dasein* always exists in one of these two modes, or in a *modal indifference* with respect to them," and, on the other hand, the *neutrality of essence is not an indifference*? The problem is that the very being who would have to draw the distinction is itself always already on one side or in modal indifference — so no neutral standpoint is available from which to draw it.

Franck closes the chapter by converting the whole analysis into a verdict that points straight at the book's thesis. The thesis of the *originary neutrality of Dasein* poses the *problem of its incarnation*, which the recourse to *transcendental dispersion* is meant to *resolve*. But does the introduction of these two concepts (neutrality and transcendental dispersion) into the existential analytic not gravely *perturb its internal equilibrium*? Does it not *withdraw Dasein from the difference of the proper and the improper*? Does it not entail that *the being we are cannot incarnate itself without excepting itself from its temporal sense* — since *propriety and impropriety are modes of temporalization*? In other words: if incarnation is grounded in a *neutral* transcendental dispersion that lies *beneath* the proper/improper distinction, then the embodied being slips out of the very framework (the modes of temporalization) by which *Sein und Zeit* claimed to account for every existential structure.

And Franck makes the closing analogy explicit. Is this *exception* not *analogous, even identical*, to that of *spatiality* — which *every dispersion*, and the very word *dis-persion*, *implies* — *spatiality that we know to be irreducible to temporality*? Is it not, he asks, *already manifest* that the questions and aporias raised by *flesh and space* lead to the very *limits of the conceptuality* whose resources *Sein und Zeit* deploys *in the service of the over-power (surpuissance) of time*? The chapter thus ends by aligning the body with space as the two phenomena that strain Heidegger's temporal framework to its breaking point — preparing the next chapter's turn to "The Hand and the World."

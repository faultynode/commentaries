# ***Chair et corps: Sur la phénoménologie de Husserl*** **(1981)**

## **Preface**

Franck opens not with Husserl but with the question of *thinking* (*la pensée*) as such, and he does so in a register that is unmistakably Heideggerian. Whatever way one determines what thinking is — and he immediately concedes that determining this may today be the very highest difficulty rather than something achievable — and whatever tasks thinking sets itself, its exercise presupposes a deepened, renewed, and as it were ceaseless determination of the *essence of philosophy*. The list of possible tasks he runs through ("to think that we do not yet think," "to think that what most gives us to think is that we do not yet think," "to think that thinking means nothing") quietly cites Heidegger's *Was heißt Denken?* The point is structural: thinking has and will have its place only "in memory of philosophy." Even if we were to renounce *overcoming* metaphysics — and Franck notes that the very effort to overcome metaphysics is still a way of relating to it — and were to leave metaphysics to itself, this renunciation and abandonment, if it is not to degenerate into pure and simple abandonment, into foolishness (*niaiserie*), will always imply at least a knowledge of what it sets at a distance. Hence the lapidary closing line: forgetting (*l'oubli*) is here the highest memory. The paragraph establishes the wager of the whole book: a return to Husserl is not antiquarianism but a condition of thinking at the end of philosophy.

Franck specifies *from where* thinking is to come: necessarily from the end of philosophy, the closure (*clôture*) of metaphysics — that is, from the accomplishment or completion of modern philosophy. And modern philosophy is, originally and fundamentally, Cartesian. He marshals an agreement between Hegel and Heidegger across their otherwise divergent concepts of history, of philosophy, and of the history of philosophy: both take Descartes to be the initiator of the ultimate epoch of philosophy. The footnote assembles the textual proof — Hegel calling Descartes the true beginning of modern philosophy, Heidegger saying that the whole of modern metaphysics, Nietzsche included, remains within the interpretation of truth and of beings initiated by Descartes, and that with Descartes begins the *completion* of Western metaphysics. The convergence matters because it licenses Franck's strategy: if Husserl is the most refined repetition of the Cartesian beginning, then reading Husserl well is reading the closure of metaphysics at its most acute point.

Continuing the sentence broken across the page, Franck spells out what the Cartesian establishment of the *cogito* as absolute and certain foundation of all truth entails: the consequent elevation of *method* to the dignity of a theme, a method whose paradigm is mathematical. Together these give modern philosophy the figure of a *mathesis universalis* that is subjectively founded — at once a science of consciousness and a formal mathesis. Thinking demands, as a precondition, the elucidation of this ultimate figure; it requires a *repetition* (in the strong Heideggerian–Kierkegaardian sense). And if Husserlian phenomenology constitutes such a repetition — the deepest and the most refined — then one must tirelessly return to it. This is Franck announcing his thesis about Husserl's place: not one philosopher among others, but the repetition in which modern philosophy completes itself and so becomes legible.

Franck now shows that Husserl himself produced a "poem of the history of philosophy" (the phrase is Husserl's own self-description, drawn from the *Crisis*) in which Descartes is the originary founding genius of all modern philosophy — and this on two counts, because of a *hidden equivocation* in Cartesian thought that rhythms and scans all philosophy up to the emergence of phenomenology. On the one hand, Descartes opens the career of systematic rationalism (more physicist than mathematical, Franck notes) by demanding that philosophical knowledge be absolutely founded, resting on immediate, adequate, and apodictic knowledge; by discovering a new ontological sphere, that of the *ego cogito*, of absolute transcendental subjectivity that precedes by right every thinkable being; in other words, by making room for a transcendental question of the world through a return and recourse to an origin whose mode of being is non-worldly in an unprecedented sense. On the other hand — and here is the equivocation — by interpreting the ego as *mens sive animus sive intellectus*, as psyche, in short as an intra-worldly being, Descartes gives free rein to psychologism, empiricism, scepticism. Descartes is thus at the origin of *transcendental psychologism*, a "displacement that falsifies everything," a falsification perceptible only from transcendental phenomenology. The double paternity is the engine: Descartes founds both phenomenology's possibility and the psychologism phenomenology must overcome.

Such a diagnosis means that philosophy is a *theory of knowledge*, of the knowing subject in its relation to the known object. But Franck immediately turns the screw: if the soul (*l'âme*) is the residue of the abstraction of the body, then after this abstraction the soul appears as a *complement* of that same body and retains, in its sense, a reference to the physical world. One is then led to make knowledge of the physical world itself a physical event, to grant that knowledge has the same mode of being as its object — which is the sceptical "bankruptcy" of philosophy and of science. The crucial move: if Descartes gave rise to psychologism, it is not principally for having identified the soul with the transcendental ego. That identification stems from his having restricted the *epochē* to a doubt that is universal only as an *attempt* to doubt, since the perceiving "I" proves indubitable. And this restriction takes its ultimate origin from Descartes's own question — how to attain certain knowledge of external things? — to which Husserl's prior question responds: how can consciousness go outside itself to posit things as transcendent? The shift is decisive: it is no longer a matter of *assuring* objectivity but of *understanding* it. Already in this preface the body, and the soul as residue-of-the-body's-abstraction, are doing structural work — the seed of the whole "flesh and body" problematic.

The reduction is rendered necessary by the directing problem — the *enigma of transcendence* — and it announces itself from the very setting-up of a descriptive phenomenology. Franck cites Husserl's 1903 warning (in a review): one must not simply call phenomenology a "descriptive psychology." In the proper and strict sense it is not. Its descriptions do not bear on the lived experiences (*vécus*) or classes of lived experiences of empirical persons; of persons, of me and others, of my experiences and others' experiences, it knows nothing and presupposes nothing; on this it poses no question, advances no definition, makes no hypothesis. Phenomenological description considers what is given in the strictest sense, the lived experience as it is in itself. For example, it analyzes the *appearing* of things, not what appears in this appearing, and it sets aside the apperceptions by virtue of which the appearing and what appears enter into correlation with the "I" for which there is an appearing. The quotation establishes phenomenology's neutrality with respect to empirical existence — a neutrality Franck will track all the way to the problem of the other, where it breaks down.

Whatever decisive nuances the opening of the *constitutive* dimension will bring to this program, phenomenology describes — all factuality reduced — the pure sense of knowing, of exteriority, and so on. If one understands the theory of knowledge in the traditional way, as the problem of the reality of the external world, then phenomenology forms its obligatory precondition. The metaphysical *neutrality* of the *Logical Investigations* has the positive significance of a juridical priority of phenomenology over all philosophy. And the passage to transcendental idealism, however strange it may seem, will not contradict this antecedence; on the contrary, it will generalize its extension and confirm its validity. Franck is establishing that phenomenology claims to come *before* any philosophical thesis about the world — a claim of right (*juridique*) that the later analysis of the other will test to destruction.

This is a description that not only does not bear on the empirical but whose very principle is not empirical. It is a matter of *seeing*, certainly, but of seeing and fixing *essences*, of describing the pure sphere of the a priori. This imposes a double enlargement: that of the a priori, through the recognition of a *material* (or contingent) a priori, on the basis of which the restriction of the *mathesis universalis* to the merely formal domain can be lifted; and that of the concepts of intuition, perception, and object, through the discovery of *categorial intuition*. Franck is compressing two of Husserl's great breakthroughs from the *Logical Investigations* — the material a priori and categorial intuition — into the claim that phenomenology widens both what can be intuited and what counts as intuition. (Categorial intuition will return as decisive in Chapter XV, where the "excess" in perception is at stake.)

However succinct these indications, they suffice to show to what depth Husserl's phenomenology *accomplishes* modern philosophy — of which it is, Husserl says, the "secret aspiration" — to the point that the 1923–24 lecture course on first philosophy legitimately opens with a critical history of philosophy teleologically dominated by transcendental phenomenology. But they also suffice to let its *limits* be sensed: the project remains the same, and the absolutization of consciousness reassures one and the same metaphysical position. Far from taking this as a pretext for negligence, Franck sees in it a motive for insistence. For is not such a situation precisely apt to favor the comprehension of the *end of philosophy*, and thereby of the *beginning of thinking*? The more radical the knowledge of subjectivity and its epoch, the more radical the risk and the chance of freeing oneself from it. The reasoning is dialectical: Husserl's very fidelity to the metaphysics of subjectivity makes him the privileged site for understanding — and possibly stepping beyond — that metaphysics.

Completing the sentence: where could one attain this knowledge par excellence if not where subjectivity is thematized in the greatest genius of its productions? In other words: the better we read Husserl, the more we can take the measure of Heidegger. And this proposition, Franck insists, does not belong to the history of philosophy as a regional discipline. This is a methodological declaration of the whole book: reading Husserl is not antiquarian scholarship but the way to measure what Heidegger attempted — and, the book will argue, the way to expose a problem (the flesh) that both Husserl and Heidegger left unthought.

Once the very general necessity of an interpretation of phenomenology is established, the questions of *procedure* arise. How is one to undertake such a work? Along what guiding thread? Which text to retain, given that most of Husserl's books have a programmatic and exemplary status, count as "introductions"? Nothing here goes without anticipation — that is, without *decision*. The affair of phenomenology, its theme, is transcendental subjectivity in the whole extent of its constituting work. This means that the *Cartesian Meditations* entitle (name, govern) all of Husserl's researches — and Husserl himself held them to be his major work. Franck quotes the 1930 letter to Roman Ingarden: this will be the capital work of my life, a fundamental compendium of the philosophy I have developed, with which I can die in peace. (That phrase about dying in peace will return, poignantly, in the book's final lines.) In fact, Franck says, the whole transcendental sphere is there very systematically articulated and explored, even into its lacunae; the different reductions are practiced and...

…set in place in relation to one another, the specificity of intentional analysis disengaged across the long course of its very implementation. All phenomenological problems receive an architectonic and stratified delimitation there, even when their examination is deferred to earlier or later elaborations. But above all, never has the phenomenology of the constitution of the ego by and for itself — which coincides with phenomenology in general — been carried so far. And this holds *in principle* (*principiellement*) for the fifth and last Meditation, which by itself is almost as important as the whole formed by the first four. Husserl repeats it: the fifth Meditation does not have as its end the intentional elucidation of the other, of *Einfühlung* (empathy); it especially does not aim to demonstrate the other's existence. Its aim is to complete the project of raising philosophy to the rank and dignity of a rigorous, absolutely founded science; to that end it deploys the reduction to its most extreme limits, tends toward an ultimate determination of the transcendental field, toward the intentional elucidation of the objectivity of the world, of all objectivity, beginning with that of ideal objects and ideality in general — whose consideration is so essential to phenomenology. In short, the fifth Meditation treats the *objectivity of the object*, thereby posing the question of *the being of intentionality*. The footnote (Fink, countersigned by Husserl, 1933\) reinforces that Meditation V is a deployment of the reduction, not a thematic interpretation of empathy. This is the hinge of Franck's whole choice of text: he will center his interpretation on Meditation V because that is where objectivity, intersubjectivity, and the being of intentionality come to a head.

For these reasons and others that he will bring out and try to justify, Franck has centered his interpretation on the fifth and last Meditation, entitled "Unveiling of the ontological sphere (*Seinssphäre*) as monadological intersubjectivity." The footnote makes three scholarly declarations that govern the whole book: (1) he constantly refers to the German text of the *Cartesianische Meditationen* (Husserliana I, 1950); (2) the 1931 French translation by Peiffer and Levinas, which he consulted, was made from a text that is not the definitive edition, so he will cite only by paragraph number; and (3) once and for all, he has often modified the available French translations, for reasons tied essentially to the intention of his own work. This is Franck telling us that the translations in the book are his own reworkings — a fact that matters because much of his argument turns on precise renderings of *Leib*, *Körper*, *Leibhaftigkeit*, *Empfindnis*, and the like.

## **Chapter One: Self-Givenness and Incarnate Givenness**

Franck opens by stating a general structural feature of phenomenology: it always begins in the space between a particular fact and the essence of that fact. This gap is not a deficiency but the very thing that gives phenomenology its field of inquiry — without the disparity between what is contingently the case and what something essentially is, there would be nothing for phenomenology to investigate. The first fact in question for Husserl is historical: the positive sciences cannot make true progress because their foundations remain obscure, and philosophy itself — which ought to express the unity of knowledge — has lost its unity, fragmented into rival schools that no longer share goals, problems, or methods. The word "philosophy" persists, but what it labels is no longer recognizable as such. Franck inserts a quotation from Husserl describing how, in place of genuine controversy among rival theories that would at least confirm a shared commitment to truth, the contemporary scene offers only the appearance of philosophy: pseudo-reports, pseudo-critiques, the mere semblance of working seriously with and for one another. The point is that even the contestation that ought to bind philosophers together has degenerated.

Building on this diagnosis, Franck adds that the crisis extends beyond philosophy alone. If religious belief has become a hollow formality, then a new faith in autonomous philosophy and rigorous science risks suffering the same fate — being reduced to insignificance. "European humanity" — and here Franck stresses that "Europe" is not really a geographical designation but rather the name for philosophical rationality as such — is therefore in crisis. The crisis is at once historical, intellectual, and spiritual. He then moves to a methodological principle: every fact has its own essence, and to recognize something as a fact is already to have some grasp of what that thing essentially is. This raises the question: on what conception of philosophy's essence does Husserl's diagnosis rest?

Philosophy, on Husserl's understanding, means the unity of all the sciences founded on something absolute — a foundation that is immediate, apodictic (so certain it excludes all conceivable doubt), and self-evident. The mere intuition of such an essence already prescribes a path: it demands reconstruction and a new beginning, because once one sees what philosophy ought to be, one cannot accept its present state. The *eidos* — the essence — functions as a *telos*, a goal that orients action. Although this situation is historical, it is not unprecedented; in fact, it repeats Descartes' situation. Franck cites Husserl's question: is not our unhappy present similar to the one Descartes faced as a young man? This repetition is not accidental — it may be the only way to access the essence (the *eidos*) of historicity itself. Such repetition reveals a crisis that is simultaneously distressing and resolute. Importantly, the return to Descartes is not a return to Cartesianism — that is, not a return to Descartes' specific metaphysical theses (which Husserl will reject) — but a return of the self to itself. Descartes' *Meditations* provides an unsurpassable model of this return because of the radical theoretical freedom that drives it. Yet this turn back to the *ego cogito*, this genuine beginning, leads — as Husserl himself warns at the outset — to a kind of solipsism. What does this solipsism mean? Why does it become an objection threatening transcendental phenomenology as a whole? These questions, Franck says, must be answered by tracing the development of the first four of Husserl's *Cartesian Meditations*.

The *ego cogito* as a beginning in principle must not be confused with a factual point of departure — it is not merely the first thing one happens to encounter. Its actual starting point is the idea of authentic science, an idea borrowed from the traditional sciences as they have been handed down. To avoid presupposing the content of those sciences — or even any norm derived from logic or theory of science — this idea must be taken as a pure hypothesis, a pure possibility. This very conversion of fact into possibility is already the beginning of the phenomenological reduction. Crucially, the idea of an absolutely founded science is not the same as the ideal of a scientifically constituted system, like the mathematical physics that Descartes and Kant took as their model. If one assimilates the two, one obscures from the start the transcendental motive that drives Husserl's project: namely, that the *ego cogito* is not the ultimate apodictic premise from which a chain of deductions can be drawn "in geometrical order," but rather the name for an absolute ontological sphere — a region of being — that is open to experience and intuition. Having reduced factuality in this way, this sphere still remains a general and vague idea that requires more determinate working-out.

No explication is possible without a second act that revives the original idea — what Franck calls *un revivre*, a re-living — meaning that explication requires a subjectivity that responds to itself. By excluding all speculative thinking, one must, as Husserl says, carry out the activity itself in a lived way and determine its immanent sense through direct analysis. The idea of science can be explicated only intentionally, that is, as a noematic phenomenon — as what consciousness has in view.

What, then, in its pure sense, is the intention that runs through and animates the scientific enterprise? What teleological idea constitutes genuine science? It is the idea of a systematic series of true judgments founded upon one another. By "true judgments" Husserl means judgments that are omnitemporal (valid at all times) and intersubjective (valid for everyone), and which, as evident, can be infinitely repeated as the same. Evidence — *Evidenz*, the self-giving of the matter itself — is thus the very meaning of science.

Now, Franck notes, the intentional analysis of evidence is not pursued for its own sake at the beginning of the *Cartesian Meditations*. The text moves quickly toward finding a first, apodictic evidence. Intentional analysis only becomes a real theme when the problem of constitution opens up — that is, the problem of how lived experience (*le vécu*) relates to its correlate, reality. By bringing reality into the life of the ego, evidence will simultaneously confirm two theses that appear opposed but turn out to be linked: solipsism and phenomenological idealism. The guiding principle of all post-*Logical Investigations* analyses of evidence is stated by Husserl in §21 of *Ideas I*: every form of judging-seeing — even the seeing of unconditionally universal truths — falls under the concept of intuitive givenness. Intuitive givenness, the giving of the things themselves, is the most general feature of all evidence, prior to any distinction between adequate and inadequate, assertoric and apodictic, pure and impure, predicative and pre-predicative evidence. Judicative evidence (the intuitive givenness of a state of affairs) refers back to the evidence on which judgment rests — the evidence of substrates. This means that the most original mode in which things themselves are given is, ultimately, perception. But this raises the question: how should perception itself be described?

Perceptual evidence, according to the Sixth *Logical Investigation*, is equivalent to a fulfilled intention. The complete fulfillment of an intention by intuition is what achieves the genuine "*adequatio rei et intellectus*" (the correspondence of thing and intellect — the classical definition of truth). When the concepts of intuition and perception are extended into the categorical sphere — that is, when Husserl shows that not only sensible objects but also categorical formations (like states of affairs, relations, universals) can be intuited — categorical acts gain the ability to give their objects in the very same manner as sensible perceptions give theirs: namely, in the flesh (*en chair*). Franck quotes Husserl: every perceiving consciousness has the peculiarity of being a consciousness of an individual object's own presence "in person." This incarnate givenness — this presence "in the flesh" — does not apply only to real objects. The first edition of the *Logical Investigations* did not characterize the givenness of transcendent things in this way and reserved adequate perception to the incarnation of immanent contents alone, but this is precisely one of the points Husserl systematically revised in the second edition (contemporary with *Ideas I*). The latter affirms from the outset that seeing an essence is intuition, and if it is seeing in the strong sense — not merely a vague making-present — it is an originarily presentive intuition that grasps the essence in its own "personal" selfhood. In other words: essences too can be given in the flesh.

The incarnate givenness that defines evidence in general — and that comes before any critical reflection on apodicticity — should not be dismissed as a metaphor, a casual figure of speech, or a quirk of Husserl's style. To dismiss it as such would rest on two presuppositions, perhaps rooted in the same error. First, it would assume trivial concepts of metaphor, speech, and style — concepts that miss the role flesh plays in Husserl's own analysis of language. Second, it would presuppose that there is some phenomenological state of affairs existing in itself, independent of any relation to flesh. But Husserl's analyses show the opposite: flesh accompanies every perception. In incarnate givenness, the flesh is simultaneously given and giving — both *donnée et donatrice*. Franck poses pointed questions: would an "adumbration" — Husserl's term for the partial perspectival profile through which a transcendent thing presents itself — have its full meaning if the perceiving subject were not an embodied subject, one capable of moving around the thing, drawing closer to it or farther away? Would the horizonal structure of perception have its full meaning — at least at this descriptive level, before considering the constitution of immanent temporality — if the subject were not an "I can," an "I can move"? Franck concludes that not every self-givenness is necessarily incarnate, but self-givenness in the flesh is its highest mode, its *telos*.

Franck now turns to Heidegger, who in one of his rare extended engagements with Husserl carefully distinguishes between self-givenness and incarnate self-givenness in the context of analyzing the perception of a thing. Franck quotes a lengthy passage from Heidegger's 1925 lectures: the perceived as such has the feature of "bodily presence" (*Leibhaftigkeit*) — the entity presents itself as bodily-there. There is, Heidegger insists, a real distinction between what is "bodily-given" and what is merely "self-given." He illustrates this with the example of the Weidenhauser bridge: one can mentally envision the bridge without representing some image of it — the bridge itself is what one means, not a picture — and yet the bridge is not bodily given. It would only become bodily given if one were to walk down and stand before it. So: something can be self-given without being bodily given, but everything bodily given is necessarily self-given. Bodily presence is therefore the superlative mode of self-givenness.

Heidegger continues: in simple perception, the authentic moment of the perceived is its bodily-thereness. And alongside this feature, every concrete perception of a thing involves another moment: the perceived thing is always intended in its totality, as the whole thing.

Franck explains that he quotes Heidegger at length for several reasons. First, using the perception of a transcendent thing as his model, Heidegger clearly identifies a difference between two modes of givenness — a *phenomenological* difference. Moreover, it is a hierarchical difference: one mode necessarily implies the other (bodily presence implies self-givenness, but not vice versa). Yet, as in Husserl, the question of what the flesh itself means is not posed.

Second, by claiming that the defining feature of perception is incarnation — and Franck notes that Heidegger does not here use the phrase "incarnate presence," but the feature he describes amounts to the same thing — Heidegger aligns himself with the transcendental idealism of *Ideas I*. This is because the perception of transcendent things had never been described as incarnate before *Ideas I*. This alignment opens onto two important sets of questions.

The first concerns the development of Husserl's idealism itself. Is there a link between incarnate perception and Husserl's transition to idealism? It is commonly said that what made the move possible was Husserl's discovery of a new sense of immanence — namely, intentional immanence — and his discovery that the noema is contained "irreally" within the noesis (i.e., contained as a structural moment of intentional sense, not as a real psychological part). This new sense of immanence made the objective term of the intentional relation phenomenologically accessible, and subsequently authorized a metaphysical decision: the abandonment of the metaphysical neutrality that had characterized the *Logical Investigations*. But how, exactly, is the transformation of immanence linked to incarnate perception? Why did such a transformation become necessary?

It became necessary phenomenologically — under pressure from the phenomena themselves. The traditional concept of immanence, as Franck recalls quoting Husserl, says: the immanent is "in me" and the transcendent is "outside of me." This definition relies on a spatial inside/outside distinction, not on a phenomenological difference between modes of givenness. Husserl's analysis of consciousness, generally speaking, replaces the concepts of internal and external perception with those of adequate and inadequate perception, based solely on distinctions between types of intuition. Phenomenology starts from the domain of evidence, and what counts as immanent is what is genuinely present to consciousness. The criterion of immanence is thus *incarnate presence*. Franck quotes a passage from the first edition of the Fifth *Logical Investigation* (reinforced by changes in the second edition): traditional "inner perception" is supposed to be self-evident in the sense of being adequate — i.e., attributing nothing to its object beyond what is intuitively present, presenting the object exactly as it is experienced. Every perception is characterized by the intention of grasping its object as present and in person, and perception achieves perfect adequacy when the object is in fact present in person and exhaustively apprehended as what it is.

At this stage, real (*reell*) inclusion in lived experience and incarnate givenness are still joined together. But once it is recognized that things are given to me in the flesh in perception, the two notions have to be separated, and the concept of immanence has to be transformed. For if the transcendent thing is given in the flesh, then "flesh" cannot just be identified with the lived experience in which the thing is adumbrated. The next questions arise: can one conceive of the thing (*res*) outside any relation to lived experience or consciousness? And can one understand that relation, in turn, as "irreal" — that is, as a non-real, intentional relation? The skeptical tradition, Franck notes, especially in Hume, answers "no" to the first question; intentionality is Husserl's answer to the second. Stated otherwise: the thing has meaning only as something intended, as a relation to transcendence that is given in the flesh within lived experience itself. If this relation is identical with lived experience, and if its objective term (the noema) is intentionally contained within it, then this new sense of immanence makes incarnate perception phenomenologically intelligible.

Flesh thereby becomes the very medium of the phenomenological gaze, prior to any distinction between immanence and transcendence. To support this, Franck recalls Husserl's "principle of principles": every originarily presentive intuition is a legitimate source of cognition; everything that is offered to us originarily in intuition — "incarnately," so to speak — is to be accepted just as it is presented, but only within the limits in which it is presented. So the original intuition is incarnate givenness.

This incarnate givenness — Franck continues — lies prior to the opposition between immanence and transcendence, that is, prior to the most fundamental difference there can be, the difference between consciousness and the world, between lived experience and the thing. This is confirmed by an eidetic law that Husserl articulates and that guides the reduction: any physical thing given "in incarnate presence" can be non-existent (it may turn out to be illusion); no mental process given "in the flesh" can be non-existent. The crucial point is that flesh appears on *both* sides of this fundamental difference — the side of consciousness and the side of world — and so flesh itself escapes this difference and in fact makes it possible. Is flesh, then, simply irreducible, or already reduced? Does it lie at the origin of the world itself? Is it perhaps responsible for the world's being brought to light? Franck sets these difficult questions aside, noting that answering them presupposes already understanding how the various senses of "flesh" form a unity.

The second set of questions that the issue of incarnate givenness opens up exceeds the scope of this immediate study, but Franck signals that it indicates one of his book's long-range goals. These questions concern Heidegger and, more specifically, the relation between Husserl's intentional analysis of consciousness and Heidegger's existential analytic of *Dasein*. A brief but pointed note in *Being and Time* sketches the outline of just such a confrontation, by indirectly proposing an interpretation of Husserlian phenomenology. The passage occurs in §69 of *Being and Time* — the section on "The temporality of being-in-the-world and the problem of the transcendence of the world." Heidegger writes there that the thesis that all cognition aims at "intuition" has the temporal meaning that all cognizing is a "making-present" (*Gegenwärtigen*). Whether all science, or even philosophical cognition, aims at making-present need not be decided here, Heidegger says. He notes that Husserl uses the expression "make present" to characterize sensory perception (citing the *Logical Investigations*) and that this temporal way of describing the phenomenon must have been suggested by the analysis of perception and intuition in terms of intention. Heidegger concludes that the intentionality of "consciousness" is in fact grounded in the ecstatic unity of Dasein — meaning Dasein's temporal structure of being-ahead-of-itself, being-already-in-the-world, and being-alongside-things — and announces that he will show how this is so in a later (in fact never published in that form) division.

Franck draws four implications from this Heideggerian note. (a) The relation between intentional consciousness and *Dasein* is a relation of *founding*: intentionality is to be founded on the ecstatic temporality of Dasein. This founding relation is precisely what was to be exposed in the unfinished section "Time and Being," the very point at which *Being and Time* breaks off and at which Heidegger's thought is supposed to "turn around" (*umkehrt*) — the famous *Kehre*. (b) The reference to perception in this context indicates that, given how radical Heidegger's claim is, Heidegger himself takes perception to play a decisive role in Husserl's phenomenology. (c) Contrary to what was said earlier, the essential characteristic of sensory perception, on Heidegger's reading, is *not* incarnation but the temporal determination of its presentation (its character as making-present). Franck asks: does this mean that incarnation can be reduced to presentation — and thus that flesh can be traced back to temporality? (d) The Husserl that Heidegger here treats is no longer the Husserl of *Ideas I* but the Husserl of the 1901 *Logical Investigations*. This already suggests a particular interpretation of Husserl's intellectual trajectory — one that Heidegger would later, in 1962, formulate explicitly in a seminar on *Time and Being*: Husserl, Heidegger says there, came close to the true question of being in the *Logical Investigations*, especially in the Sixth Investigation, but could not persevere in the philosophical atmosphere of that time. He came under the influence of Natorp and turned to transcendental phenomenology, which reached its first culmination in the *Ideas*. The principle of phenomenology, Heidegger says, was thus abandoned. Franck observes that, on this reading, it is "atmosphere" and "influences" that prevent Heidegger from asking whether this turn — assuming there was one — might itself have been motivated by phenomenology's own principle of returning to the things themselves.

Suppose, for a moment, that intentional consciousness cannot in fact be founded on the ecstatic temporality that defines Dasein's being. And suppose that incarnation cannot be reduced to temporality — given that intentional consciousness is essentially perceptual. What would follow? First, the originality of the starting point of Heidegger's question of being would be challenged. Second, his analysis of worldhood would be put into question. Finally, the existential analytic as a whole — both as fundamental ontology and as the destruction of the history of ontology — would be challenged. In short, the question of being as such would not necessarily be challenged, but its specific form in *Being and Time* would. Through a complex series of further questions, all of Heidegger's thought would become implicated. To say it is "implicated," however, is not yet to say it is overturned or refuted.

So the question becomes: under what conditions could incarnation be traced back to temporality? Staying within the context of Heidegger's existential analytic, Franck notes that the incarnation of Dasein is phenomenologically inseparable from its spatiality. Is it possible to derive Dasein's spatiality from its temporality? §70 of *Being and Time* is devoted to exactly this derivation. And yet, Franck points out, Heidegger himself later wrote, in "Time and Being," that this attempt to derive human spatiality from temporality is untenable. This admission is striking and disorienting: the very work of derivation that *Being and Time* attempted is later disavowed by its author.

To narrow the question further, Franck observes that Heidegger's statements about flesh do not form a unified position. On one hand — especially in the Nietzsche lectures — flesh is sometimes reduced to "animality" or to "subjectivity," without ever being thought on its own terms. On the other hand, Heidegger sometimes distinguishes the body from animality. His understanding of flesh is sometimes subordinated to his understanding of life, and other times — in a phenomenologically more promising direction — his understanding of life is guided by his understanding of flesh. Even stranger is Heidegger's remark that the mode of understanding accompanying "the animalistic in humans" is something metaphysics has "not yet" touched upon — a remark that grants metaphysics a future, when all of Heidegger's thinking about being seeks to overcome or even abandon metaphysics.

Interpreting these passages, Franck says, calls for a repetition of the existential analytic capable of understanding how and in what sense Dasein "harbours the intrinsic possibility for being factically dispersed into bodiliness and thus into sexuality," and what Heidegger calls "a primordial bestrewal (*Streuung*) which is in a quite definite respect a dissemination (*Zerstreuung*)" — once one has accepted that spatiality cannot be reduced to temporality.

If transcendence has its origin in temporality, then this question must be reopened. On one side, the existential concept of transcendence aims to unfold the radical implications of intentionality. On the other side, the meaning of intentionality is itself worked out in an analysis of perception, and the full depth of this meaning, Franck contends, cannot be reached without a thematic treatment of flesh. So it appears necessary to undertake an analysis of the sense and function of flesh. With that conclusion, Chapter One reaches its programmatic end: the book has identified its central problem.

## **Chapter Two: Science as Egology**

Franck begins the second chapter by restating the goal of Husserl's *Cartesian Meditations*: to raise philosophy to the rank and dignity of a rigorous science — to fulfill the very idea of true science. If evidence and its noematic correlate, truth, are the *telos* of all scientific activity in general, then science is concerned with truths and evidence that are repeatable as the same — valid once and for all, valid for everyone. This places science under a double constitutive condition of ideality: its truths must be omnitemporal (true at all times) and intersubjective (true for all subjects). Even when this ideal cannot in fact be attained, science as a noematic phenomenon — as the meaning aimed at — remains oriented by this idea. The *eidos* of science is therefore a horizonal *telos*, normative and prescriptive. Franck quotes Husserl: the idea of science and philosophy involves an order of cognitions proceeding from earlier to later cognitions, a beginning and a path of advance that are not chosen arbitrarily but rooted "in the nature of things themselves."

As a systematic series of statements grounded by — and accepted on the basis of — evidence, drawn from evidence either directly or indirectly, all science in general (and first of all philosophy) must rest on one or more pieces of evidence carrying the mark of absolute priority. So Franck restates the central question: what is evidence? And what conditions must it satisfy?

At first glance, Husserl seems to follow Descartes' path — casting all received knowledge into doubt in search of an indubitable fixed point. But the differences between them, although subtle, are decisive. On the one hand, the phenomenological reduction does not operate through doubt: it never involves negation. It is definitive and universal, and it also encompasses the psyche (whereas Descartes leaves the psyche intact as the foundation). On the other hand — and crucially — truth and reality are the noematic correlates of evidence: they are what evidence gives us. According to Husserl, evidence demands an enormous work of clarification, which is precisely the task of intentional analysis — a clarification Descartes never undertook because he was too confident about the meaning of being and reality. This clarification of evidence begins by distinguishing types of evidence — a distinction whose full importance only emerges later. The "perfection" of evidence can be understood in two ways. First, evidence is perfect when intuition completely fills the intention — when signifying intentions do not outrun intuitive givenness. Second, evidence is perfect when its object, posited as existing, withstands an attempted imaginary annihilation (one cannot, even in imagination, conceive it as not existing). The first kind of perfection is *adequation*; the second is *apodicticity*. Apodicticity can belong to evidence that is not adequate. The question of the beginning of science thus becomes: is there an evidence that is prior in principle to all other conceivable evidence and that has at least a recognizable apodictic content — something that gives us a being secured "once for all," absolutely, by virtue of its apodicticity? At this threshold, Franck notes a striking detail: Husserl drops the requirement of "and for everyone." For the beginning, omnitemporality without intersubjectivity will have to do — and this already foreshadows the solipsistic problem.

The reduction is the pivot and the permanent theme of Husserl's entire enterprise. It is the pure origin of all meaning and what opens the phenomenological "domain." Unlike in other expositions, here in the *Cartesian Meditations* the reduction is introduced and put to work very rapidly. This is done under the pressure of the demand for apodicticity, a demand that itself can only be formulated on the presupposition that the field of transcendental experience is already pre-given. Husserl's brevity here is probably owed to his confidence that the Cartesian heritage will carry the argument along. What, then, is the first apodictic evidence? Is it the evidence of what is given constantly in experience — the world as the totality of objects, the world within which I exist, the world that the established sciences continually return to as their ground? No: the necessary existence of the world is not required by the way the world gives itself. The argument in the first Meditation faithfully reproduces the one in §49 of *Ideas I*. It is possible for the flow of lived experiences to no longer be guided by the world; experience could, "in conflict," dissolve into illusion not only in detail but in whole, so that, in the limit, there might no longer be any world. The evidence of the world is a simple matter of fact and thus cannot claim apodicticity. Could the totality of being be merely a "coherent dream"? Franck quotes Husserl: at this point, following Descartes, we make the great reversal that — if made correctly — leads to transcendental subjectivity: the turn toward the *ego cogito* as the ultimate and apodictically certain basis for judgments.

The natural ontological belief in the world is the object of a suspension that turns the world into a phenomenon claiming to exist. What is "put out of play" by the reduction is the thesis of the natural attitude — the thesis that the world is a reality in itself, the root of all naturalism. At the threshold of his "fundamental phenomenological consideration," Husserl writes that he is conscious of a world endlessly extended in space, endlessly becoming and having become in time; he is conscious of it in the sense that he finds it immediately by intuition, by seeing, touching, hearing — corporeal physical things with various spatial distributions are simply "on hand" for him.

The world of the natural attitude, Franck explains, is not some primitive world that would precede all idealization; it cannot be confused with the lifeworld. It is the Copernican-Galilean world — a world made up of material things situated within a homogeneous and infinite spatio-temporal framework. But this world is accompanied by an ontology: the perceived thing refers to a physical truth that its sensible appearances both signal and conceal. The reduction therefore has, beyond its methodological function, a historical sense: it puts out of play the traditional, modern ontology — the ontology of mathematical-physical nature.

The first consequence of this "great reversal" is that one is no longer allowed to speak in the plural. Franck cites Husserl: the *epoché* affects the intramundane existence of all other egos, so that we should no longer speak communicatively in the plural. Other human beings and animals are given to me for experience only through my sensory experience of their bodily organisms — and since the validity of this experience too is in question, I cannot use it. Along with other egos, I also lose all the formations belonging to sociality and culture. The whole concrete surrounding lifeworld becomes for me, from this point on, only a phenomenon of being, not something that simply is.

What could this prohibition mean, Franck asks, if language and speech, as social and cultural formations, always point back to intersubjectivity, and if communication is the horizon of all language? The very word "solipsism" — in order to be understood by the one who speaks it and by the one who hears it — already involves the pre-givenness of the other. To prohibit plurality, even as a self-imposed restriction, is to produce a statement whose very meaning summons the other whom it seeks to exclude. And more broadly: does not the ideality of meaning, precisely as ideality, already require intersubjectivity (since ideal meanings are valid for all)?

This line of argument, Franck observes, would lead Husserl to a form of skepticism and cannot be sustained within phenomenology itself. For two reasons: first, the linguistic layer was never taken by Husserl as original; second, in his analysis of expression, Husserl works out a notion of pure expression precisely by reducing the indicative function — that is, by reducing the relation to the other. So if for Husserl the bare fact of giving a lecture or speaking does not by itself undo solipsism, that is because Husserl's entire phenomenological analysis of language is itself solipsistic.

The world as phenomenon is irreducibly mine, and only on this basis can any decision about the being of the world be made. If the reduction is freed once and for all from realism, that is because it delivers an apodictic ego — or, more precisely, in genuinely Husserlian terms, a transcendental *field of experience*. The world as an intentional aim belongs to my pure egoic life, irreally contained within the transcendent intentional aim. If the question of the world plays out within an absolute egology that grounds objectivity and forgoes the Cartesian appeal to the infinite (to God), then solipsism is the threat of a transcendental illusion — and recourse to intersubjectivity is the only counter-measure. One can therefore appreciate the magnitude and difficulty of an inquiry that has to begin with a methodological exploration of the transcendental field of the ego.

Before listing the general structures of this field of experience, Husserl raises a critical question and marks out a distinction. The question concerns the *scope* of apodicticity: if the ego is not a premise in a chain of reasons but an ontological sphere open to intuition, then one must know what within this sphere is apodictic and what is not. The question already arose in Descartes: for the apodicticity of the ego to ground a deductive science, memory must traverse the chain of reasons without error, must be infallible, must be apodictic evidence. But it is not. In general, the past temporal horizon of transcendental subjectivity is not apodictically secured, at least not immediately. The past is not given in adequate perception — only the core of transcendental experience, the core of "living presence to oneself," is given adequately. This living self-presence expresses the grammatical sense of "ego cogito" (the present-tense, first-person verb). By analogy with the transcendent thing that is brought to light in perception, given there in the flesh with its infinite horizon of possible perceptions, the apodictic "I am" implies the opening of temporal horizons — an essential law described in great detail in the 1905 lectures on time-consciousness. The actuality of the transcendental domain *as a whole* is absolutely assured, but questions about the critique of transcendental experience and transcendental knowledge in general remain open. Transcendental phenomenology proceeds in two steps. The first step "is not philosophical yet in the full sense," because at this stage the phenomenologist is simply devoted to the evidence of experience in order to describe its pure essence. The second step is critical. Husserl, in the conclusion to the *Cartesian Meditations*, acknowledges that he has only taken the first step there.

Descartes neglected the question of memory in order to make the ego the indubitable foundation of an axiomatic order of reasons, and in doing so missed the transcendental ontological sphere altogether. Put differently, Descartes too quickly interpreted the ego as a psyche. Without the distinction between the psychological ego and the transcendental ego that only phenomenology brings to light, one falls into what Husserl calls the "nonsense of transcendental realism." This distinction, which contains the very possibility of phenomenology as a questioning back to the origin of the world, is just as enigmatic as the psycho-phenomenological parallelism that stems from it. What distinguishes the "region" of the soul — privileged in relation to all other regions presented within it and derived from it — from transcendental consciousness as an "archi-region" (a primordial region)? How can the domain of phenomenological psychology be distinguished from that of transcendental phenomenology, if their "extension" is the same? Husserl's response is brief: the soul retains within itself a relation to the world; mental life is always in the world. In what way? Husserl says: in essence, all objects of the world are "embodied" (*verkörpert*), and the soul is what remains after abstracting away from a being's corporeal component — its body — which is united with a psychic component. This traditional definition of the human being is one Husserl himself accepts. After this abstraction, the soul still refers to the other (corporeal) component and thereby to the fundamental layer of corporeality (*Körperlichkeit*) that belongs to the world.

To gain access to the non-psychological consciousness that carries within itself both the world and the psyche, as intentional unities of sense, is to liberate the world's transcendence as a theme for transcendental philosophy. It is to do philosophy that does not try to elucidate the world by reference to a being having the same mode of being as the objective world itself. But what *is* the mode of being of the ego, if the transcendental ego is not different from the mundane ego — given that the mundane ego is its self-objectification, its self-mundanization (its becoming-worldly)? Franck quotes Heidegger asking: what is the mode of being of this absolute ego — in what sense is it the same as the factual I, and in what sense is it not the same? Franck flags that he will return to this critical Heideggerian question later in order to understand the "same" and the "other" involved here.

The *epoché* has opened up a new and infinite sphere of being available for experience. Husserl writes in a marginal note: "Where there is a new experience, a new science must arise." But if this new science is characterized exclusively by its theme — the field of transcendental experience — then it threatens to give rise immediately to a kind of transcendental empiricism, a mere recitation of factual events occurring in a factual absolute ego. The eidetic reduction is meant to ward off this danger. As Husserl correctly observes, from the very beginning of the establishment of the universal structures of subjectivity, each actual experience corresponds to a pure fiction, a quasi-experience — and "fiction constitutes the vital element of phenomenology as it does for all eidetic sciences."

Yet Husserl, Franck notes, will only explicitly convert transcendental analysis into eidetic-transcendental analysis when the question becomes the self-constitution of the ego. In one sense, that suffices, since the constitution of the ego by itself encompasses all constitution in general. But there are at least two other important considerations. First, it is hard to proceed to an eidetic analysis as long as the question of the limits of apodicticity is left in suspense — and the *Cartesian Meditations* themselves are not concerned with this problem. Second — and this is the more profound reason, to which Franck will return — to access the *eidos ego*, the essence of an ego whose temporality has not yet been demonstrated, one would have to presuppose another ego in order to carry out the variation by which the eidos is reached. That would mean escaping the solipsism into which the reduction has confined us, but without phenomenological warrant.

What, then, are the structures of the transcendental field? By shifting the center of gravity of transcendental evidence from the *ego cogito* (the singular act of self-positing) to the multiple *cogitationes* (the multiple acts of consciousness), the phenomenologist brings out the first of these structures: intentionality. At any moment I can reflect on my conscious life, and within the reduction — where all worldly positings are suspended — it remains the case that each *cogitatio* carries within itself, as its aim, a relation to the world. Each lived experience of consciousness is a consciousness of …, regardless of whether its object actually exists. The relation to the perceived, for example, is essential to perception itself; it contains the *cogitatum* (the perceived as perceived) in an irreal sense within the *cogitatio*. Franck cites Husserl: the word intentionality means nothing other than this universal fundamental property of consciousness — to be consciousness of something, as a cogito to bear within itself its cogitatum. The act of reflection therefore no longer involves the ego exclusively but also the noema of the world. But — Franck raises a worry — does this not make reflection something secondary, contradicting the original "archaeological" intent of phenomenology to return to origins? Does not reflection replace an originally lived and spontaneous experience with a reflective experience that turns what was first an act into an object? Does the method contradict the project? No argument can settle this except a phenomenological one, namely the return to the mode of givenness itself.

Reflective lived experience is indeed entirely different from spontaneous lived experience, and reflection alters — Husserl says *verändere*, "modifies" — the original lived experience. But reflection *gives* the original lived experience as the intentional correlate of the reflective lived experience. Since reflection does not repeat the original but rather *describes* it, nothing in reflection undermines the phenomenological project at its roots. As Husserl says, reflection's beginning gives pure expression to pure experience according to that experience's own silent meaning. The reflective attitude yields a new intentional process, one with the peculiarity of "relating back to the earlier process" (*Rückbeziehung auf das frühere Erlebnis*), and this new process is awareness — even, possibly, evidential awareness — of just that earlier process itself, and not of any other.

From the outset, reflection has a temporal sense. The reflecting, giving lived experience necessarily comes *after* the lived experience that is given and reflected on. Without this delay, no access to origins would be possible; without this delay — which Husserl calls *retention* — consciousness could not be taken as an object and phenomenology as a science could never exist. But this delay also has the effect of splitting the ego (*Ichspaltung*). Under the reduction, the ego can be described thus: the mundane ego has an interest in the world; the world can only appear as such to a transcendental ego who regards the world as noema; this transcendental ego can only emerge from anonymity (Husserl thus notes how the ego withdraws from its own constitutive activity) through a transcendental spectator who is absolutely disinterested in the world and attentive only to the noeses — or more precisely, to the noema as intentionally included in and constituted by the noeses. Put another way: transcendental reflection requires the reduction. But then the problem arises of the *identity* of these various egos, which Husserl says conceals "the most basic insights into the architectonic of the phenomenological system." Franck turns here to Heidegger's earlier question about the mode of being of the ego. For Husserl, what unifies these egos is the instance of the *living present*: in the living present, I coexist as a doubled "I," and the "I-acts" (*Ichaktus*) are doubled. So retention must not alter the living present — retention must itself be a kind of perception.

Husserl, Franck notes, oscillates on this decisive point. On one hand, by contrasting the self-giving act of perception with reproduction (the re-presentation of what is not present, as in memory), Husserl treats retention as a form of perception: if we call perception the act in which all origin lies, the originally constituting act, then primary memory (retention) is perception. For only in primary memory do we see what is past — only there does the past become constituted, and constituted presentatively (giving the thing itself), not re-presentatively.

But on the other hand, Franck continues, when Husserl analyzes perception specifically as *presentation* (giving the now), he sets retention up as the contrary of perception: if we tie the word "perception" to the differences in givenness with which temporal objects present themselves, then the contrary of perception is precisely primary memory (retention) and primary expectation (protention); perception and non-perception continuously blend into one another.

Franck would not press this point if not for the fact that an analogy between the modes of givenness of the past and of the *other ego* is frequently affirmed by Husserl. If the condition of possibility for the reduction is drawn from retention, does that not signify that the alter ego is already implicated in retention, and that an absolutely pure egology is impossible? Can the splitting of the ego and its alteration be conceived without the pre-givenness of the alter ego? How is it to be phenomenologically established that retentional past and other ego have analogous types of givenness? Conversely — could these questions even have a sense outside an egological approach? Is phenomenology not condemned to contradict its initial premises? Can this be explained, and if so, phenomenologically? With this striking series of pointed questions, Chapter Two ends, setting up the problem of constitution and otherness that the following chapters will pursue.

## **Chapter Three: Flesh and Body in Perception**

The demonstration that consciousness is intentionally correlative with its objects forces a dual orientation onto the analysis of consciousness — a correlative structure. On one side, the analysis examines the intentional object in the manner (*quomodo*) of its modes of appearing, that is, the noema; on the other side, it examines the acts of the ego (the noeses) that constitute and bestow sense on the noemata. Each particular object refers to a horizon that, ultimately, is the world itself; each noesis refers to the totality of transcendental life. Intentional analysis is thus the analysis of the transcendental ego insofar as the ego includes and constitutes all possible objectivity. Franck cites Husserl: when the phenomenological reduction is consistently carried out, what is left on the noetic side is the openly endless life of pure consciousness, and as its noematic correlate, the meant world purely as meant. With this, Franck says, phenomenology is assured of its universality.

Husserl then provides an immediate example of intentional description drawn from sensible perception. This example originated in the *Logical Investigations* and is repeated constantly thereafter, and it responds to a very deep necessity. Phenomenology seeks to overcome transcendental psychologism — that is its very destiny. Transcendental psychologism is rooted in the conception of consciousness as a psyche actually linked to the real world. In other words, it is rooted in a conception of perception that is *not* faithful to original intuitive givenness. The accomplishment of phenomenology therefore requires a new analysis of perception. If phenomenology is always a phenomenology of perception, this is for essential and profoundly historical reasons. Franck cites Husserl: involuntarily, he says, we began with the intentional analysis of perception — and indeed gave privileged status to intuitively given physical bodies (*Körper*). Might this not point to essential necessities? Husserl then offers the well-known example of perceiving a die: it is given continuously as an objective unity in a multiform and changing multiplicity of manners of appearing. In their temporal flow, these are not an incoherent sequence of subjective episodes. Rather, they flow away in the unity of a synthesis, such that in them one and the same die is intended as appearing. The same die appears now in "near appearances," now in "far appearances," in changing modes of Here and There, against an always co-intended though perhaps unheeded absolute Here — namely, my co-appearing flesh-body (*Leib*). Each continued mode of appearance (e.g., "the die here, in the near sphere") shows itself, in turn, to be a synthetic unity belonging to a multiplicity of manners of appearance. The near-thing as "the same" appears now from this side, now from that; visual perspectives change, as do tactile and acoustic ones, depending on where attention is directed.

Franck draws out the implications. The unity of the object is first dissolved into a multiplicity of "adumbrations" (*Abschattungen*), the concept at the heart of Husserl's novelty. The adumbration is not a sensible appearance referring to some intelligible "in itself" that a God endowed with perfect knowledge could perceive. Such a conception is absurd: it neglects the essential difference between immanence and transcendence and bypasses the horizon-structure of perception. The adumbration gives the thing to me in its incarnate selfhood (*ipseity*), and the unity of the thing is grounded phenomenologically in the agreement (the harmonious convergence) of the adumbrations. That is to say, it rests on a *synthesis of identification* whose fundamental form is internal time-consciousness. And — just like the temporal flow itself — the adequate givenness of a transcendent thing is an Idea in the Kantian sense (a regulative idea, never fully realized but always aimed at).

This description of perception, like others, revolves around an absolute *here* — the absolute *here* of my flesh, which is, as Husserl says, "always co-conscious but unperceived." Is this why Husserl has analyzed the role of flesh only discreetly and rarely? Franck sets out several points that need to be noted.

(1) The fundamental Husserlian claim about perception is that things are given in the flesh — which also means that they are given *to* my flesh and *through* my flesh. Givenness in the flesh therefore refers both to a mode of givenness and to the recipient of this givenness. On the basis of this claim, Husserl can criticize philosophical analyses that replace perception with a consciousness of signs or images, and that subsequently take sensible (secondary) qualities as signs of primary qualities (as in the classical empiricist tradition).

(2) The perception of a spatial thing is always accompanied by the perception of my flesh. As an absolute *here*, my flesh is not located in the space of a relative and interchangeable here-or-there; it is the *origin* of that space. Flesh thus "sets the stage" for perception (Franck's phrase: *met en scène*). But, since flesh is the non-spatial origin of space, can it itself be perceived — given that, as Husserl says, where there is no spatial being, it is senseless to speak of seeing from different standpoints with changing orientations in accordance with different perceptions, appearances, adumbrations? Does the fact that flesh cannot be perceived strictly through adumbrations imply that it has the purely temporal mode of being of lived experiences? If, as Husserl says, *der Leib* (flesh) is first and foremost the medium of all perception, the organ of perception necessarily implicated (*dabei*) in every perception, then flesh cannot be purely temporal.

(3) Will it solve the problem to say that my flesh is also a body — one body among others? Perhaps, but this claim cannot be made until one has first, within the context of pure egology, retraced the constitution of my flesh as a body among other bodies. And doing that would force us to abandon solipsism prematurely, at a stage of the journey that still requires solipsism.

(4) Franck sets that question aside for now, but provisionally assumes that my flesh is also a body. Does not the unity of adumbrations — the unity of the thing — depend on the unity of *my flesh* even before it depends on a temporal synthesis? Has Husserl not neglected a constitutive layer that allows a thing to be one-and-the-same? Each adumbration gives me the thing in its flesh; it gives me what unifies the thing with all the others. Moreover, if the adumbration depends on the reciprocal relation between my flesh and the thing (since flesh is also a body in the same space as the thing), then any change in this situation gives rise to a new adumbration that is unified with the previous one *by having the same origin* (the same flesh in motion). None of this would have escaped Husserl entirely. His analysis of perception in *The Crisis* — which proceeds by questioning back to the lifeworld as a tacit presupposition of Kantian thought — bears this out. After noting that everything in the lifeworld is presented as a concrete thing (including cultural and historical objects) and possesses a corporeality given to sight and touch, Husserl writes that obviously and inevitably participating in this is our living body (*Leib*), which is never absent from the perceptual field, along with its corresponding organs of perception (eyes, hands, ears, etc.). The subjective movements of these organs — the kinaestheses — are necessarily linked to adumbrations: the aspect-exhibitions (*Aspekt-Darstellungen*) of any body appearing in perception are not processes running alongside the kinaestheses; rather, they work together such that the aspects have the ontic meaning of aspects of the body only because they are those aspects continually required by the kinaestheses. The kinaesthetic-sensual total situation, in each of its working variations, "sets in motion" particular kinaestheses, and the aspects correspondingly fulfill the requirement.

Flesh, then — at least *as* flesh — unifies the adumbrations. But flesh is also a *body*, extension *partes extra partes* (parts outside parts, the classical Cartesian definition of corporeal extension). As body, however, flesh can no longer play this unifying role. What happens then? The adumbrations turn into "appearances" that require a principle of unity heterogeneous to themselves. The thing is no longer given in the flesh — instead, an entirely different phenomenal situation imposes its law. This other situation is the basis of the traditional philosophical analyses of perception, and the 1901 *Logical Investigations* still refer to them. If the *Logical Investigations* do not yet arrive at the descriptive evidence of incarnation, that is because they obey the descriptive evidence of another phenomenal situation, one governed by its own laws.

Having slowed the pace of Husserl's argument momentarily, Franck now resumes the analysis at its proper rhythm. What was just found — in the exemplary analysis of perception, which holds *mutatis mutandis* for all conscious life — can be condensed thus: the intentional object as such is conscious as the identical unity of various modes of noetic-noematic consciousness; conscious life is essentially synthetic. And in the final analysis, internal time-consciousness is the fundamental form of this synthesis. The phenomenology of perception implies a phenomenology of temporality that joins lived experiences together into a single, unique flow. This synthesis is not a simple connection unifying lived experiences one at a time; rather it links them all within an entire consciousness. This shows that the givenness of each lived experience can appear in attention only against the background of the universal conscious life it presupposes — and the same holds for each noema, which ultimately refers back to a broader noema: the world.

By making internal time-consciousness the fundamental form of synthesis, Husserl encounters what he calls "extraordinary difficulties" — though he does not dwell on them. Internal time-consciousness has immanent time as its intentional correlate. This is a distinction between, on the one hand, the intra-temporal lived experience and, on the other hand, its temporal modes of appearing — modes that are themselves given to reflection, as intentional lived experiences, as temporal. In other words, the lived experiences that constitute internal time appear *within* internal time itself. Franck cites Husserl: we encounter here a paradoxical fundamental property of conscious life, which seems to be infected with an infinite regress. The task of clarifying this and making it intelligible is extraordinarily difficult. Be that as it may, the fact is evident — even apodictically evident — and indicates one aspect of the ego's marvellous being-for-himself: above all the being of his conscious life in the form of reflexive intentional relatedness to himself.

The multiplicity belonging to the intentionality of each cogito is not exhausted only by consideration of actual lived experiences. In my actual perception of a cube from a particular angle, the side I properly perceive refers to the other sides — co-seen but only expected and anticipated in a non-intuitive way as future perceptions. My perception would take another course if I decided to touch the cube, or if I changed my position relative to it. Each actuality implies its own potentialities. These potentialities, of course, depend on the free mobility of my flesh, and the constitution of the thing is grounded in the constitution of my flesh. But Husserl, Franck observes, does not insist on this point, because it raises difficulties analogous to those of temporality. First, my flesh can only be constituted as a body through a kinaesthetic system — and that system already presupposes that my flesh is the organ of perception. Furthermore, and more importantly, the constitution of original temporality leads back to the constitution of *hyle* — to the sensuous data that cannot be given to me unless flesh is already there. Husserl was aware of this in his 1905 lectures on internal time-consciousness. In §11 he writes that the "source-point" from which the "production" (*Erzeugung*) of the enduring object begins is a primal impression: the tone-now present "in incarnate presence" (*leibhaftig*) continuously changes consciously "in" consciousness into something that has been. This is also attested by Husserl's frequent use of the expression "incarnate presence" in *Experience and Judgment*.

The horizon-structure remains an essential feature of intentionality insofar as intentionality is positing of objects. Every cogitatum is given within a horizon that in turn gives rise to new horizons; therefore the sense of the object — the noema — can never be given as something complete. Instead, it is illuminated through a continual, harmonious progression. The object is a pole of identity, an index of noetic constitutive intentionalities. At the same time, intentional analysis acquires a distinctive structure. It is not a real (psychological) analysis — there is no empiricist sensualism of intentionality; rather, it unfolds, distinguishes, and clarifies the richness of horizons and infinite potentialities harboured and concealed in each actuality. This means that each cogito intends *more* than what is actually there (the *Mehrmeinung*, the "meaning-more"), and without this supplementary aim intentional analysis could not even take place — could not inquire back to the multiplicities of noetic syntheses that continually give the object as the same. But conversely, only intentional analysis can give access to this state of affairs; it begins by revealing intentionality as its own basis. This strange circularity can only be accepted under two conditions, which have the same root. First, the intentional object must serve as a *transcendental clue* for displaying the constitutive noetic structures. This affirms a relative and methodological privilege of the constituted (in phenomenology, the question of method is sovereign). Without the guidance of the object — simply given and already constituted as one-and-the-same — intentional analysis could not orient itself within the fluid multiplicity of lived experiences, and transcendental subjectivity would be given to us chaotically. Second, the totality of objects — whatever their type of objectivity, real or categorial — must form a universal unity, a world, as the correlate of a total, infinite, and absolute subjectivity. If each object in general is a structural rule for the transcendental ego, then the world itself must be its universal rule. So Franck asks: is phenomenology not based on a kind of *credit* extended in advance to the unity of the world — that is, ultimately, to the possibility of a system? Without this credit, would it not be bankrupt? And does not this credit belong to the level of facticity (and thus threaten phenomenology's claim to essential necessity)? Nothing, Franck says, is less certain. Husserl is only deploying the pure sense of the idea of an authentic, absolutely founded science, taken solely as a possibility. The credit is in fact the idea of an essential correlation between reason and reality — and this correlation is what is called truth. With this, Chapter Three closes, having both unfolded the centrality of flesh in perception and signaled the difficulties about the unity of flesh, body, and time that will continue to drive the analysis.

## **Chapter Four: Constitutive Analysis**

Franck opens Chapter Four by announcing that we can now approach the problem of *constitution* in its full extension and depth. As to its extension: this "marvellous operation" (*Leistung*) — Husserl's term for the work consciousness does — brings to light the synthetic unity of the various acts of consciousness, delivers the object over to a pure regard, and concerns all possible types of objects, including, first of all, the possibility of objects in general. The constitution of the object — formal ontology — is the basis for the hierarchy of *a priori* levels and thus for material and regional ontologies (i.e., the ontologies specific to particular kinds of beings: physical things, living things, persons, cultural objects, etc.). But constitutive analysis is not restricted to taking real or ideal objects as its guide. It also examines "purely subjective objects" — namely, immanent lived experiences, in which transcendent objects (real and ideal) are themselves constituted — as objects of internal time-consciousness. Constitution thus extends as far as the self-constitution of the ego, and constitutive syntheses define the field of all transcendental phenomenology. As to its depth: the concept of constitution permits ontology in its traditional sense to be converted into egology in the phenomenological sense. This conversion, Franck notes, brings with it the appearance of solipsism. The question of the reality of objects has not been abandoned by the reduction; it must be rediscovered *within* the reduction as a moment of objective sense. If it were not possible to show that being, or reality, is wholly reducible to sense, then phenomenological idealism would be absurd. It is therefore necessary to show that being and non-being, reality and irreality, are constituted in and through intentional life — such that, as Husserl says, the idea of investigating anything outside the parentheses placed by the reduction "cannot occur to us within the sphere of phenomenological reduction." It is now a matter of finding what eidetic features of lived experience give me reality and of clarifying phenomenologically whether the relation to the object is valid or not. In short, it is a question of clarifying the relation between reason and reality.

Within the realm of the reduction, the question of reality is first posed on the *noematic* level. What is the relation of the noema to the object, given that when one speaks of "objects" one normally means actual, truly existing objects belonging to a particular category of being? Franck returns to the concept of the noema. The noema is an intentional component of lived experience — for example, perception has its noema, most basically the *perceptual sense*, the perceived as perceived — given in the immanence of lived experience. As a result, sense is *indifferent* to the reality or non-reality of the object. Does the noema then just provide a copy of reality? No. First, the noema is not a real component of perception, nor is it a mental copy of a real object. Second, it would be absurd to substitute the consciousness of an image for perceptual consciousness, since image-consciousness intends an object that is represented in the image — and thus image-consciousness already presupposes perception. Does the noema then have *no* relation to reality? To say so would misunderstand the sense of the reduction: consciousness remains consciousness of reality, which can be described in its pure appearing. The noema thus retains the characteristic of reality.

To account for this phenomenologically, a more detailed analysis of the noema is needed. The same tree can be given in lived experience under very different noematic states. It can appear in perception as an incarnate reality, in imagination as a fiction, in memory as a re-presentation. The object that is intended cannot be confused with the object as it is intended. The noema is broken down into a *core* and the *characteristics of its being*. Reality, fiction, re-presentation — these are characteristics belonging to the object as intended, and they vary with the modes of givenness by which the object is given to consciousness. The being-characteristics are the noematic correlates of the doxic characteristics (the modalities of belief: certainty, doubt, supposition, etc.). Reality is thus the noematic correlate of *certainty*.

This initial result is not without its difficulties. Apart from the question of what motivates and grounds certainty in the first place, an essential feature has been omitted from the description: sense — the perceived as such — is related to an *object*, and the problem of reality is raised essentially on the level of the object. Franck quotes Husserl: the noema has an objective relation in its own right, by virtue of its own sense. If we ask how this consciousness-sense has access to the object that belongs to it — and how the same object can be the "same" in multiple acts of very different content — then new structures emerge whose significance is extraordinary. By moving in this direction and, conversely, reflecting on parallel noeses, one finally confronts the question of what the "claim" of consciousness to "relate" to something objective, to be "well-founded," actually signifies — how "valid" and "invalid" objective relations become phenomenologically clarified on the noetic and noematic sides. This is the great problem of reason.

Franck returns to the noema. Is the noematic *core* the pure objective sense we are looking for? And is it through this core that the noema refers to the object? This can be shown by transposing onto the noema — as the noetic-noematic parallelism authorizes — a distinction Husserl made earlier, in the analysis of acts in the Fifth *Logical Investigation*. There, what Husserl calls the intentional *matter* of an act establishes the act's relation to an object, while the intentional *quality* specifies whether the act is representational, judging, etc. The matter of the noema — its "*quid*" (its "what") — is what *Ideas I* later calls its core, and this core is what places the noema in relation to an object (whereas the quality corresponds to the thetic modality, the positing-character). But the object must be contained within the noema, just as the noema is contained within the noesis. And a noematic intentionality, parallel to the noetic intentionality, must be exhibited if the object in question is to be the same as the object of the noesis. So a finer analysis of the core becomes necessary.

Each lived experience is directed toward an object, and every noema has its own object. If, in describing the noema — that is, the object as intended — I abstract from everything belonging to the mode of givenness, then an invariable content appears, the *quid* (*Was*, the "what"). This is a pure objective sense, which can in turn be described in the language of formal ontology and thing-determinations. This pure sense — or this objective core, strictly speaking — can be explicated in a system of formal or material predicates that determine it. The predicate always signifies the predicate of "something," and this "something" necessarily belongs to the core as its "most intimate moment," its "central point of unity," the "point of junction" or "bearer" (*Träger*) of the predicates. One must distinguish this "something" from the predicates, even though it cannot be separated from them insofar as they are *its* predicates. The object pure and simple — the point of identity — must be distinguished from the object in the *how* of its determinations. Husserl reserves the word "sense" for the second of these concepts of the object; it is by means of this X — the bare pole — that all sense has its object, since the object in the how of its determinations refers back to this X as that which is "noematically identical within the continually changing noemas." So within the noema there is an intentionality parallel to that of the noesis. But this sense has been obtained by abstraction and cannot constitute a *concrete* essence. The object in the how of its determinations can be given with greater or lesser clarity; the sense can be either fulfilled or not. The *complete core* is the sense together with its mode of fulfillment.

The question of reality is reopened here. On the one hand, every intentional lived experience has a noema, and the sense of this noema places it in relation to the object. On the other hand, no object in general — no reality, no world — can be independent of the consciousness in which it necessarily announces itself. Is the identical X that consciousness attains through the synthesis of multiple noemas "really" the same? Is the object real? Franck quotes Husserl's formulation: when, it may everywhere be asked, is the noematically intended-to (*Vermeintes*) identity of X "actually" the identity of X, instead of the "merely" intended-to identity? And what does this "merely intended-to" everywhere signify?

To speak of a real or true object means that everything that can be said about it is capable of being grounded and justified. Franck cites Husserl: in the logical sphere — the sphere of the assertoric statement — "being truly" (or "being actually") and "being something that can be rationally shown" are necessarily correlated. This holds for all modalities of being, all doxic positional modalities. The real and the rational are thus correlative. Franck makes a slight Hegelian gesture: with a small shift of sense one can reiterate the Hegelian thesis "that which is rational is real, and that which is real is rational." What, then, is the original form of rational consciousness, and what is the original form of constitutive consciousness?

Franck now follows Husserl's analysis closely. There are two classes of positional lived experiences: one class gives the posited thing originally (perception, vision); the other class gives the same posited thing re-presentatively (imagination, memory). The analysis of the noema has already drawn a contrast between the original and the reproduced — but it did so in order to reveal a characteristic *of the noema itself*. The objective sense (the pure X and its determinations) can enter consciousness through different modes of givenness belonging to the noema, since description shows that these modes are features of the noema. Contrasting memory with perception, Husserl observed that memory is, precisely in its own peculiar essence, a "modification of" perception. Correlatively, what is characterized as past in itself is presented as "having been present" — thus as a modification of "the present," which, as the unmodified, is precisely "the original," the "incarnately present" of perception.

This difference leaves the *sense* untouched and concerns only the mode of *fulfillment*. Two types of fulfillment are thus possible: original and non-original. They correspond to the earlier distinction between self-givenness and incarnate self-givenness. If the fulfillment is original and intuitive — first in the foundational order — then, Husserl observes, focusing on the noema we find, fused with the pure sense, the characteristic "in incarnate presence" (*Leibhaftigkeit*), as original fulfilledness. And the sense, bearing this characteristic, now functions as the basis for the noematic positional characteristic — the being-characteristic. The same finding holds for the corresponding focus on the noesis.

A position is rationally motivated if it is based on an original, incarnate, evident givenness. *Evidence* is what reason ultimately points to. The original form of constitutive rational consciousness is *evident consciousness*. This allows Franck to give answers to two questions that had been left open: only evidence can motivate and ground the certainty for which reality is the noematic correlate; only evidence as givenness-in-the-flesh can assure me that the intended object is indeed a real object.

As original givenness, evidence is the archi-phenomenon of intentional life — the *a priori* structural form of consciousness. This means that reason is an essential and universal structure of transcendental subjectivity, and perhaps already implies that transcendental subjectivity is intersubjective. To constitute an object is to return it to the synthesis of evident acts that have given, do give, and will under certain conditions give it in the flesh. To constitute, in short, is to provide evidence of incarnate givenness. But this runs into some difficulties concerning the constitution of *flesh itself*. Franck flags that, for the time being, these difficulties run parallel to those of the constitution of time: in both cases, what is constituting must also be what is constituted. (Time and flesh both seem to be both the foundation of constitution and themselves constituted — a vicious circularity that will demand resolution.)

Constitutive analysis has universal status and concerns all types of objects. So it takes the form of a tireless differentiation of types of evidence correlative to types of objects: if the sense of the being of real objects is not the same as that of ideal objects, then the evidence given to us in each case must be carefully distinguished — in relation to the universal definition of evidence supplied by the principle of principles. Franck quotes Husserl: evidence, in the maximally broad sense, is a concept correlated not only with the concepts of being and non-being. It is also modalized in correlation with the other modal variants of simple being — being possible, being probable, being doubtful — as well as in correlation with variants that do not belong to this series but originate in the spheres of emotion and volition, such as being valuable and being morally good. From this, Franck says, two problems emerge.

First: if reality is the correlate of a *verification by evidence* — to the extent that we cannot posit anything if the synthesis of identity were contradicted by an evident given — then are we not tying evidence to a reality that is valid for me, namely a natural reality reduced to and by the solipsistic ego? And does this not strain transcendental phenomenology with an irreducible facticity or contingency? Only the use of the eidetic reduction can escape this problem. But that is not yet possible: how can we reach the *eidos* of the world or the *eidos* of reality without first having reached the *eidos* of the ego? Franck flags that he will return to this. The establishment of the eidos of the ego gives rise to a specific set of problems within the solipsistic attitude to which Husserl confines himself. Moreover, the exercise of the eidetic reduction cannot take place without imaginative variation, and imaginative variation presupposes that the evidence proper to the imagination has itself already been brought to light.

Second: if reality is the correlate of verification by evidence, how can its character as *in itself* be explained? How does it avoid dissolving into the presence of a singular act of confirmation? Franck cites Husserl: every evidence "sets up" or "institutes" for me an *abiding possession*, since it is always possible for me to return to the intuited reality and to re-establish, in evidence, the first founding evidence. Without such possibilities, there would for us be no fixed and abiding being, no real and no ideal world. Reality can indeed be independent of *actual* consciousness, but it cannot in any way be independent of *potential* consciousness. In pointing back to the infinite field of possible evidence, does not reality at the same time point back to an *infinite* subjectivity? The passage to intersubjectivity will perhaps transform this question.

In the case of the transcendent object — and ultimately of the world — evidence still refers to an infinity of evidence, but in a more complex way. The transcendent thing is given by adumbrations, in a multiplicity of incarnate appearances. What is thus offered to consciousness is always *more* than what literally appears, since what appears is the thing in its flesh, in its totality. Each presentation of the same transcendent thing refers to a harmonious infinity of other presentations of the same thing. In other words, the adequate givenness of the thing is an Idea in the Kantian sense. More broadly, the *world* is an Idea correlated with the complete empirical evidence, evidence which is itself a Kantian Idea. Is there an evidence of the Idea itself? Husserl answers: the idea of an infinity motivated in accordance with its essence is not itself an infinity; seeing intellectually that this infinity necessarily cannot be given does not exclude — but rather requires — the intellectually seen givenness of the *idea* of this infinity. (That is, although we never actually possess the totality, we can intellectually grasp the idea that there is such a totality.)

Everything is now in place to define the tasks of transcendental self-explication of the ego, of its conscious life and the objects constituted there together. We are now able to undertake a constitutive transcendental analysis of real objectivity and respond to the question of the origin of the world. By taking the formal and material ontological regions as indices for the system of evidence, there is a need — Franck cites Husserl — for a constitutional theory of physical nature (which is "given" as always existing and, in being so given, is always presupposed), a constitutional theory of the human being, of human community, of culture, and so on. At the same time, Franck notes that intersubjectivity is presupposed as soon as one speaks about the "real world" and "real objectivity." Is concrete subjectivity therefore intersubjectivity, with the monadic ego abstracted from it? It is still too soon to answer. Franck quotes the closing lines of Husserl's third Meditation: as we pursue this path, an extremely complex intentional composition of the constituting evidences becomes apparent in their synthetic unity — a founding by levels of non-objective ("merely subjective") objects, ascending from the lowest objective basis. The lowest basis is the continual function of immanent temporality, the flowing life that constitutes itself in and for itself. Its constitutional clarification is undertaken by the theory of original time-consciousness, in which temporal data are constituted.

## **Chapter Five: Eidetic Reduction and Archi-Facticity**

The constitution of temporality leads back to the constitution of the ego itself. Without it, the task of constitution would suffer from lacunae. This is not self-evident, Franck says, since in his 1901 *Logical Investigations* Husserl had rejected all "egological principles" — and it is on the basis of such egological principles that both solipsism and the attempt to overcome it acquire their meaning. So why does Husserl come to regard a transcendental ego, a transcendental I, as inseparable from intentional lived experiences? If, under the reduction, we run through the flux of lived experiences, we will never grasp the pure I as a lived or original experience itself. Franck cites Husserl: if an intentive mental process is *actional* — that is, carried out in the manner of the cogito — then in that process the subject is "directing" himself to the intentional Object. To the cogito itself belongs, as immanent within it, a "regard-to-the-Object," which on the other side wells forth from the "Ego," which therefore can never be lacking.

The ego can thus be taken as the source of this regard directed at the object — the regard runs through every actual cogito. As a result, the ego remains identical, and as the subject of its own predicates it corresponds noetically to the noematic X (the bare pole of identity discussed in the previous chapter). The ego cannot be turned into a real moment of lived experience, a real or immanent moment. Its life unfolds in each actual cogito, but all of the background lived experiences belong to it, and it belongs to them. Everything that belongs to a single flow of lived experiences — *my* flow — must be capable of being converted into actual cogitations or of being included immanently. Franck recalls Kant's formula (without thereby implying that Husserl uses it in a strictly Kantian sense): "The 'I think' must accompany all my representations." Franck cites Husserl: if we retain a pure Ego as a residuum after the phenomenological exclusion of the world and the empirical subjectivity included in it (and a specifically distinct pure Ego for each stream of mental processes), then we encounter a transcendency of a peculiar kind — one that is *not* constituted — a transcendency within immanency. Because this transcendency plays an immediately essential role in any cogitation, we must not undertake its exclusion. Though in many investigations questions about the pure Ego may remain in suspense, the pure Ego is to be counted as a phenomenological datum to the extent of its immediately, evidently ascertainable essential peculiarity and its givenness along with pure consciousness.

The descriptive "argument" here is analogous to the one that earlier isolated the pure identical X (the noematic pole). In both cases, the issue is establishing an identity, even if it is a polar and teleological identity. It was not possible to unify intentional consciousness by its *object*. First of all, the object itself is the product of a synthesis of identification; second, granting the privilege of unity to the object would contradict the absolute character of consciousness. With regard to the synthesis of identification itself — i.e., the temporal synthesis — it requires a unifying egological principle, since "every mental process, as temporal being, is a mental process of its pure Ego." This amounts to making the ego the temporal form of all lived experiences and indicates, at least, that the constitution of the ego and the constitution of time are correlative and allied.

Why did Husserl modify his earlier position? Why has he "learnt not to be led astray from a pure grasp of the given through corrupt forms of ego-metaphysic," as he says? What makes a unifying egological principle necessary? In the *Logical Investigations*, lived experiences were unified solely on the basis of their contents and the laws governing them. These were *real* contents (real psychological constituents). But with the discovery of the irreal inclusion of the noema — the discovery necessary to explain the incarnate givenness of transcendent things in perception — it is no longer possible to unify the flow of lived experiences on the basis of their contents: some of those contents must now be counted as transcendent entities that emerge and are constituted within the flow itself. What unifies the flow cannot itself be unified by the flow. If to constitute is always to unify, then the unifying principle cannot itself be constituted. It must therefore appear as something presupposed by all constitution and as something given in every lived experience as absolutely identical: namely, *absolute ipseity*. To withdraw the ego from one type of constitution — the constitution of objects — is not to withdraw it from constitution in general. Franck cites Husserl's warning from *Ideas I*: the transcendentally "absolute" that the reductions have produced is not really what is ultimate; it is something that constitutes itself in a profound and completely peculiar sense of its own, with its primal source in what is ultimately and truly absolute.

Franck restates: the ego always lives in those harmonious networks of intentionality that are called objects. The ego, as the source of constituting acts, exists in itself with continual evidence. It continually constitutes itself as a central pole of identity. "I perceive," "I imagine," "I remember" something — each of these signifies the constitution of an objective pole, and in this constitution the ego simultaneously constitutes itself. And the ego is not constituted as an empty pole. Each act is *actively born* from the ego. This presupposes that the ego has *already* been affected — that a passive intentionality, not under the control of the actual ego, has already delivered an object capable of affecting the ego and prompting it to act. The act initiated in this way is a lived experience that takes place in the same flow to which passive lived experiences belong. This is to say: the world of objects, once constituted, can continually re-affect the ego. Given that the world is an intentional production of the ego, it belongs to the ego in the sedimented form of a *habitus*. The ego is the substrate of an abiding habitus.

Franck cites Husserl: the centring Ego is not an empty pole of identity, any more than any object is. Rather, according to the law of "transcendental generation," with every act emanating from the ego and bringing a new objective sense, the ego acquires a new abiding property. For example: if in an act of judgment I decide, for the first time, in favor of a being and a being-thus, the fleeting act passes; but from now on I am abidingly the Ego who is thus and so decided — "I am of this conviction." Habit, decision, conviction sediment in the ego and continue to characterize it after the act itself has passed.

How are we to define this *habitus* more precisely? This is not a question of noematic identity but of the identity of the ego — and whether the ego can remain in the same aim. It concerns the identity of an ego capable of repeating the same acts. The *habitus* refers back to a "first time" and to the possibility of reactivating that first time. It therefore refers back to immanent temporality. The ego's own *historicity* emerges from its habitus. Franck asks: does it also indicate the ego's finitude? He quotes: the I has its history, and on the basis of its history it creates an I which persists for it habitually as the same I.

If the habitus is a permanent noetic structure — that is, freely repeatable — then its noematic correlate is nothing other than the constituted object in the full depth of its in-itself. The in-itself, as the correlate of *potential* evidence, is thus integrated into the full concreteness of the ego. Franck cites Husserl: from the Ego as identical pole and as substrate of habitualities, we distinguish the ego taken in full concreteness — in that we add that without which the Ego cannot be concrete. The ego taken in full concreteness, Husserl proposes to call by the Leibnizian name *monad*. The Ego can be concrete only in the flowing multiformity of its intentional life, along with the objects meant — and in some cases constituted as existent for it — in that life. The abiding existence and being-thus of any so-constituted object is a correlate of the habituality constituted in the Ego-pole itself through its position-takings.

To the degree that I am always evidently given to myself as myself — in the full extension of my actual and potential intentional life, including the world manifest as a total noematic correlate — the phenomenological explication of *my monad* counts as the constitutive explication of the world and its origin. In conclusion, Franck says, the phenomenology of monadic self-constitution merges with universal phenomenology itself.

What remains here is a project to be carried out in its infinite detail. But only the principles of this project have been defined, and its possibility has been established only within an egological framework. The absolute foundation of universal science is henceforth assured of its sense and its possibility, of its domain and method. However, an objection can be raised: the transcendental monadic ego under discussion is *my* ego. Is this not a *de facto* ego? Does it not merely describe the de facto occurrences in the de facto transcendental ego, in the style of a genesis and a history that are themselves de facto? Have we not effected a pure and simple transfer of the empirical onto the transcendental, without thereby reaching the essential features that must form the elements of a universal science? Is this not a more refined reinstitution of the transcendental psychologism Husserl has just denounced? Indeed — this would be the case if the transcendental analysis did not become an *eidetic* analysis.

Why was the eidetic reduction not invoked sooner? Husserl seems to ask this question himself and to answer: "We have delayed mentioning it only to facilitate entrance into phenomenology." Franck judges that pedagogical reasons do not suffice. He proposes several reasons for the delay. First, Husserl paved the way for the eidetic reduction by noting from the outset that a pure fiction corresponds to every lived experience — a correspondence that opens the field of an *a priori* science of possibilities. Second, all of the analyses carried out so far have been general analyses, and although not all generalities are essential generalities, such generalities can nonetheless lead to what is essential. Third, after having reduced all phenomenology to a phenomenology of the ego, and after the passage from the de facto ego to the eidetic ego, all constitutive investigations acquire eidetic generality. But there are still other reasons.

How does one carry out the eidetic reduction? The starting point is any factual datum whatsoever — a *factum* — which the imagination can freely vary. The imagination can transform the factum into an example or model for the production of an infinite multiplicity of variations. Through this multiplicity, an invariant — a general essence, an *eidos* — appears. The eidos sets the limits to the variations such that they retain the sense of being variations of the initial factual example. The eidos is that without which the object in the free play of variation can neither be thought nor intuitively given. This *a priori* is accessible to intuition and does not necessarily refer to subjectivity. This operation presupposes an active identification capable of revealing what is congruent over against what is different. Such an identification can take place only on the condition of keeping the multiple variations under one's regard — and that depends on a productive, connecting unity that operates passively over the course of variations. In other words, the eidetic reduction presupposes *immanent temporality*. More precisely: the imagination does not *alter* a self-identical datum; it *varies* it. Franck cites Husserl's distinction between variation and alteration: in all alteration, the individual remains identically the same; variation, on the contrary, depends on dropping the identity of the individual and changing it imaginatively into another possible individual.

These brief indications suffice to bring to light the difficulties associated with reducing to the *eidos ego* within the confines of an egology. The variation leading to the intuition of the eidos ego would presuppose that other egos can be given. As a result, either solipsism is broken without phenomenological justification, or phenomenology as a whole falls into the skeptical absurdity of a transcendental empiricism. This aporia did not escape Husserl. In one of only two footnotes in the *Cartesian Meditations*, he proposes the following answer: in the transition from my ego to an ego as such, neither the actuality nor the possibility of other egos is presupposed; I phantasy only myself as if I were otherwise — I do not phantasy others. But this "solution," Franck says, is impossible as long as the immanent temporality of the ego has not been exhibited, since self-variation (*l'auto-variation*) cannot occur without reference to the sphere of immanent temporality. It is here, surely, that the deeper reason for the late arrival of the eidetic reduction is to be found.

Considered on its own, is this solution even possible? Nothing could be less sure, and Husserl is not unaware of it. To imagine myself as another might eventually deliver my essential forms — but certainly not those of the *eidos* ego in general. The forms reached would remain those of a *de facto* ego. More profoundly: the use of the eidetic reduction presupposes that immanent temporality — the temporality of the ego — has been constituted. It implies that *an ego has been given*, since without that, the reduction to the eidos cannot take place. As the condition of its possibility, the ego will always escape variation. It thus becomes necessary either to allow a duplication of the temporal flow — which would amount to giving the other and abandoning egology — or to recognize an *original fact* in the ego. In a text from November 1931, Husserl explicitly recognizes that the eidos ego implies intersubjectivity and points toward an *archi-facticity*. Franck cites Husserl: here we have a unique and remarkable case, the case of the relation of *factum* to *eidos*. The being of the eidos, the being of eidetic possibilities and the universe of these possibilities is free from the being or non-being of the realization of such possibilities — it is ontologically independent of any corresponding reality. *But the transcendental eidos ego is unthinkable without the transcendental I as factual.*

What can phenomenology say about this original *fact* that is the ultimate foundation of the distinction between fact and essence, between possibility and reality — in short, that is the foundation of phenomenology's own discourse? At the end of its faithful descriptions, does phenomenology not encounter something that will never be capable of being described or reduced? Will the passage to intersubjectivity make it possible to reduce this archi-facticity? Whatever the answer, it appears that the constitution of the alter ego will be responsible for the whole of phenomenology. Franck cites the same 1931 text: I cannot surpass my facticity and my belonging to others intentionally included in it, the absolute reality. The absolute has its ground within itself, and in its being-without-a-ground its absolute necessity is that of "absolute substance." Its necessity is not an eidetic necessity that leaves contingency open. Every eidetic necessity is a moment of facticity — a mode of its function in relation to itself, its mode of understanding or being able to understand itself.

These questions — ultimate in more than one sense — are not addressed in the *Cartesian Meditations*. After aligning the eidetic reduction with the necessities of an egology to which he still holds, Husserl writes, strangely, with respect to the phenomenological explication of the ego: therefore, as eidetic, the explication is valid for the universe of these, my possibilities as essentially an ego, namely my possibilities of being otherwise; accordingly, it is valid also for every possible intersubjectivity related (with a corresponding modification) to these possibilities, and likewise for every world imaginable as constituted in such an intersubjectivity. With this strange anticipation of intersubjectivity from within egology, Chapter Five comes to a close.

## **Chapter Six: Phenomenological Idealism**

Franck opens Chapter Six by citing Husserl's 1930 preface to the English edition of *Ideas I*: in that book, Husserl says, he treated of an *a priori* science — "eidetic," directed at the universal in its original intuitability — which appropriates, though as pure possibility only, the empirical field of fact of transcendental subjectivity with its factual experiences, equating them with pure intuitable possibilities that can be modified at will, and sets out as its *a priori* the indissoluble essential structures of transcendental subjectivity that persist through all imaginable modifications. This characterization, Franck says, applies as well — once the preceding questions have been resolved — to the *Cartesian Meditations* (1929). By elevating the ego to an *eidos ego*, phenomenology attains the universal *a priori*. Neither the ego nor the world would be conceivable without this universal *a priori*, since it is the condition for everything conceivable. Then and only then is it legitimate to address the universal problems pertaining to the constitution of the monadic ego. As the source of all possible experience, the pure transcendental ego harbours an infinity of possible forms of experience — both actual and potential — as well as the intentional objects constituted there. These types of experience are not all simultaneously possible. That is why, as Husserl himself notes, the construction of any theory whatsoever cannot be realized through any variant whatsoever of my ego; it can only be constructed by a "rational" ego, in the mundane sense in which one speaks of the human being as a rational animal. Moreover, that ego could not be devoted to theoretical activity in its infancy: the eidetic intuition of infancy excludes actual theoretical construction, but nonetheless contains its possibility. Experiences are thus ordered by an essential, genetic lawfulness in immanent temporality. Franck cites Husserl: whatever occurs in my ego, and eidetically in any ego whatsoever — in the form of intentional processes, constituted unities, Ego habitualities — has its temporality, and in this respect participates in the system of forms belonging to the all-inclusive temporality with which every imaginable ego, every possibility-variant of my ego, constitutes itself as itself.

Once again, imaginative variation allows time to be seen as the universal form of all egological genesis. If one asks "under what conditions is the ego a unity?", temporality appears as the invariant form. This form is not a *causal* form (which would refer to nature and to mundane positings) but a form of *motivation* with an "If … then …" structure. This opens an ultimate genetic dimension for two reasons. First, the constitutive system of all objects in general is based on the constitution of the authority for which there are objects — namely, transcendental subjectivity. Second, the genetic constitution of the ego authorizes, as far as possible, an absolutely universal eidetic phenomenology. After all, if "the beginning phenomenologist is bound involuntarily by the circumstance that he takes himself as his initial example," then one might object that the ego is always found in a *pre-constituted* world — a world whose ontological type, as Husserl curiously says, is "known by all" (*allbekannten*). This world supplies a guiding thread for the description of the structures of transcendental subjectivity. Here again the risk is facticity — not in the sense of some "posterior" archi-facticity that would remain after any reduction, but in the sense of the fact from which the eidetic reduction is supposed to deliver us. As long as the ego has not yet performed the self-variation that yields the *eidos* ego — by showing that time is a universal genetic form (and self-variation is impossible without this form) — then the world correlated with the ego and of which the ego is conscious is not yet an *eidos* world. To recognize the genesis of the ego is to liberate oneself from the pre-constituted world and to perform the reduction to the *eidos* world. Strange as it may seem, the discovery of the genetic motif consolidates and confirms the eidetic motif, while at the same time making it possible to thematize a historicity that escapes all forms of empiricism. If phenomenology can only begin from what is constituted (and Franck parenthetically asks whether Husserl ever analyzed this constraint) in order to go back to the original constituting acts, then it will always have to begin with *static* analyses analogous to those of natural history, before undertaking properly *genetic* and *historical* analyses.

What are the fundamental principles of constitutive genesis? Franck cites Husserl: if we inquire first about the principles of constitutive genesis that have universal significance for us, as possible subjects related to a world, we find them divided according to two fundamental forms — into principles of *active* and principles of *passive* genesis. In the accomplishment of certain acts, the ego produces new objects offered to intuition. Starting from the *Philosophy of Arithmetic*, whose analyses often transgress the psychologism that guides them, Husserl regarded number as the result of specific activities exercised over concrete contents. In the subsequent development of phenomenology, these initial investigations were repeated and extended to all ideal objects, after being removed from their psychological dimension. In this sense, phenomenology will describe the intentional products of a renewed *practical* reason — since logical reason is derived from practical reason as well — and the concept of constitution will have a strictly active sense. But on the one hand, such objects do not necessarily belong to every monadic ego at every moment in its history (a child cannot construct a theory), so the field of active genesis is limited. On the other hand, Franck cites Husserl: anything built by activity necessarily presupposes, as the lowest level, a passivity that gives something beforehand; and when we trace back anything built actively, we run into constitution by passive generation. The "ready-made" object that confronts us in life as an existent mere physical thing — when we disregard all the "spiritual" or "cultural" characteristics that make it knowable as, for example, a hammer, a table, an aesthetic creation — is given, with the originality of "it itself," in the synthesis of a passive experience.

So every activity rests on a prior passivity. The role of passive syntheses, Franck says, is thus decisive. To clarify this, he returns to perception. When I actively perceive a thing, I explicate its horizons, take inventory of its properties, inspect its details. While I do this, the thing continues to appear to me as this thing I am examining — and as identical with the one given before this examination began. There is therefore an intuition that *precedes* all explication, an intuition that gives the object to me "in its entirety," as an object of a familiar type but with vague sense. Since nothing can appear except through the synthetic work of consciousness, we must accept that a passive synthesis unifies the adumbrations, supplies the "matter" for synthetic acts, and explains my own familiarity with things. From this point of view — which makes the notion of habitus explicit — the passive syntheses and the entire passive genesis perhaps play a role similar to the role played by *being-in-the-world* in Heidegger's existential analytic. Husserl provides a very fine analysis of this simple apprehension, an analysis that distinguishes different senses of passivity and lets us recognize the passive constitution of temporality as the precondition for all objective constitution. Franck cites the example: as a simple instance of simple apprehension, hearing the continuous ringing of a sound will do. Suppose the sound is continually the same — invariable in intensity and pitch — in the temporal flow and continual change of the phases of its ringing. The sound sounds in single phases; these are modes of appearance of the temporal object (the enduring sound), whose duration extends continuously with every moment. It appears in the form of a concrete present with the now-point and the horizons of continuous past (on one side) and continuous future (on the other). This phenomenon of the present is in constant original flux, going from the now into an ever new now, with corresponding changes in the horizons of past and future. The sound is moreover given for the most part as spatially localized — apprehended as sounding in spatial proximity or remoteness, determinations that have reference to a spatial null-point (*Nullpunkt*), our own body (*Körper*), on which every "here" and "there" is oriented. In this way the sound is passively pre-given as a unity of duration.

To understand the sense of this passive pre-givenness, Franck says, one must first contrast it with the *active* apprehension of the ringing sound. Active apprehension lasts as long as the sound. It obeys the same temporal structure, because it too always takes place in a living punctual now with a double horizon of retention and protention. But it is not *oriented toward* the now of the sound. Franck cites Husserl: to lay hold of such a now, such a phase of duration, as a moment, and to make it an object for itself, is rather the function of a specific act of apprehension of another kind. If we apprehend the sound as enduring — in short, as "this sound" — we are not turned toward the momentary and yet continuously changing present (the phase sounding now) but, through and beyond this present, in its change, toward the sound as a unity that by its essence presents itself in this change, in this flux of appearances.

The active apprehension issuing from the ego thus traverses the original punctual now of the sound and extends toward the identical sound across the flow of nows. This implies that each new original now continually and *passively* coincides with itself in order to remain one and the same. It implies, moreover, that this activity always retains a hold on what is passing and what has passed. The maintaining of the now is *a passivity within activity* — an activity that becomes passivity in order to remain an activity. The sound in its unity is thus passively constituted in order to be given as "matter" for acts of synthesis.

But more must be added. Acts — all lived experiences in general — possess a temporal form; they enter into the flow of original temporality. This temporal form is itself constituted (the lectures on time-consciousness analyze this constitution from an essentially noematic point of view), but it cannot be *actively* constituted, since it is the very condition of possibility and presupposition of every act. Original temporality therefore points back to an *absolutely passive* constitution. It provides all "matter" in general and is thus connected to the constitution of *hyle*. Franck cites the conclusion of the analysis: in this activity it is necessary to distinguish between the active ray actually springing up continuously, and a fixed passive regularity which, however, is a regularity pertaining to the activity itself. With the active apprehension goes hand-in-hand, in a double direction and according to a double form of modification, a modified activity belonging essentially to it. Accordingly, there is not only a passivity *prior to* the activity — as the passivity of the originally constitutive temporal flux, which is only preconstitutive — but also a passivity *erected on* this, a passivity which is truly objectivating: a passivity that thematizes or co-thematizes objects, a passivity belonging to the act not as a base but *as act* — a kind of passivity-in-activity.

This passive genesis is a "history" the ego can have access to. Starting from the constituted object — described in its structure and its modes of appearing as a given meaning that can be explicated — intentional analysis goes back to the acts that were originally and intentionally productive of meaning. It seeks the origin of sense, since this originating act (or those acts) is always present in a sedimented manner in the entire intentional life correlated with the object in question. The original acts first gave the object its sense and continue to do so passively (without being reactivated). According to a genetic law, each intentional production refers to a "first time" that began its history. Everything known refers to an act of knowing that passively persists in all subsequent progress in knowing. The noematic correlate of this passive persistence is called the "in itself." If passive genesis encompasses and in a certain sense precedes all active genesis, then historicity takes hold of the entirety of egological life — including its constituted objects.

How does passive genesis operate? Franck cites Husserl: the universal principle of passive genesis, for the constitution of all objectivities given completely prior to the products of activity, bears the title *association*. What does this mean? In the *Cartesian Meditations*, Husserl explains very little. After distinguishing the phenomenological concept of association from Hume's concept, he simply notes that here belong, for example, "sensuous configuration in coexistence and in succession." Franck once again returns to perception. As an active operation of the ego, perception refers back to a field of passive pre-givenness where something is already pre-given toward which the ego can turn. What is pre-given *affects* or "excites" the ego (Husserl often uses this word) and prompts it to perceive. What is the most general structure of this field? Consider the simplest model — a sensible field like an array of colors. This is obtained by abstraction, since the colors are always those of concrete extended things. The very possibility of such an abstraction means that the colors — the sense-data — are *constituted unities* and can be thematized on their own. They are the products of a constitutive synthesis that points back to the synthesis of time as "a universal form of order of succession and a form of coexistence of all immanent data." To echo Kant, time is a form of sensibility.

Every form is the form of some content. So one must return to the synthesis of contents in order to understand the structure of the sensible field. Considered on its own, the sensible field is given as a homogeneous unity in a heterogeneous relation to other fields of sensibility. Within the field of an array of colors, the contrast between red and white does not prevent the two from being homogeneous as visual data — they emerge in their contrast, for example, against the heterogeneous fields of acoustic or tactile data. This is to say that "the most general syntheses of sensuous data raised to prominence within a field — data which at any given time are united in the living present of a consciousness — are those in conformity with *affinity* (*Verwandschaft*, homogeneity) and *strangeness* (*Fremdheit*, heterogeneity)." This affinity is also referred to as *analogy* (*Ähnlichkeit*), and it can show itself in varying degrees, ranging from a contrast where two dissimilar data appear against the background of a community (red and white as two visual data) to the repetition of two absolutely similar data. In both cases, a synthesis of coincidence is at work, whether partial or total. Though similitude and analogy are offered to static analysis, they are already the work of a passive synthesis of coincidence — what Husserl calls, using the traditional term but with a changed sense, "association." When there is a synthesis of coincidence between two contents, there is an immanent referral (*renvoi*) of one to the other, and this referral is itself a genesis (since the form of these contents is temporal): the first content is evoked by the second. When the contents fuse together, association produces homogeneity. When they do not overlap, association produces heterogeneity. So homogeneity and heterogeneity are the result of two different fundamental modes of associative unification.

Associative genesis, as an essential law of intentionality, is operative in all passive constitution — including the constitution of immanent temporal objects (lived experiences). Consequently it is an *a priori* for all constitution of any ego. The demonstration of this "innate" *a priori* (the scare quotes are Husserl's own) enables phenomenology to accomplish its goal — namely, to be the theory of static and genetic constitution of the objects of all possible consciousness. In a deep sense, phenomenology seeks to make the ego comprehensible "as an infinite nexus of synthetically congruous performances — at levels, all of which fit the universal persisting form, temporality, because the latter itself is built up in a continual, passive and completely universal genesis, which, as a matter of essential necessity, embraces everything new."

To respect the closure of egology, the auto-constitution of the monad ought never to presuppose the alter ego — and especially not on the ultimate level of passive constitution and associative genesis. But, Franck asks, if association operates by affinity and strangeness (*Fremdheit*), does it not already imply the other? How can the sense of this *Fremdheit* be understood if the other has not been given? In the fifth of the *Cartesian Meditations*, Husserl will say that "accordingly, the intrinsically first other (the first 'non-Ego') is the *other Ego*." This statement bears weighty consequences. It amounts to affirming that the other operates at the deepest levels of the constitution of the ego, since the sense of strangeness at work in association must be derived from what is the first stranger-in-itself (*l'étranger-en-soi*) — namely, the alter ego. More profoundly still, this perhaps implies a definition of pure temporality as the *relation to the other*. For if the other ego is what is originally foreign to me, then the other ego is what originally affects me. Does it not then merge with the original temporal *hyle* that a 1930 manuscript describes as the "core of the other-than-me"?

Unless we could establish that the alter ego is a moment of the eidos ego (but what then would be the sense of alterity?), we would again be led back toward an *archi-facticity*. Doubtless, Husserl can only say that "the particular fact is irrational, but it is possible only in the *a priori* form-system pertaining to it as an egological fact," or that "the 'fact', with its 'irrationality,' is itself a structural concept within the system of the concrete a priori." The system of this concrete *a priori* would be founded on this archi-facticity. Reason and irrationality could no longer be measured on the scale of an archi-facticity. Instead, they would have to be interpreted in the horizon of the relation between ego and alter ego — and through a development that could no longer be called phenomenology. Franck flags that he will return to this point.

After connecting all the constitutive analyses to the analysis of the ego and describing the style of intentional genesis, it is now possible to define phenomenology philosophically and philosophy phenomenologically. As a transcendental theory of knowledge, is phenomenology an *idealism*? Certainly — but in a very novel sense. What was the theory of knowledge for Descartes, according to Husserl? After establishing that all evidence and justification of being and truth is accomplished *in me*, the task was to understand how mental life can acquire an objective meaning — or rather, how a clear and distinct perception can claim to be more than, and other than, a pure and simple internal characteristic of consciousness. "Divine veracity" is the Cartesian name for the solution: God guarantees that what I clearly and distinctly perceive corresponds to reality.

Franck cites Husserl: what does phenomenology's transcendental self-investigation say about this? Nothing less than that the whole problem is counter-sensical (*Widersinn*). It involves an inconsistency into which Descartes necessarily fell because he missed the genuine sense of his reduction to the indubitable — we were about to say: his transcendental *epoché* and reduction to the pure ego.

Where does the counter-sense reside? The *questioning* ego is *already* a psyche, a mundane ego; so Descartes already accepts the validity of the very world he is trying to question. The phenomenologist, by contrast, has access to the field of transcendental experience. In the infinite detail of intentional analysis, the phenomenologist shows that all beings — whatever their type of being — are a formation of sense from pure consciousness, and that every conceivable sense is derived from transcendental subjectivity. Franck cites Husserl: the attempt to conceive the universe of true being as something lying *outside* the universe of possible consciousness, possible knowledge, possible evidence — the two being related to each other merely externally by a rigid law — is nonsensical. Phenomenological idealism is not a psychological idealism in Berkeley's sense; intentionality prevents this. And, since it is continually attentive to the *horizonal* aspect of all sense, intentionality also excludes the possibility — and so the *sense* — of an in-itself, and thereby distances itself from Kantian idealism. Husserl's idealism is an idealism *without limits*: an absolute idealism.

The absoluteness of this idealism is co-substantial with phenomenology — to the point that, as Husserl writes, it is its "proof." This, of course, depends on the thesis that consciousness has no exteriority. At the same time, this gives the charge of solipsism the status of an objection that threatens in principle the totality of the phenomenological edifice. Moreover, Husserl was unable to prevent himself, within the framework of pure egology, from sometimes tacitly resorting to intersubjectivity. The problem of the intentional experience of the *other* is not a regional problem. It is the greatest difficulty of phenomenology, since it bears on phenomenology's very possibility and its very sense. It would be a grave error, Franck warns, to read the Fifth Meditation as merely an explication of the alter ego. The question is indeed about the sense of intersubjectivity — but it also concerns whether this sense is *compatible* with the sense of phenomenology itself. It is at once a test of the other and of sense, of experience and of the purified expression of phenomenology's own sense — a test of phenomenology by and with itself. With this charged formulation of the stakes — phenomenology will now be tested on its very capacity to do justice to otherness without losing itself — Chapter Six closes.

## **Chapter Seven: The Objection of Solipsism**

Franck opens the chapter by stating that the initial discussion of the problem of the other has shown that what is really at stake is phenomenological idealism itself. By defining transcendental subjectivity as something concrete, Husserl seems to permanently rule out the positing of another ego. The consequence is severe: this makes the objective world phenomenologically unthinkable and thereby undermines the philosophical project as a whole. Objectivity can no longer be grounded in divine veracity (Descartes' solution, where God guarantees the correspondence of clear ideas to reality). This "cause of distress" arises for two reasons: first, because divine veracity appeals to a metaphysics that falls under the scope of the reduction; second, because it already presupposes precisely what is in question — namely, the constitution of an alien subjectivity. This holds whether such an alien subjectivity is conceived as infinite (a divine intellect) or as a total, infinite subjectivity that unifies all monadic subjectivities at once in the manner of Leibniz. So under the guise of the "other," what is really at stake is the very being of objectivity, and consequently the being of intentionality itself. The question is essential enough that Husserl is willing to entertain a hypothetical retraction. Franck quotes the passage where Husserl asks whether phenomenology has done transcendental realism an injustice: perhaps that doctrine lacks a phenomenological foundation but is essentially right in seeking a path from the immanence of the ego to the transcendence of the other. As a phenomenologist, can one avoid saying that nature and the whole world constituted "immanently" in the ego are only my "ideas," behind which lies a world existing in itself whose path must still be sought? And is it not "self-understood" (*selbstverständlich*) from the very start that my field of transcendental knowledge does not reach beyond my sphere of transcendental experience and what is synthetically contained within it — that all of it is included without residue in my own transcendental ego?

Franck draws out the implications. The first result of the reduction is to bind me to the flow of my own pure lived experiences and to the unities constituted by their actualities and potentialities. Moreover, the reduction ultimately reveals the profound identity between this flow and the monadic ego. From this point on, everything that can be constituted belongs to me and is inseparable from me — or, what comes to the same thing, the constituting egological life is an infinite life under an absolute law of *closure*. But, Franck quotes Husserl, what about other egos — who surely are not a mere intending and intended in me, not merely synthetic unities of possible verification in me, but precisely, according to their very sense, *others*?

The sense of this question, Franck explains, involves three things. (1) A certain way of understanding the concept of constitution: constitution would always have to do with the ego, and in that case one would have to conclude that phenomenology is unable to think transcendence at all. (2) An understanding of the other as an *exceptional* object whose sense contradicts the sense of constitution — a sense "in rebellion" against the origin of sense. (3) The recognition that the alter ego, in the end, carries the whole weight of exteriority and transcendence. One should therefore expect that the analysis of the constitution of the alter ego is at once the analysis of original transcendence and of the origin of transcendence — in short, the analysis of the origin of the world. These remarks in turn raise questions. Where does this understanding of the sense of the other come from? By what laws can one say that the sense of the other contradicts the sense of sense, if not by the laws of pure logical grammar? Is one not thereby forced to refer to the sense-giving activity of transcendental subjectivity in order to bring out the paradoxical sense of the other? And does this not indicate that the other has *already* been constituted in me?

The only way to break this "circle," Franck says, is to undertake the phenomenological explication indicated by the term "alter ego" and to carry it through in concrete work. But this procedure entails at least one decision: the other can only be a *sense*, and as such can have its origin only in me. This decision simply repeats the founding decision of phenomenology itself: every conceivable object in general is a formation of sense by pure subjectivity. Franck presses the worry: does one not risk, from the very start, missing the other precisely as *irreducible* to me — and thereby risk carrying out one of the most subtle and violent reductions of all, the reduction of the phenomenon itself? Is not the other the unthinkable *par excellence*? But — and here Franck turns the worry back on itself — in order to denounce this operation, to say that the other has been missed and is phenomenologically unthinkable, must one not have had *prior* access to the other? This objection rests on the same erroneous principle as the theories that replace the perception of incarnate givenness with the consciousness of a sign or an image (a recurring target from earlier chapters). If the other's being-for-us is "something very strange that we all feel," it is only at the end of an intentional analysis that this strangeness can be recognized in its full legitimacy. Or rather: only a phenomenology can say that the other escapes its jurisdiction, and then turn this very lack of conformity into an essential and positive structure. Franck quotes Husserl: we must obtain insight into the explicit and implicit intentionality in which the alter ego becomes evinced and verified in the realm of our transcendental ego; we must discover in what intentionalities, syntheses, and motivations the sense of the "other ego" becomes fashioned in me and, under the title "harmonious experience of someone else," becomes verified as existing. These experiences and their accomplishments are facts belonging to my phenomenological sphere, and there is no other way to explicate the sense "existing others" than by examining them.

The sole point of departure for phenomenology, then, will be, as always, the *mode of givenness* taken as transcendental guiding thread. The other is a noema constituted in and through specific noeses; after a static analysis, genetic study can later work back toward them. The other can only be perceived, and regardless of the singularity of this perception (its noema is itself a noesis — that is, the other is given as another consciousness), it does not, at this stage of description, seem to require any transformation of principles. The other appears on the scene of perception without contributing to the establishment of that scene, without, so to speak, participating in its operation. To make the other a noema amounts to excluding the other from playing any role in the constitution of the ego. The purity of the egological-monadic closure is thereby maintained. Franck flags this point deliberately, because it will later turn out that Husserl is *forced* to alter this purity.

Franck then gives Husserl's first exemplary description. In changeable but harmonious multiplicities of experience, I experience others as actually existing and, on one hand, as objects in the world — not as mere physical things belonging to nature, though they are such things in one respect. They are experienced as psychically governing their respective natural organisms. Thus, peculiarly interwoven (*verflochten*) with flesh, as "psychophysical" objects, they are "in" the world. On the other hand, I experience them at the same time as *subjects* for this world — as experiencing the same world I experience, and in doing so experiencing me too, even as I experience the world and others in it. From this, Husserl says, I can noematically explicate a variety of further moments.

The most basic feature of the perception of the other described here is its *ambiguity*, and this on several levels. (1) The other is given as an object in the world, in a synthetic multiplicity of adumbrations, and *also* as a subject outside the world, derived from an entirely distinct mode of givenness. As another origin of the world, the other is given to me in the same way that I am given to myself. The human being I am is the self-objectivation of the transcendental subject I am. The givenness of the other *as in the world* raises no problem; it is the other's givenness *as outside the world* that constitutes the entire difficulty. (2) The other is a mundane psycho-physical object — an object with two sides. On its "physical" side, the other is a thing in nature, a body (*Körper*) individualized by its position in the homogeneous continuum of space and time. On its other side, the other is manifest "psychically," and I cannot have direct access to that sphere — for if I could, it would be *mine*, not the other's. (3) The body of the other is also *flesh*. In the cited description Husserl does not yet mark this difference neatly, but strictly speaking the psycho-physical unity cannot be confused with the psycho-somatic unity, and — to return to the question of individuation — flesh is individualized differently from body.

It is difficult to unify all these ambiguities at once under a single systematic law. At best, one can highlight the ambiguities Husserl explicitly mentions (leaving aside, for now, the flesh/body difference). As an object in the world and as a physical body, the other can be directly given in intuition; as a transcendental subject and as a psyche, this is excluded in principle. The phenomenon described is ambiguous because the other is given to a *fulfilled* intuition in one respect but *refuses* such intuition in another. This is a decisive characteristic — Franck says he will return to it continually. In this regard, the initial description already sets in place everything that will follow: from the operative levers to the most complex aporias. Yet the description was only meant to establish that, in my pure and reduced conscious life, I experience not only others but also the world *as intersubjective*. In short, the sense of my transcendental experience of the world is an *objective* sense. But this objective sense belongs to "my" phenomenon of the world; and if there are others, then each of them has their own phenomenon of the world. How can this presence in me of a sense that *exceeds* me be understood and elucidated, if all sense is the work of my intentional life?

Franck reformulates the problem in another way. The *epoché* yields an I that exists in solitude. The radicality of this solitude is commensurate with the demands of philosophy itself — meaning it cannot be measured by philosophy. In this sense the I is absolutely "indeclinable" (it cannot be put into other grammatical persons). However, Husserl himself remarks that there remains a difficulty, an incomprehensibility: the I has appeal only to itself, and yet just as I say "I," all the personal pronouns are already there as correlates. What do I say when I say "I"? Franck cites Husserl's claim that in solitary speech the meaning of "I" is realized in the immediate idea of one's own personality — which is also the word's meaning in communicated speech — so that each person has his own I-presentation, and the word's meaning differs from person to person. But Franck argues that Husserl's own investigations into the *ideality* of meaning ought to prohibit such a statement. If meaning is ideal, then we must be able to understand the word "I" even in the absence of the one who utters it. I can only say "I" and understand what I say against the horizon of my own *death*. And if meaning is ideal, it is also omnitemporal and intersubjective: I can only say "I" against the horizon of a *We*, against the horizon of a declension of the I (its grammatical variation across persons). The testimonial value of all meaning thus points back both to my own death and to others. By uttering the word "I," I address my own death to others; I "expropriate" myself of it (Franck's term: *en exproprie*). Should one then think that the relation to my death merges with my relation to the other? Would the ego then be structurally *expropriated* from itself? And what would become of ownness and of the egological closure? These questions push the analysis toward the suspicion that the self's relation to itself is already mediated by alterity and finitude.

Husserl, once again, responds by deferring to method: in order to provide the basis for answering every imaginable question that can have any sense here — indeed, in order for these questions even to be properly posed and solved step by step — it is necessary to begin with a systematic explication of the overt and implicit intentionality in which the being of others for me is "made" and explicated with respect to its rightful, fulfillment-content. With this methodological deferral, Chapter Seven closes, having sharpened the objection of solipsism into its full paradoxical form.

## **Chapter Eight: Flesh and the Sphere of Ownness**

The phenomenological approach always seeks to free itself from all presuppositions. Without entering into a deeper analysis of the possibility and limits of this requirement, Franck specifies the form it takes at the threshold of the intentional analysis of the other. What is in question is the constitution and transcendental sense of the other — that is, the universal stratum of the sense "objective world." The alter ego must therefore not be presupposed, either explicitly or implicitly, at any moment. This calls for a *new* epoché of a unique kind. Franck cites Husserl: for the present we exclude from the thematic field everything now in question — we disregard all constitutional effects of intentionality relating immediately or mediately to other subjectivity, and we delimit first of all the total nexus of that actual and potential intentionality in which the ego constitutes within himself a peculiar ownness (*Eigenheit*). This reduction, Franck stresses, does *not* put out of play the other as posited in the natural attitude (that would merely duplicate the phenomenological reduction); nor does it aim at a mundane solitude, which as such would still refer to others; nor is it, more subtly, a reduction to the phenomenon of the human ego, which again refers to others but reduces them to the phenomenon of the world.

Instead, an abstraction is first made of everything that gives human beings and animals their specific sense as "ego-like" living beings. For the animal, Husserl says, "has something like an I-structure"; animals are subjects of a conscious life that we can access by an "assimilating modification of inter-human empathy." But this means that the phenomenology of animality *presupposes* the phenomenology of intersubjectivity — so the reduction to the sphere of ownness must abstract from any egoic animality. Second, everything derived from culture is abstracted. Franck poses a sharp question: must one therefore also reduce *phenomenology itself*? Is not the abstraction from culture the most cultural gesture of all? Perhaps — but this point can only emerge *after* phenomenology, that is, after the reduction. Third, there is an abstraction from *Umweltlichkeit*, environing-world-character. The surrounding world, according to Husserl, is a concept that only has a role in the world of spirit, so everything depending on it must be reduced. Franck notes that Husserl also has in mind the Heideggerian analysis of worldhood here: by interpreting *Being and Time* as a regional ontology explicating the *a priori* of the surrounding world, Husserl maintains the juridical priority of the universal *a priori* of constitution and is thus, in his own eyes, warranted in reducing Heidegger's worldhood too.

Before positively defining the "residue" of this reduction to the sphere of ownness (*réduction au propre*), Franck adds one more remark: this reduction is only possible on the condition that one can abstract from the totality of intentional productions that intend or imply the other. But since the other is not an adequate datum that could simply be abstracted away at its source, but rather a transcendence constituted in a synthesis that is in principle open to the possibility of discordance, it would be necessary to have *completely reactivated* all the intentional production in question and to have traversed all its horizons. Is this possible? Is it not even contradictory, given that there is no constitution of *a* horizon but only horizons of constitution? Does one not here run up against, in various forms, the limits that mark the *finitude* of the phenomenologist? Husserl, Franck observes, says nothing about this.

Franck then cites a point Husserl marks as important: when we thus abstract, we retain a unitarily coherent stratum of the phenomenon "world" — a stratum that is the correlate of continuously harmonious, ongoing world-experience. The reduction to the sphere of ownness is not equivalent to the destruction of the world and of all experience. On the contrary, this abstraction delivers, in a certain sense, the *condition* of all experience of the world. For no experience of the world is possible unless that experience is composed of a certain alterity: the real world always means a world *other than me*. The very syntax of this "other than me" (*autre que moi*) already indicates that I cannot have an experience of the other — however singular or strange — without first having an experience of what is *ownmost* to me. The experience of ownness (*du propre*) is the very essence (*le propre*) of experience. Franck remarks that this proposition is as important for phenomenology as the Kantian thesis that the conditions for the possibility of experience in general are at the same time conditions of the possibility of the objects of experience. The layer of ownness is foundational — though Franck inserts the question of whether it can really be considered merely a "layer" — in the sense that no experience of transcendence is possible without presupposing the experience of ownness. And here, Franck notes, the condition of the possibility of experience is itself *given to experience* — which, in Kant's eyes, would have been a monstrosity (since for Kant the conditions of experience are not themselves objects of experience).

What, then, is this "residue"? Husserl says that from the phenomenon "world" — the world appearing with objective sense — a substratum becomes separated: the "Nature" included in my ownness. What is this nature included in my ownness? Since all objectivity and all idealization are put out of play, it is not nature in the sense of physical science (which abstracts everything mental from the lifeworld to deal only with an idealized corporeality). This exposed nature — idealization, Husserl sometimes says, is a kind of "clothing" thrown over things — is a pure multiplicity of *perceived* bodies that cannot be put into the form of objective space and time; it is a purely "subjective" nature. Even while retaining the sense of a group of extended things, it is characterized first and foremost by its *heterogeneity*. Franck then cites at length: among the bodies belonging to this "nature" and included in my peculiar ownness, I find my flesh (*Leib*) as *uniquely singled out* (*in einziger Auszeichnung*) — namely as the only one of them that is not just a body but precisely a flesh. It is the sole object within my abstract world-stratum to which I ascribe fields of sensation (tactual, of warmth and cold, and so on), and the sole object "in" which I immediately "rule and govern" (*schalte und walte*). I perceive "with" my hands, "with" my eyes, and so on; the kinaestheses flow in the mode "I am doing" and are subject to my "I can." By calling these kinaestheses into play I can push, thrust, act somatically. As perceptively active, I experience all of nature, including my own flesh, which in the process is *reflexively related to itself*: I can perceive one hand by means of the other, an eye by means of a hand — a procedure in which the functioning organ must become an object and the object a functioning organ.

Franck attends closely to this long description, which leads further into the sphere of ownness and gives rise to considerable problems. The first thing to bring out is that the sphere of ownness — anchored in my flesh and characterized by the flesh/body distinction — is *not homogeneous*. It is not homogeneous, first, because my flesh is distinct from all bodies, for several reasons. He has already made clear that no syntheses giving any body whatever would be possible without a correlative system of dispositions of my flesh (tactile movements, ocular movements, and so on) — dispositions Husserl analyzed painstakingly as early as his 1907 course *Thing and Space* (*Ding und Raum*). In this respect, flesh is the condition of the possibility of the thing; better, the constitution of flesh is presupposed by all constitution of things — that is, by all constitution of worldly transcendence in general.

This means that flesh is the *universal medium* for the givenness of bodies within the sphere of ownness. Franck cites Husserl: my flesh is a given thing in its original properties; I move originally, change originally in all such "movements," and all other original changes constitute for me an existing unity that remains present and takes on the character of self-givenness; by means of my flesh and its originality, by means of its original kinaesthesis, the I is able to put seeing, hearing, and so on into play. Always accompanying whatever is given to me, flesh is *what is ownmost* — most my own — in the sphere of ownness. Franck cites Husserl again: among all spatial things, my universal practical sphere is "my" flesh, that which is most originally mine (*das ursprünglichst Meine*); it is the original source of what is continuously my own, the only thing that is immediate, the first thing immediately appropriated (already as a child), and the organ for the appropriation of each and all. And what holds for ownness holds also for nearness: my flesh is, among all things, the nearest — nearest for perception, nearest for my feeling and willing. Franck presses the point rhetorically: is there anything more originally my own, more near, more mine, than the very origin of ownness, of nearness, and of mineness? Ownness, proximity, and mineness are not even conceivable prior to flesh. Nothing grounded in the pre-understanding of my sphere of ownness, proximity, or mineness could be thought prior to flesh; flesh is their *origin*. If the essence of experience is the experience of ownness, then the most essential experience of ownness is my flesh. Without a thematization of flesh, Franck concludes, phenomenology risks losing its radicalism — risks contradicting its most essential vocation and lending support to the charge that it is insufficient.

The sphere of ownness is heterogeneous, in the second place, because my flesh is *also bodily*. This is given descriptively, but the givenness must be explained. So the question becomes: how, within the purely egological sphere of ownness, can my flesh acquire the *sense of a body*? To answer, an analysis of flesh's constitution *as flesh* is a necessary prerequisite. Husserl undertakes this in the second volume of *Ideas*. There, after noting that flesh is the organ of all perception and necessarily accompanies the experience of spatio-temporal objects, he turns to the constitution of flesh itself. As his guiding example, he takes the perception flesh has *of itself* as a corporeal flesh — a choice that seems already to assume that the question of incorporation has been resolved.

Franck follows Husserl's celebrated analysis of *touch*. First, set aside whatever, in the perception of flesh (as a genitive object — flesh as the thing perceived), does not distinguish it from any other body: seeing my hand can be described just like seeing the table before me. But it is no longer the same when it comes to how things appear in *tactile* form. When I touch my left hand with my right, I have movement-sensations and tactile sensations that objectify my "left hand" as a thing; these sensations belong to my *right* hand. Yet my left hand *also* experiences tactile sensations that, without being constitutive, are "localized" there. The physical thing "left hand" is what I get after abstracting these sensations away; if I take them into account, the physical thing is not enriched but rather "becomes flesh, it senses" (*es wird Leib, es empfindet*). The roles of right and left hand are *reversible*, and can be reversed constantly. What matters here is the *self-relation* of touch, the endless exchange between organ and object in which the two can no longer be distinguished. If touch is privileged, it is because of this double "sensing" (Franck uses the French *sentance*): what touches is also touched, whereas what sees is not seen and what hears is not heard. We can thus eliminate all reference to the physical thing, to the body, to exteriority. Flesh is *pure auto-affection*. Franck cites Husserl: the touch-sensing is not a state of the material thing "hand," but is precisely the hand itself, which for us is more than a material thing; and if I convince myself that a perceived thing does not exist — that I am subject to illusion — then, along with the thing, everything extended in its extension is stricken out too, *but the sensings* (*Empfindnisse*) *do not disappear*.

It is Husserl's conviction, Franck says, that this constitution of flesh through touch — in which flesh appears as the basis of *Empfindnisse*, as an organ of the will, and as the basis of free movement — can be achieved *within* the egological sphere of ownness. The same does *not* hold for flesh's constitution as a *material thing*, as a body, that is, as *res extensa*. In *Ideas II*, when Husserl describes the constitution of a center of orientation in space — an absolute *here* — he is not describing the body but the *flesh*. Bodies, by definition, occupy a place within an isotropic, homogeneous, intersubjective space. When Husserl claims that flesh is constituted as a body in a multiplicity of concordant adumbrations but that this constitution is "strangely incomplete," does he not *still presuppose the other*? Does he not presuppose the other's point of view, from which the missing completeness could be accessed, and to which he implicitly refers when he calls the constitution "incomplete"? Husserl maintains that my flesh, in its physical truth, is a unity of possible perceptions that *all others* could have of my flesh — which again smuggles in the other. Finally, to make flesh the "turning point" (*Umschlagpunkt*) in the causal nexus, where free conditionality is inserted into causality, is to withdraw flesh from corporeality. If, within the closure of the reduction to ownness, no constitution of flesh as body seems possible, this is because the union of flesh and body (*l'incorporation*) presupposes intersubjectivity.

Aware of this, Franck notes, Husserl continually returns to the question of incorporation in the manuscripts on intersubjectivity. After 1921, the constitution of flesh as a physical thing is regarded as problematic from the solipsistic standpoint. The analysis runs roughly thus: each member of my flesh appears to move freely, but it can also appear to be moved "mechanically" by another member — my right hand can lift my left hand. The two types of movement (subjective and "mechanical") coincide and are identified, so that my flesh is constituted both as flesh and as a physical thing. But, Franck asks, what *entitles* the passive movement of my left hand to be called "mechanical"? Above all, it is not the relation of these organs to one another that must be constituted as a body, but the *flesh* for which they are organs — which is why, Franck suggests, the very concept of "organs" needs to be renounced. After showing that the movement of flesh can never be confused with the movement of a thing, Husserl ends with the admission that it is a fundamental problem to think through and clearly define how flesh is *also* constituted as physical flesh. Franck says that everything to follow will be nothing but the development of the consequences of this fundamental aporia.

What is the sense of this demonstration of my flesh within the sphere of ownness? Franck cites Husserl: bringing to light my flesh, reduced to what is included in my ownness, is itself part of bringing to light the ownness-essence of the objective phenomenon "I as this man." Within the transcendental reduction, I carry out a further reduction to my sphere of ownness, and other people become nothing more than bodies; by contrast, the person I am is *soul and flesh* (*âme et chair*), a psycho-physical unity in which my personal ego operates in this flesh and, "by means of" it, in the external world. Franck grants the point, then presses three objections. (1) How can I know and say that my flesh is a component of the proper essence of the objective phenomenon "I-the-human," without intending this phenomenon and without its being given to me — and how can this be done when all phenomenal objectivity has been put out of play? Must not the other *already* be given? (2) To identify the soul-flesh compound (Husserl gives the name "somatology" to the analysis of flesh in *Ideas III*) with the psycho-physical compound is precisely to take for granted the very incorporation of flesh within the sphere of ownness that was to be explained. (3) If in the sphere of ownness flesh cannot be body, this implies that the limits of my flesh are not the limits of the body. What could those limits be? Franck does not answer immediately, because a prior question intervenes: can my flesh have, in the sphere of ownness, any other limits than those of the world of ownness itself — limits that might make it possible to speak of an external world, a world "external to flesh" (*ausserleibliche Welt*), of things "external to flesh" (*ausserleiblichen Dinge*)? Nothing is less sure. As the organ of all perception, as the site and means of all perception, my flesh extends *as far as my perception extends — all the way to the stars*. Flesh is co-extensive with the world of ownness, and without its incorporation it is very hard to understand what an "outside-of-flesh" (*hors-chair*) could even mean.

Of course, Franck acknowledges, Husserl does not raise these questions in the course pursued in the *Cartesian Meditations*; where some emerge in the unpublished manuscripts on intersubjectivity, they all lead phenomenology, directly or indirectly, toward its *condition of impossibility*. So Franck announces that his approach will henceforth be twofold: on one hand, he will force himself to follow the winding path of Husserl's descriptions; on the other, he will lay out the consequences of the impossibility of incorporation. Since Husserl himself admits that incorporation is the precondition for all *Einfühlung* (empathy, the experience of the other), Franck will continually have to *undo* what Husserl seeks to constitute. This work, he says, would have only limited interest if it did not progressively point toward the need for an analysis of flesh and of the *carnal difference* (by which Franck means the flesh/body difference). The focus on the Fifth Meditation is justified by its decisive position within the phenomenological system and by its permanent appeal to flesh and body. Without this conjunction — which he will try to analyze — no interpretation of phenomenology within the horizon of carnal difference would be possible, and the book would be nothing more than a monograph with almost no philosophical value.

Franck then states the result of the reduction to ownness: there is a "world" of ownness in which the psycho-physical ego is integrated by virtue of its corporeal flesh, and this world is the noematic correlate of a transcendental ego. What is attained is monadic subjectivity in its full concreteness. My own world, Husserl says, conserves the "personal" — that which displays value-predicates and predicates of "works" (*Wert und Werkprädikate*) — and remains within a spatio-temporal form reduced to ownness, in which "objects" (physical things, the psychophysical ego) are likewise outside one another. Franck finds this surprising. What sense can a *personal* ego have in a world *without others*? What sense can there be in positing a value, or recognizing the value of work, when the axiological world and the world of work are essentially intersubjective? And, more seriously, what can be the sense of a *spatio-temporal form reduced to ownness*? One might provisionally accept reducing temporal form to the sphere of ownness, at least before any deeper explication of temporality; but one cannot say that the sphere of ownness, being fundamentally heterogeneous, is also subject to the same *spatial* form. Either spatial form is one and the same — and then intersubjectivity is *given* — or homogeneous space is not constituted, and then it is impossible to say, for instance, that things are "external to" the incarnate ego. Husserl returns to this question frequently. Franck cites a short text from March or April 1934 titled "The problem of the constitution of homogeneous and objective space in primordiality and through *Einfühlung*," which begins by asking whether it is even conceivable, setting others aside, to constitute space in the full sense as a form of corporeality, such that my flesh would have the same properties as external bodies. Husserl distinguishes the immobility of flesh from a body at rest and concludes that my corporeal flesh (*Leibkörper*) in its primordiality is so constituted that the claim that it has locomotion, including location in space, has no meaning. This response troubles him enough that he tries — in vain — to resolve it by announcing further analyses that are "not very easy to pursue." With this confession of an unresolved difficulty about flesh and space, Chapter Eight ends.

## **Chapter Nine: Flesh, Ego, Psyche**

The world of ownness is the world of purely original experience — of incarnate experiences and data. It contains nothing that is *other* in the strong sense, and Franck explains why: the other acquires its sense only in relation to the ego. There can be an alter ego only in relation to my ego reduced to the sphere of ownness. The other can be constituted only in my own world; every *other* world already presupposes the constitution of the other. Franck cites Husserl: the sphere of my transcendental ego's primordial ownness must contain the motivational foundation for the constitution of those genuine transcendencies that go beyond it and originate first of all as "others"; all objectivity in this sense is constitutionally related back to the first thing other than my ego's own — and that first "other-than-my-own" takes the form of *someone else*, that is, the non-ego in the form "another ego."

The analysis of my own world is thus a necessary prerequisite. My psychic life remains an *intentional* psychic life that experiences the world. This means in particular that putting out of play what is foreign to me does not prevent me from having the experience of something foreign: my consciousness *of* the foreign is not itself foreign to my consciousness. This experience rightfully belongs to my consciousness. Indeed, all constitution of the world is inherent in my psyche — including the systems that constitute the foreign. The reduction to the sphere of ownness does not undermine the possibility, within my psyche, of having an experience of the other or of constituting the other. But this can occur only in a *psychological* sense — unless it can be shown that my psychic life is itself wholly constituted by my transcendental ego and thereby belongs to it. The transcendental ego would then constitute the world internal to it, a world in which the psychophysical ego could have experiences of external things, of the other, and *with* others.

Franck recalls that we have already confronted the problem of the relation between the transcendental ego and the psychological ego — between phenomenology and psychology — the problem of the mundanization and humanization of the absolute ego. And he recalls that the question posed by Heidegger (and Ingarden) was left unanswered: can the ego be transcendental *and/or* psychological, and what play between identity and difference hides under this "and/or"? The elegance of Husserl's solution, Franck says, barely conceals its obscurity — even from Husserl himself. In constituting the world as the absolute transcendental ego, I continually have a *worldly self-apperception* (*eine verweltlichende Selbstapperzeption*), or rather a *self-objectivation* (*Selbstobjektivierung*). This self-apperception is always already accomplished, since the reduction begins precisely by *inhibiting* it. In a sense, the proof of this apperception lies in the very possibility of putting it out of play. Through this strange circularity, the reduction brings to light the difference between the transcendental and the psychological ego, and in doing so confirms the apperception it inhibits.

Franck tries to analyze more finely this apperception through which my consciousness takes on the sense of a mundane, psychological consciousness. The mundane ego designates a consciousness and a mental life *localized in a corporeal flesh*. One can ask, as Husserl does: why do we not say that the *body* reigns (*waltet*) over the soul — why do we always say that the *soul* reigns over the body? By qualifying the primordial human ego as a "concretely incarnated I" or an "egoic corporeal flesh," the traditional definition of the human being as a union of soul and body, soul and flesh, is nonetheless presupposed. My mundane experience of myself is an experience of an incarnated and incorporated psychological ego, and through this I have a mundane experience of the world. Now, since this experience does not survive the thought experiment of the annihilation of the world, it appears that a consciousness *without* flesh and *without* body is possible — and even without a soul, since the soul is correlative with corporeal flesh and founded upon it. Transcendental consciousness is thereby given as radically *disincarnated and decorporealized*. The worldly self-apperception would then signify incarnation and incorporation. But, Franck insists, we cannot stop there. Transcendental consciousness remains an intentional consciousness related to the phenomenon of the world; it remains a *perceiving* consciousness. And no perception is possible without my flesh. So it is necessary to admit that flesh *belongs to transcendental subjectivity itself*. This, moreover, is the only way to give the expression "incarnate givenness" its full weight and its enigmatic sense. Worldly apperception therefore cannot simply be identified with incarnation.

Once again, Franck stresses, flesh is not something material or corporeal linked to conscious phenomena by a functional dependence. Husserl indicates this with a curious fictional example, which Franck cites: imagine a consciousness — my consciousness, say — standing in relation to a locomotive such that, if the locomotive were given water, this consciousness would have the pleasant feeling we call satiety; if the locomotive were heated, it would have the feeling of warmth, and so on. Obviously the locomotive would not thereby become *flesh* for this consciousness. If, instead of the thing I call my flesh, the locomotive stood in my consciousness as the field of my pure ego, I still could not call it flesh — it simply would not *be* a flesh. The point is that mere functional or causal correlation between consciousness and a physical thing does not make that thing into flesh. If my consciousness were merely mechanically united with the locomotive, it would not be able to *perceive*: what sense would the concept of adumbration have under that hypothesis? Could one speak of "incarnate givenness"? Under no circumstances. Yet it remains true that my flesh is always *also* constituted as a body, and that corporeality is the layer of the world founding all others. Husserl writes that all objects in the world are in essence "embodied." Through incorporation, flesh *enworlds* (*mondanise*) transcendental consciousness. And this also implies — since incorporation refers to intersubjectivity — that neither the worldly self-apperception nor the reduction can take place *without the other*. Is not the very expression "self-objectification" a tacit confession of this? Franck asks: why does intersubjectivity increasingly appear *primary* within a framework that was initially an egology? What animates phenomenology in this contradictory way? The impossibility of constituting flesh as a body within the egological sphere of ownness provides a first — but negative — indication. It requires us to investigate the *positive* meaning of this impossible incorporation, that is, to interrogate the sense of flesh.

Franck then takes up Heidegger's criticism. In the letter of 22 October 1927, Heidegger states that the pure psyche does not result from an ontology of the human being as a whole, and at the same time reproaches Husserl for clinging to the traditional concept of the human being. Heidegger thus holds psychology and somatology to be *derivative* from the concrete totality of Dasein. But Franck offers two responses. (1) Since the question is to determine the place of the transcendental and to analyze the "mode of being of the being in which the world is constituted," absolute constitutive subjectivity remains untouched by Heidegger's line of argument — unless one immediately identifies it with pure psychological subjectivity. But that would be to misunderstand the sense of the reduction (the very reproach Husserl continually directed at Heidegger), interpreting the being of transcendental subjectivity only within the horizon of *subsistence* (*Vorhandenheit*, mere presence-at-hand). For transcendental subjectivity is essentially *temporal* — and Heidegger himself claims in *Being and Time* to be the first to break with the vulgar concept of time that accompanies *Vorhandenheit*. (2) The real question is whether one can reduce the *soul-flesh* relation to the *soul-body* relation inherited from the Cartesian tradition, and whether one can identify flesh and body. Nothing is less sure, Franck says — even for Heidegger. One might object that flesh is a *living* body, but this hardly advances the problem: phenomenologically, no analysis of life is possible without first accounting for flesh, through which life is shown and given. One might object more pointedly by rejecting the question itself: the fact that Husserl maintains a distinction between two regions, soul and flesh, changes nothing — because *flesh alone, and not the soul, belongs de jure to the constituting transcendental subjectivity*, and consequently flesh cannot be taken as regional. So, strange as it may seem, flesh, in the sense at issue here (as non-corporeal), is *not correlative to the psyche*. In response to Heidegger's question in the letter — whether the absolute ego is in one sense identical with, and in another sense different from, the de facto ego — Franck answers: it is so because of the *dual phenomenal status of flesh*. It is because flesh is always *also* a body.

Finally, Franck traces the reversibility of the analytic in both directions. By virtue of psycho-phenomenological parallelism and worldly apperception, everything belonging to my transcendental ego's sphere of ownness belongs equally to my psychological ego. Conversely — and in the Fifth Meditation Husserl often runs the analysis in two opposite directions — starting from the *soul*, I can return to the *transcendental ego*: everything that belongs properly to the soul will be found to belong to the ownness of transcendental subjectivity. So when, as transcendental ego, I reduce the phenomena of the objective and intersubjective world to my ownness, and add what is absolutely my own (the absolute ego), all of this property — within the phenomenon of the reduced world — becomes the property of my *soul*. This psychological property, the mundanization of the transcendental ego, is a *secondary* transcendental phenomenon precisely because of this mundanization. But this secondary transcendental phenomenon is itself constituted by the absolute ego; and if the sphere of this experience splits into a sphere of ownness and a sphere of the alien, then this division must belong immediately to the sphere of experience of the *constituting* ego. The constitution of *other transcendental egos* is thus possible. With this, the chapter establishes — at least formally — the route by which intersubjectivity becomes thinkable from within the egological framework.

## **Chapter Ten: The Alteration of Ownness**

The concept of ownness used so far has signified that which is *not-alien* (*non-étranger*). But this negative characterization depended on — and thus presupposed — the other and alterity in general. It therefore becomes necessary to elaborate a *positive and autonomous* concept of ownness (*propriété*), so that the reduction to ownness can be carried out rigorously. Once again, it is by returning to the phenomenology of perception that Husserl achieves, or at least tries to achieve, this. What happens in perception that might lead to a proper concept of ownness? First, an object is *passively pre-given*: it is given to intuition as the "indeterminate object of empirical intuition." This pre-givenness, or simple apprehension, founds active perception (Franck has already analyzed it). Active perception then enters into the horizons of the object and leads to a pure *explication* of it.

Franck describes this. Phenomenologically, every conceivable object is a *structural rule* for its modes of givenness — meaning that single apprehensions are ordered relative to one another as apprehensions of the *same* object. In perceiving an object, I link up — on the basis of a continually given and constituted identity — the determinations belonging to the object with those anticipated on its horizon. So perceiving is never merely *explicating* what is there, as intentional analysis reveals. But how can we account for the fact that the apprehension of a new property is not the apprehension of a *different* object but of a property belonging to the *same* object? The structure of explication must constitute in a *double* sense: the sense of the object-substrate and the sense of the qualities. In other words, the logical categories of "subject" and "quality" must derive their origin from explication. Franck cites Husserl's example: we observe a copper bowl; our glance runs over it, fixes for a moment on its roundness, returns to it, is drawn to a spot that stands out, jumps to a large shiny patch, follows the shimmer, is struck by the bosses, takes the cluster as a unity, runs over the bosses one by one, and so on.

In doing this, the I is always oriented toward the object *as a whole*, and each particular orientation coincides *partially* with the overall aim, producing a synthesis of overlapping and coincidence. But for such a synthesis to take place, the I must keep the *total apprehension* in view and maintain the whole as something *more* than what is actually perceived, since the whole "encroaches" on this or that current perception. The *presence* of the thing is not to be confused with the *present* of the giving perceptions. Having-in-grasp, as an enduring activity, constitutes the substrate; thanks to constantly new partial coincidences, it is always a *different* having-in-grasp, and at every step what is grasped as singular is "incorporated" (*einverleibt*) by the coincidence into the sense-content of the substrate.

Ownness, Franck now says, is *what is explicated* — what is displayed by the intentional analysis of the actualities and potentialities of the flow of lived experiences. The brief analysis above focused on the object and presupposed the identity of the ego performing the apprehension of the whole and the ego absorbed in the progressive inspection of details. A *parallel* analysis is possible, one that touches the ego itself, and it can give us the concept of ownness needed for the constitution of the other. By performing the reduction, I am given to myself *perceptually*. It belongs to the sense of this self-perception that I appear as given *prior to* this actual, intuitive, apodictic apprehension. The reduction opens me onto the infinite horizon of my properties, which intentional analysis tirelessly explores. Franck cites Husserl: my own is discovered by explication and gets its original sense thereby; it becomes uncovered *originaliter* when my experiencing-explicating regard is directed to myself, to my perceptually and even apodictically given "I-am" and its abiding self-identity in the continuous unitary synthesis of original self-experience. Whatever is included in this identical being's own essence is characterized as its actual or possible *explicatum*.

In the case of the *object*, each new explication is a new *perception*; but this does not hold for the *ego*. The explication of the ego, Husserl says, is largely carried out in acts of consciousness that are *not perceptions* of the own-essential moments they discover. For, when reduced to the mode of givenness — to what is phenomenologically determined — the essential feature of ownness can be described thus: what is one's own is *what is given to oneself in an apodictic perception*. But, Franck asks rigorously, does this not amount to defining ownness *temporally*, since an apodictic perception of oneself can occur only in the *incarnate living present*? Should one then exclude from the sphere of ownness both the *past* and the *future* of transcendental subjectivity? Does not the immanent temporality of the ego — the form in which lived experiences are ordered — contradict the very project of a reduction to ownness? If so, what relations obtain between temporality and alterity? Would one not have to recognize the relation to the *other* within temporality itself? Franck flags that he will return to these themes, which do not belong solely to the Husserlian horizon.

How does Husserl ward off the objection that, by drastically restricting the scope of apodicticity, threatens the establishment of philosophy as rigorous science? He responds with an argument privileging *original* givenness over *apodictic* givenness. In this he is faithful to the "apodictic naiveté" deliberately adopted by the *Cartesian Meditations*, whose aim is a pure *description* of experience rather than a *critique* of it. In essence: the past is given only in *memory*. Memory is the most original mode of access to the past; it is always a possibility for me, an "I can" derived from the proper essence of my ego. The evidence of "I was" or "I will be" participates in the apodictic evidence of "I am": memory and expectation are lived experiences with a complex temporal structure rooted in the living present. For this reason, all explication participates in apodicticity, according to a formal (and itself apodictic) law — "so much appearing (*Schein*), so much being" — on the condition of being as original as possible, that is, of bringing to light what is experienced in the type of self-givenness appropriate to it.

The fact remains, Franck notes, that this positive characterization of ownness *based on intentional analysis* precludes from the outset a question like: does intentionality not receive its innermost, deepest sense from the *intersubjective relation*? This is the same question raised earlier about time. The reasons that have paved the way for this rapprochement are altogether decisive, and Franck pursues them. If the sphere of ownness is the sphere of the flux of actual and potential lived experiences, then it also contains *intentional* lived experiences that intend and constitute "objective" unities — lived experiences with a noetic-noematic structure. Since the noema is inseparable from the noesis, the intentional object *also* belongs to the sphere of ownness. Husserl maintains that not only the constituting perceiving but also the perceived existent belongs to my concrete very-ownness, provided they are concretely connected there and that the intentional explication of one immediately implies the other. This concerns, first, the pure sensible data constituted within me as "immanent temporalities"; then my *habitus*; and, last but not least, *transcendent objects* — "the objects of external sensibility," as Husserl says. These objects must be constituted through my own sensibility, and so are always given through the mediation of *flesh*. Franck presses: what would "external sensibility" mean if flesh received no assignable limits in the sphere of ownness? What would "one's own sensibility" mean if sensibility always implies a relation to exteriority? By extending the sphere of ownness to transcendent objects, does Husserl not open it up to *alterity*?

The intentional ego is not conceivable without the *non-ego*. Despite its Fichtean resonances, Franck says, this can be found in Husserl. In a text edited between 1925 and 1928 on the concept of originality (or rather primordiality), Husserl splits the original experience of oneself into what is specifically *egoic* and the *hyle that is foreign to the ego* (*der ichfremden Hyle*): the I and consciousness are not thinkable without "unity," without a subjective substrate, without a hyle. The concrete I continually has in its conscious life a *kernel of hyle*, a non-I that nonetheless essentially belongs to the I; no I is possible without a domain of pre-givenness, a constituted domain of unity constituted as the not-I. For — and Franck calls this perhaps the most important statement in the *Cartesian Meditations* — "the intrinsically first other (the first 'non-Ego') is the *other Ego*." How, then, are we not led to define the being of the ego and of intentionality by the relation to the *alter ego*? And since the hyle foreign to the ego is *temporal*, should not the analysis of temporality unfold within the same horizon? Does not thinking the ego and time on the basis of the *other* take us to the very limits of phenomenology — to the point of turning against its own principles? Is there a phenomenal given capable of explaining this? What is it in the intentional analysis of the other that leads phenomenology to *go beyond itself*? All these questions, Franck says, show that it would be a mistake to regard the problem of the other as merely a regional problem.

The broadening of the sphere of ownness to transcendent "objects" can be regarded as the final "preparatory" stage on the way to an explication of intersubjectivity. It is the last but not the least, because by bringing to light an "immanent transcendence" characterizing intramonadic "objects," it shifts the balance of the relation between immanence and transcendence. Franck notes that Husserl came across this concept long before the *Cartesian Meditations*. In a course from the winter semester 1910-11, *Basic Problems of Phenomenology*, Husserl described how phenomenology necessarily *exceeds* the domain of what is absolutely given — which opened the path to a critique of pure experience and a delineation of the field of apodicticity. Phenomenological perception is temporal, and only the present *now* is given absolutely; this now is subject to a law of retentional modification. The past *transcends* the present now. Must this transcendence therefore be reduced? If so, the reduction would be absolutely radical — but phenomenology would *vanish for lack of a theme*. Hence Husserl's exclamation, which Franck cites: "Nevertheless, we are not deterred\!"

When we doubt a memory, Franck continues, we place in doubt the *actuality* of the past to which we are returned — and this presupposes that what is doubted is itself *absolutely given*. In other words, the givenness of the past is *also* an absolute givenness. Husserl writes that in every case, doubt is the structural givenness of the intention put in doubt; this lasting empirical phenomenon of givenness is given, and absolutely given, in its own individuality and duration. It is thus necessary to admit a "transcendence" *within* phenomenological immanence, for what is received phenomenologically is not only the *retention* as such but also *that which is retained*: each now of a retention is a retention of a *not-now*. This "transcendence in immanence" is irreducible; any attempt to reduce it presupposes it. If I want to exclude the not-now from the field of phenomenal givenness, I can only do so by knowing what the now is and that it is the fluid limit between two not-nows (past and future) — but this knowledge implies that the not-now *is given to me*. And how could it be given if retention, as retention-of-…, were put out of play? How could I even reach the field of phenomenal givenness if "transcendence in immanence" fell under the reduction?

What does Husserl do by going back to this concept in order to characterize the objects reduced to ownness? Just as he included a *not-now* in a "now" broadened to absolute givenness, so he includes a *not-me* in the monadic ego. This is both an intentional and a *real* inclusion — since placing the object or the past on the side of the non-me, as what is foreign to the ego, is to place them on the side of the *hyle*: that is, to establish them as the *non-intentional* component of the act, its real component (at least at the level of constituted temporality). Here inclusion is at once intentional and real. Without inclusion, "transcendence in immanence" — which also characterizes the *self* in its relation to the lived experiences in the temporal flow — would be inconceivable, and phenomenology as a science would be impossible, since this "transcendence" gives it a field of objects. But at the same time, the entire system of relations between immanence and transcendence — indeed, intentionality itself — is precisely what is here being put in search of itself.

Franck demonstrates this further. In the same 1910-11 course, Husserl insists on the plurivocity of the concepts of immanence and transcendence and tries to determine their phenomenological sense. First, he characterizes the *object of knowledge* as transcendent insofar as it is not present in the act of knowing — and this applies even to phenomenological *seeing*, since what is seen is not literally *in* the act of seeing. But, Husserl notes, one does not speak of transcendence in this respect, because then the contrast with immanence would lose its meaning. The contrast becomes meaningful only when one contrasts being *bodily present in the flesh* for consciousness with what is *purely intended* (for example, memory as such, and what is remembered in it). The criterion of this first difference is *incarnate presence*. One might wonder whether the incarnate present does not simply reproduce the difference between real immanence and intentional immanence — and ask what transcendence would then be, and whether one can distinguish intentional immanence from intentional transcendence.

Transcendence and immanence can be understood in another sense, having to do with a division of *individual objects* according to whether they are given in *absolute self-presence* or through *appearances and presentations*. If the immanence of the former is self-evident, the transcendence of the latter is not immediately evident. Husserl affirms that in this case every phenomenological consciousness refers to *immanence*: consciousness intends the *adumbration*, not what natural consciousness posits as the thing. The transcendent is thus the object offered in a multiplicity of harmonized adumbrations. Later Husserl ties this transcendence back to a form of immanence: the transcendence of intuitive things — of what is given to me in perception "in the flesh" — is, we can now say, only a *form of immanence*, an immanence in a good sense. Once more, we have two types of immanence. There is nonetheless a difference between the two pairs of concepts: the second pair (transcendence/immanence of objects) is *founded on* the first (real/intentional immanence, via incarnate presence). To distinguish immanence from transcendence on the basis of a distinction between objects is to presuppose the very *possibility* of those objects, and without the "transcendence in immanence" of retention no object could be given at all. The concepts in play here are those of the *first* pair. It is too early, Franck says, to grasp why and how the concept of "transcendence in immanence" comes to *dismantle* all the conceptuality from which it originates. But he offers one indication: the identification of intentional inclusion with real inclusion presupposes a *unity of hyle and morphé* prior to their separation — and consequently points back to the *original role of constitutive temporality*.

The primordial world belonging to the ego's sphere of ownness does not have the sense of an objective, intersubjective world. The task is thus to understand how, at the founded higher level, the sense-bestowal pertaining to *transcendency proper* — to constitutionally secondary objective transcendency — comes about, and does so *as an experience*. This is not a matter of temporal genesis but of a purely *static* analysis of experience. This experience of the objective world always has the sense of an experience of something *other than me*, something that totally transcends my own being. The analysis of the constitution of the other must therefore be able to answer the question of the origin of experience in general. In the most basic sense, Franck concludes, it is a *transcendental* analysis — and with this the chapter sets the stage for the analysis of how the other is concretely constituted.

## **Chapter Eleven: The Incarnation of Another Body**

Franck opens with Husserl indicating, in a programmatic way, the approach needed to arrive at an intentional elucidation of how the other becomes constituted in me. (The chapter's text begins with Franck following Husserl's indication that the experience of the other must be traced as a layered intentional accomplishment.) The other, as we have seen, is given in a peculiar manner: it is at once present to me as a body in my perceptual field and yet, as another subjectivity, can never be made *originally* present. The whole problem is to understand how a body appearing within my primordial sphere can come to be apprehended as another *flesh*, another seat of an "I," without ever being given to me in the originary way my own flesh is.

Franck stresses the foundational asymmetry. My own flesh is the one body that is given to me from the "inside," as the bearer of sensings (*Empfindnisse*), as the zero-point of orientation, as the organ I directly move and govern. Every other body in my field is given only from the "outside," through adumbrations, as a physical thing. The question of the constitution of the other is therefore the question of how a merely externally given body can take on the sense "flesh of another" — how the sense that belongs uniquely and originally to *my* flesh can be transferred to a body over there.

The mechanism Husserl proposes is an *apperceptive transfer* (also called an analogizing apprehension). Just as, in ordinary perception, the seen front of a thing "apperceives" its unseen back — co-intends it without presenting it — so the body of the other, perceived as similar to my own, apperceptively carries over to itself the sense "flesh." This transfer is not an inference, not a reasoning by analogy in the logical sense, and not an act of judgment; it is a passive, associative accomplishment that happens automatically once the resemblance between the other's body and my own becomes salient. Franck emphasizes that the transfer rests on *resemblance* (the similarity between the body there and my body here) and that this resemblance functions as the associative trigger for the pairing that the next chapter will analyze.

Franck draws out the difficulties packed into this account. First, for the transfer to work, my own flesh must already be available to me *as a body* — incorporated, possessing the sense of a physical thing among physical things — since otherwise there would be no common ground of resemblance between "my body" and "the body over there." But the previous chapters have shown precisely that the incorporation of flesh (its constitution as a body) cannot be carried out within the solipsistic sphere of ownness without already presupposing the other. So the apperceptive transfer that is supposed to *constitute* the other seems to presuppose an incorporation that itself presupposes the other. The circularity that has haunted the analysis returns here in a sharpened form.

Second, the transfer is supposed to operate from what is *originally given* (my flesh) to what can *never* be originally given (the other's subjectivity). In every ordinary apperception, the co-intended, appresented component can in principle be converted into a presentation — I can walk around the thing and bring its hidden back into view. But the appresented interiority of the other is, by essential necessity, something that can *never* become a presentation. If I could make the other's conscious life originally present to me, it would be *mine*, and the other would cease to be other. So the apperception of the other is a unique kind of apperception: one whose appresented component is, in principle and forever, inaccessible to original fulfillment.

Third, Franck underscores the role of the *living present* and the permanence of my flesh. For the other to be constituted as the permanent correlate of an objective world that is always there, the "primally instituting original" — my own flesh — must be *livingly and permanently present*. The transfer is not a one-time event but a continually effective accomplishment: my flesh is always here, always functioning as the standard from which the sense "flesh" is carried over to other bodies. This is why Husserl will say that ego and alter ego are always and necessarily given in an original *pairing*.

Franck also returns to the *ambiguity* of the other's mode of givenness, now sharpened. The other is given to a fulfilled intuition in one respect — as a body, a physical thing in my perceptual field, presented through concordant adumbrations — and *refuses* fulfilled intuition in another respect — as another subjectivity, another "I," which is only appresented and never presented. The other is thus the site where presentation and appresentation are inseparably combined into a single, unitary, transcending experience. The body of the other is given originally; the egoic life "governing" that body is given only appresentatively, carried along with the presentation by the force of resemblance and pairing.

Throughout, Franck keeps pressing the question of whether this account does justice to the *otherness* of the other or whether it reduces the other to a mere intentional modification of myself. If the other is constituted by an apperceptive transfer originating in *my* flesh, and if the other's sense is derived by analogy from *my* self-givenness, then the other threatens to be nothing more than a variant or "modification" of me — an alter *ego*, an *other* I, modeled on and derived from my own ego. This is an intentional, not a real, modification: the other is not literally contained in me as me (that would collapse the distinction entirely), but is constituted as a *modified* repetition of my own egoic sense. The deep question, which Franck keeps in view, is whether such a derivation can ever reach a *genuine* other — an other irreducible to me — or whether the very method of constitution guarantees in advance that the other will be missed precisely *as* other.

Finally, Franck situates the incarnation of another body within the broader stakes of the book. The constitution of the other's flesh is the *first* and most basic layer of intersubjectivity: before there can be a shared objective world, a community of monads, or a culture, there must first be the bare apprehension of another flesh. And this most basic layer turns entirely on the relation of one flesh to another — on *carnal difference* and resemblance. If this foundational layer cannot be secured within the egological framework without covertly presupposing what it is supposed to produce, then the entire edifice of phenomenological intersubjectivity — and with it phenomenological idealism — rests on something it cannot, by its own resources, constitute. The chapter thus prepares the detailed analysis of *pairing and resemblance* that follows, where the associative mechanism of the apperceptive transfer is examined in full.

## **Chapter Twelve: Pairing and Resemblance**

Franck opens by citing the key Husserlian passage on the analogizing apprehension by which a body in my primordial sphere, being similar to my own animate body, comes to be apprehended as likewise *flesh*. Three features stand out. First, the "primally instituting original" — my own flesh — is *always livingly present*, so the primal instituting is always going on in a livingly effective manner. Second, what is appresented by this analogizing can *never* attain actual presence, never become an object of perception proper. Third — closely connected to the first feature — ego and alter ego are always and necessarily given in an original *pairing*.

The constitution of the other, Franck explains, is the centerpiece of the constitution of the objective world, which is given as a world always there and constantly present. So in analyzing the intentional constitution of the alter ego, one must also be able to determine the origin of this *permanence*. Husserl locates it in the constancy of the *living present of my own flesh*. For flesh to fulfill this assigned role, several presuppositions must be admitted. (1) If my flesh must be given in the constancy of a living present, then it must be *fully there*, with none of it merely appresented or co-presented. But what is constantly and permanently living-and-present is my *body-flesh* (*Leibkörper*), not simply my flesh — and the presentation of a *body* never occurs without an *appresentation* (a body always has hidden sides). Normally this appresentation can in principle be converted into a presentation, but the body-flesh is an exception: I cannot modify my position relative to my own body so as to see originally what I have only anticipated of it. And even if flesh were understood merely as a basis for the various *Empfindnisse*, nothing would change, since flesh is always and essentially *incompletely constituted*. (2) For my body-flesh to play its role, it must be supposed that it can *neither be born nor die*. Franck notes that Husserl, as a general and explicit rule, always treated death as a *mundane accident*, devoting very few pages to it, because death so understood does not affect the possibility of the constitution of the world. But, Franck argues, this surely also has to do with the absence of a thematization of flesh. Once one insists on the flesh, it is no longer possible to treat the problems of *generativity* (birth, death, the succession of generations) as secondary, or to leave the question of *finitude* outside phenomenology. In a note from 1915-17, Husserl defines death as the "decay" (*Zerfall*) of flesh, the dissolution of the mode of appearing of the thingly material. So Husserl does recognize that death belongs to the sense of flesh — but he also recognizes that the egological constitution of the world is thereby affected by *contingency*, since flesh is the "zero-object" (*Nullobjekt*), the condition of possibility of all other objects. A fortiori, the disappearance of flesh annuls the appearing of an intersubjective world, because flesh performs the constitution of that world. And if no constitution *in* or *of* the world is conceivable without flesh, Franck asks, does this not also apply to the self-constitution of the intentional ego, which contains the world as its noematic correlate — and even to the ultimate constitution of *temporality*? (3) The objective world is a *common* world, where each thing is the same for all; it has the sense of a *normal* world. So my flesh — from which comes the apperceptive transfer constituting the other — must be a *normal* flesh. But, Franck observes, while the determination of a transcendental intersubjective *normality* already poses formidable problems (which Husserl increasingly engaged as he showed the historicity of intentional genesis), the problems arising from an *intra-egological* norm may be insoluble: it is difficult to grant any sense to the very idea of an egological normality. Franck cites Husserl's awkward definition of normality, which makes reference to two normal individuals who exchange or can exchange their places and find the same appearances in their consciousness. This definition, Franck notes, first appeals to intersubjectivity and then to a *bodily* normality of the individual prior to any relation to the other — and ultimately understands normality in relation to *objectivity* (my "normal" vision of a thing is the one I have when I take the other's place in an objective space that already presupposes the other). What, then, can egological bodily normality mean? How could it explain *anomalies* in solipsistic experience? Under what conditions is it possible? Husserl answers that normal experience is a *harmonious* experience of the same world, while an anomaly is a *discordant* one. But if the harmony in question is one prevailing over my various kinaesthetic systems — I see what I touch, and it is the same thing — then the norm established is the norm *of my flesh*, which does not imply that my flesh is itself normal. With respect to the kinaesthetic system as a whole, this harmony can only mean a *harmonizing with itself*, a constancy in the functioning of my flesh. In that case the result is the same: I never reach a *norm*, and moreover birth and death become unthinkable. When the objective world is normalized, it is impossible to define the normality of flesh within the sphere of ownness: *my flesh becomes the norm*. But to posit my flesh as the norm for all other fleshes — is this not, once again, to expose the phenomenology of intersubjectivity to *contingency*? Do we not rediscover, by another path, the *archi-facticity* that the paradoxes of the reduction to the *eidos ego* already forced us to admit? Can this be understood on the basis of flesh?

The other, Franck continues, acquires its sense from an apperceptive transfer coming from *myself*: a bodily ownness resembles my own body and, for that reason, acquires the sense of flesh that belongs only to me. (Franck flags Husserl's strange parenthesis — "later, that of the other" — which intensifies the difficulty: how can I know in advance that the body over there *can become* the body of an other?) For the moment, Franck retains only the point that ego and alter ego are always given in an original *pairing*.

What is pairing? Franck cites Husserl: it is an original form of *passive and associative synthesis* by which two distinct data are offered as a *unity* within the same consciousness. This is the basis of a unity of resemblance and constitutes the two data as a *couple* or *pair*. When two analogous bodies are perceived simultaneously in the same space, consciousness is conscious of a kind of unity in which "one is not without the other," the similar refers to the similar, the one "awakens" (*weckt*) the other. When I am affected by one, this reinforces the affection by the other, and vice versa. This mode of consciousness has an essential tendency to pass from one to the other — an "intentional overreaching" (*Übergreifen*) that comes about genetically, by essential necessity, as soon as the paired data have become prominent and are simultaneously intended. The members of the couple *exchange their sense*; the one is apprehended in conformity with the sense of the other. Two conditions are required for pairing: (1) the preliminary constitution of the *objective sense* of each member of the couple; (2) the purely egological constitution of *temporality* through associative and passive syntheses (within which a genesis is possible) — for if, in passing from one objective sense to the other, I could not maintain a *retentional* hold on the first, then no coincidence of sense, not even partial, could appear to me.

Can these conditions be fulfilled in the case of the pairing of ego and alter ego? To answer, Franck examines the constituting apperception of the other, whose description, he says, is perhaps not always as accurate as it should be. It begins: "pairing first comes about when the Other enters my field of perception." But, Franck objects, if the other can *enter* my perceptual field, then pairing is not a *permanent and constitutive* structure (contradicting the earlier claim that ego and alter ego are *always* paired). And how could I know that what appears is *the other* and not just *any body whatsoever*, unless I abandon the regime of the transcendental reduction and the reduction to ownness and return to the *natural attitude*, where the other is *already* constituted? And even if the description is carried out more rigorously, it still presupposes that flesh was able to be *incorporated* (to possess the sense of body) within the sphere of ownness — since, as Husserl says, my live body (*Leibkörper*) is always there and sensuously prominent and is at the same time, with primordial originariness, equipped with the specific sense of a flesh.

When a body resembling my own is presented in my primordial sphere, it should at the same time acquire the sense of *flesh* by transfer — that is, it would be a *second flesh* within my sphere of ownness. No alterity could be constituted, however, without the *second* property of the analogizing experience: what is appresented can *never*, in principle, be presented. (Franck inserts a crucial caveat: for this argument to be fully valid, it must presuppose that my *own* flesh, by contrast with the other's, *can* be intuitively and completely given to me — and thus fully constituted. But that is precisely *not* the case, as the analyses of incompleteness have shown.)

Franck stresses the point on which Husserl insists more than any other: the givenness of the other is *irreducibly mediated*; it cannot be offered in a direct intuition; it cannot present itself. The phenomenon of the other is a *non-phenomenon*. This, Franck says, is why the Fifth Meditation leads to the *limits of phenomenology*, and why he has undertaken to interpret it. Nevertheless, this non-phenomenality has its *own style of confirmation*. But confirmation generally means *evidence* and *intuitive fulfillment*: an intention is confirmed when its aim is given in an incarnate intuition, and such givenness grounds a rational positing and a value of being. So, to respect the non-phenomenality of the other, the transfer of sense must be done in such a way that the transferred sense — the sense "flesh" and "ego" — acquires a *value of existence without becoming the object of an original giving intuition*. In other words, the question is how the analogizing apperception of the other is possible, and why it is not *crossed out* or annulled by the very non-intuition that defines it.

There is no phenomenological difficulty here, Franck says (echoing Husserl's confidence), that a careful description cannot in principle resolve or at least elucidate. Husserl examines the intentional situation more closely: the appresentation that gives the inaccessible component of the other is *combined* with an original presentation (of "his" body as part of the nature given in my ownness), and in this combination the other's animate body and his governing ego are given in the manner of a *unitary transcending experience*. Franck finds this a strange description: how can the body of the other — "his body" — be a part of *my* sphere of ownness? How can the body presented to me already be a body-*flesh*, when it is precisely *through* apperception that it is supposed to acquire the sense of flesh? Everything here happens, Franck says, as if respect for *alterity* were incompatible with respect for the *closure of egology* — the very point of departure required for a transcendental phenomenology.

Provisionally accepting Husserl's account, Franck examines the *mode of confirmation* of analogical apperception. The presented body is given in concordant adumbrations and confirmed by them; in virtue of the resemblance that *pairs* it with my flesh, it receives the sense of flesh and thus appresents an egoic life, *another I*. The harmony of *presentations* establishes (or "indicates") a harmony of *appresentations*. So the experience of the other is the experience of an *indication*: what gets confirmed is an *index*, not what it indicates. The experience of the other obtains the status of an experience through *interpretation*. It thus falls under the broadest definition of indication from the first *Logical Investigation*: that certain objects whose reality someone actually knows *indicate* to him the reality of certain other objects, such that belief in the reality of the one is experienced — though not evidently — as *motivating* belief or surmise in the reality of the other.

In phenomenology, Franck stresses, it is absolutely *exceptional* that an experience can *interpret* its object (normally experience *gives* its object directly). One might be tempted — and here Franck announces the direction in which he is heading — to turn this *exception* into the *basis of the rule*, and so to ask whether the relation to the alter ego (more precisely, the relation of one flesh to another, which is the first constitutive layer of intersubjectivity) is what *animates and mobilizes* Husserl's entire enterprise while at the same time *escaping* it. How, then, could we think a *carnal relation*? The scope of this question, Franck says, extends beyond phenomenology — if one grants that phenomenology is prior to all philosophy. If what sets phenomenology in motion remains outside its grasp, this applies *a fortiori* to philosophy understood as metaphysics. And, Franck asks pointedly, if a thought attempts to step outside philosophy and metaphysics (as Heidegger's does), why did it not pursue an *analysis of flesh*? Does not thought receive its very vocation from the *carnal relation*? All these questions, he says, belong to the field of an *analytic of incarnation*.

The apperception of the other finds its confirmation in a presentation functioning as the *indication of something that cannot be presented* (*un imprésentable*). Husserl asserts that the character of the existent "other" has its basis in this kind of *verifiable accessibility of what is not originally accessible*. The other is henceforth determined phenomenologically by its *mode of givenness*. Before pursuing the analysis in which the possibility and scope of a transcendental constitution of the objective world become evident — and transcendental-phenomenological idealism becomes fully manifest — Franck makes two further remarks. First, to define the other by a mode of givenness that is *not original* but originally *modified* amounts to turning the other into an *intentional modification of myself*. This is an intentional, not a *real*, modification — since in the latter case the other would simply be in me, as me. Second, intentionality itself thereby *carries the burden of alterity*: the intentional modification is an element composing its very sense. The same holds for my relation, within the sphere of ownness, to the *past*: the past is given to me through memory, that is, through a present lived experience that points within itself back to the past as a *modified* present. Husserl underscores the analogy: somewhat as my memorial past, as a modification of my living present, "transcends" my present, the apprehended other "transcends" my own being (in the most fundamental sense: what is included in my primordial ownness). But there is a difference: the past that is mine was once necessarily a *non-modified* present offered to direct intuition, whereas the other has *never been and never will be* offered in this way. In this regard, the modification is more *complex* when it comes to alterity. So although Husserl frequently associated the past with intersubjectivity, this does not mean that the transcendence of the other is of the *same order* as the transcendence of the past. On the contrary, it is *more radical* — and for that very reason risks being *more fundamental*. Is this to say, Franck asks in closing, that, despite all "appearances," the past itself comes to the ego *through its relation to the other*? It is the question already met earlier: whether temporality is not, at bottom, *defined as a relation to the other*. With this question — which gathers together the threads of flesh, time, and alterity that run through the whole book — Chapter Twelve ends.

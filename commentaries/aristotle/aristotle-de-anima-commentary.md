# Aristoteles Περὶ ψυχῆς

## **Book I**

### **Chapter 1**

Aristotle opens the treatise by ranking the investigation of the soul among the finest and most admirable objects of knowledge. He says that we place the study of soul among things of the highest value, both for its exactness and for the worthiness and wonder of the things it treats. Knowledge of the soul, he argues, seems to contribute greatly to all truth, and above all to the understanding of nature, since soul is, as it were, the principle of living things. Aristotle's aim is to examine and come to know both the nature of the soul and its essential being, and then whatever properties belong to it. Of these properties, some seem to be distinctive affections of the soul itself, while others seem to belong to living things through the soul but not to the soul in isolation. He acknowledges at the outset that this is in every way one of the hardest investigations to carry through. Since the inquiry concerns the being and the what-it-is of the soul, one might think there could be a single method applicable to everything whose essential nature we wish to know — the way demonstration is the method for a thing's incidental properties — and that we should seek out this one method. But if there is no single common method, the task becomes still harder, since we would need to determine in each case which procedure to follow. And even if it becomes clear whether the right approach is demonstration or division or some other method, there remain many puzzles and false starts concerning the first principles from which the investigation must begin.

Aristotle goes on to observe that the starting points differ for different subject matters — one set of principles for numbers, another for planes, another for soul — and he raises the question of what genus the soul falls under. Is it a "this something" — an individual substance — or rather a quality, or a quantity, or something in one of the other categories of being? Furthermore, is it something that exists potentially or rather an actuality of some kind? These questions, he says, make no small difference to the inquiry. One must also consider whether the soul is divisible or without parts, and whether every soul is of the same kind or not. If not all souls are of the same kind, one must ask whether they differ in species or in genus. As things stand, he notes, those who discuss and investigate the soul seem to examine only the human soul, and one must be careful not to let it escape notice whether there is a single account of soul — as there is of "animal" in general — or a different account for each kind, as for horse, dog, human, or god. The universal term "animal," taken as such, is either nothing at all or posterior to the particular kinds. The same worry applies to any other common predicate asserted of soul.

Furthermore, even if one discovers that there are not many souls but merely parts of one soul, the question arises whether one should investigate the whole soul first, or its parts. It is difficult to determine which of these parts are genuinely different from one another in nature, and also whether one should investigate the parts first or their functions — for instance, the act of thinking or the intellect, the act of perceiving or the perceptive faculty. And if the functions come first, one might again ask whether the objects should be investigated before the corresponding faculties — the perceived object before the perceptive capacity, the intelligible object before the intellect. It does seem that knowing what a thing is — its essential definition — is useful for understanding the causes of its incidental properties, just as in mathematics knowing what "straight" and "curved" and "line" and "surface" are helps us see how many right angles the angles of a triangle are equal to. Conversely, knowing the incidental properties makes a considerable contribution to knowing what a thing is. Whenever we can give an account, based on how things appear, of all or most of a thing's incidental properties, we will be in the best position to say what the thing essentially is. For the starting point of every demonstration is the what-it-is, so that definitions which do not lead us to know the incidental properties, and which do not even make it easy to form conjectures about them, are clearly stated in a merely dialectical and empty fashion.

The affections of the soul also raise a special puzzle: whether all of them are shared with what has the soul — that is, the living body — or whether some belong to the soul alone and are peculiar to it. It is necessary to grasp this, though it is not easy. In most cases, it appears that the soul neither undergoes anything nor acts without the body — as in being angry, being confident, desiring, and perceiving in general. Thinking, above all, seems to be distinctive of the soul alone. But if thinking too is a kind of imagination, or at least does not occur without imagination, then even thinking could not exist without the body. If, then, some one of the functions or affections of the soul is peculiar to it, the soul could be separated from the body. But if nothing is peculiar to the soul in this way, then the soul would not be separable, but would be like the straight, which as straight has many properties — for instance, it touches a bronze sphere at a point — and yet straightness itself, separated from the bronze, would not so touch the sphere. For the straight is never found apart from some body. It seems, then, that all the affections of the soul involve a body: spiritedness, gentleness, fear, pity, courage, and further joy, loving, and hating. For together with these affections the body is affected in some way. Evidence for this is that sometimes, when strong and obvious sufferings befall us, we are not provoked to agitation or fear, while at other times, when the bodily condition is tense and resembles the state of anger, we are moved by small and faint stimuli.

This point is made even clearer by the fact that people sometimes fall into the states characteristic of those who are terrified even when nothing fearful is happening. If this is so, then the affections are evidently "enmattered accounts" — formulae embedded in matter. Consequently their definitions must be of a corresponding kind, such as: "being angry is a certain motion of a body of such-and-such a kind — or a part or capacity of it — produced by this cause and for the sake of that." And it is for this reason that the study of the soul falls to the natural philosopher — either the whole of it or at least the soul insofar as it has such properties. But the natural philosopher and the dialectician would define each of these affections differently. Take anger, for example: the dialectician would define it as a desire for retaliation, or something of the sort; the natural philosopher would define it as a boiling of the blood and the hot stuff around the heart. Of these two, one gives the matter, the other the form and the account. For the account is the form of the thing, but if it is to exist, it must be in matter of a certain kind. So the account of a house, for instance, is something like this: "a shelter that prevents destruction by wind, rain, and heat." One person will describe it as stones and timbers and roof-tiles; another will say it is the form in these materials for the sake of certain ends. Which of these is the natural philosopher? Is it the one who concerns himself with the matter but is ignorant of the account? Or the one who concerns himself only with the account? Or rather the one who proceeds from both? And what about each of the others? Is there anyone who deals with the inseparable affections of matter, yet not qua separable? The natural philosopher deals with all the functions and affections of a given kind of body and a given kind of matter. What does not belong to the body in that way falls to another inquirer — in some cases to the craftsman, such as a carpenter or a physician. The affections that are not separable from body but are not considered as affections of a given kind of body — these the mathematician treats by abstraction. Those that are considered as separated from all body belong to the first philosopher.

But to return to the point from which we digressed: we were saying that the affections of the soul are, in this way, inseparable from the physical matter of living things — insofar as such things as spiritedness and fear belong to them — and not like line and surface.

### **Chapter 2**

In surveying what has been handed down about the soul, it is necessary, when inquiring into it alongside our own investigations, to take into account the views of those predecessors who said anything about it, so that we may accept what they said well and guard against whatever is not well stated. The starting point of the inquiry is to set down those properties that seem above all to belong to the soul by nature. The ensouled thing seems to differ from the unensouled in two respects above all: by movement and by perception. And these are roughly the two characteristics we have received from our predecessors concerning soul. For some say that the soul is above all and primarily that which produces movement. But believing that what does not itself move cannot move another thing, they supposed the soul to be something that is itself in motion. Thus Democritus says the soul is a sort of fire and a hot thing. For of the unlimited atoms — the unlimited shapes — he says those that are spherical are fire and soul, like the so-called motes in the air visible in sunbeams coming through windows. He says the whole seed-mass of such atoms is the elements of the whole of nature. Leucippus says much the same. Of these shapes, the spherical ones are identified as soul, because atoms of this configuration are most able to permeate everywhere and to set other things in motion while being themselves in motion — the assumption being that soul is what furnishes animals with motion. This is also why breathing is taken to be the defining mark of life. For as the surrounding air compresses the bodies of animals and tends to squeeze out those atoms that are spherical in shape — the very atoms that furnish animals with motion, since they are themselves never at rest — help comes from outside when other such atoms enter in breathing, since they too prevent the atoms already within the animal from being forced out, by counteracting the compressing and solidifying effect. And animals continue to live, they say, as long as they can carry on doing this.

The doctrine attributed to the Pythagoreans seems to involve the same idea. For some of them said the soul is the motes in the air; others, that it is what moves the motes. These motes were singled out because they are seen to be in continuous motion, even when there is a complete calm. The same tendency — to say that the soul is what moves itself — is found in those who say that soul is that which moves itself. For all of these thinkers seem to have supposed that motion is the most characteristic feature of the soul, and that while all other things are moved by the soul, the soul is moved by itself. This is because they see nothing causing motion that is not itself in motion.

In a similar fashion, Anaxagoras too says the soul is what produces motion — and so does anyone else who has said that mind set the whole in motion — though not quite in the same way as Democritus. For Democritus flatly identified soul with mind, since he held that what appears is what is true, and therefore Homer rightly composed the line about Hector lying with "other-thinking," that is, delirious. Democritus does not use mind as a faculty concerned with truth, but identifies soul and mind. Anaxagoras is less clear on these matters. In many places he says that the cause of beauty and order is mind, but elsewhere he says that mind is the soul — for he says it is present in all animals, both large and small, both honored and less honored. But mind in the sense of intelligence does not seem to belong in the same way to all animals, nor even to all human beings.

Those who focused on the fact that the ensouled thing moves have taken the soul to be the most movement-producing thing. But those who focused on the fact that the ensouled thing knows and perceives the things that are — these thinkers identify the soul with the principles of things. Some posit many such principles, others only one. Empedocles, for instance, says the soul is composed of all the elements, and that each of them is in fact a soul. He puts it thus: "By earth we see earth, by water water, by air divine air, and by fire destructive fire; love by love, and strife by baneful strife." In the same way, Plato in the *Timaeus* constructs the soul from the elements. For like is known by like, he holds, and things are composed of the principles. In a similar way it was laid down in the lectures "On Philosophy" that the Animal-itself is constituted from the Idea of the One together with the primary length, breadth, and depth, and that other things are constituted in a similar fashion. And again it is put differently: mind is the One, knowledge is the Two (for it goes in a single line to one point), opinion is the number of the plane, and sensation is the number of the solid. For numbers were said to be the Forms themselves and the principles, and they are composed of the elements. Things are judged either by mind, or by knowledge, or by opinion, or by sensation, and these numbers are the forms of things.

Since the soul was taken to be both productive of motion and capable of knowing, some thinkers have woven the two together and declared the soul to be a self-moving number. But they differ about what and how many the principles are, especially those who make them corporeal versus those who make them incorporeal — and both of these differ from those who mix the two and declare the principles to come from both. They also differ about how many principles there are: some say one, some several. And they fashion their account of the soul to match. For they have assumed, not unreasonably, that what is by nature productive of movement is among the most fundamental things. Hence some believed the soul to be fire — for fire is the finest-grained and most nearly incorporeal of the elements, and furthermore it is both primarily in motion itself and sets other things in motion. Democritus has expressed the reason for each of these views with particular elegance. Soul is the same as mind, he says, and mind consists of the primary, indivisible bodies, and is productive of motion by virtue of the fineness and shape of its particles. Of shapes, the spherical is the most mobile, and this is what both mind and fire are.

Anaxagoras seems to say something different about soul and mind, as we mentioned before, but he treats them in practice as a single nature, except that he makes mind above all the principle of all things. At any rate, he says mind alone of the things that are is simple, unmixed, and pure. But he assigns both functions — knowing and moving — to the same principle, in saying that mind set the whole in motion.

Thales too, from what is reported about him, seems to have supposed the soul to be something productive of motion, if he said that the magnetic stone has a soul because it moves iron. Diogenes, like some others, identified soul with air, believing air to be the finest-grained of all things and a first principle. This is why the soul knows and produces motion: it knows insofar as air is primary and the other things are derived from it, and it produces motion insofar as it is the finest-grained thing. Heraclitus too says the soul is a principle, if it is the exhalation from which, he says, the other things are constituted. Also, the exhalation is the most incorporeal thing and is always flowing. And what is in motion is known by what is in motion. He, like most others, supposed that the things that are are in motion.

Alcmaeon seems to have had a similar conception of the soul. He says it is immortal because it resembles the immortal things, and that this characteristic belongs to it by virtue of its being always in motion. For all divine things are in perpetual continuous motion — moon, sun, the stars, and the whole heaven. Some of the cruder thinkers, such as Hippo, even declared the soul to be water, having been persuaded of this, it seems, from the fact that the seed of all things is moist. For Hippo refuted those who say the soul is blood by pointing out that the seed is not blood, and that this seed is the primary soul. Others, like Critias, said the soul is blood, taking perception to be the most characteristic feature of the soul and holding that perceiving belongs to the soul by virtue of the nature of blood.

So then, all the elements have found their champion among these thinkers, with the exception of earth. No one has proposed earth as the soul, except someone who has said the soul is composed of all the elements, or is all the elements. Practically all define the soul by three marks: motion, perception, and incorporeality. Each of these is traced back to the first principles. Hence those who define the soul by knowledge also make it either an element or derived from the elements, speaking in broad agreement with one another — with one exception. For they say that like is known by like, and since the soul knows all things, they compose it out of all the principles. Those who posit a single cause and a single element make the soul one thing as well — for instance fire or air. But those who posit many principles also make the soul many. Anaxagoras alone says mind is impassible and has nothing in common with any of the other things. But how and by what cause it will know, if it is of this nature, he has not explained, nor is the answer clear from what he says. Those who set up pairs of opposites among the principles compose the soul out of the opposites too. And those who take only one of each pair — hot or cold, or something of the sort — correspondingly make the soul one of these. This is why they also pursue the meanings of names: those who say the soul is the hot say that this is why "to live" is so called, from "to boil"; those who say it is the cold say the soul is called *psychê* from "cooling" by means of respiration.

These, then, are the views that have been handed down about the soul, and the grounds on which they are held.

### **Chapter 3**

We must examine first the question of movement. For perhaps it is not only false that the being of the soul is the sort of thing its advocates describe — that it is what moves itself or is capable of moving itself — but it may be one of the things that are impossible: that movement should belong to the soul at all. Now it has been said earlier that it is not necessary for what produces motion to be itself in motion. Everything that is in motion is moved in one of two ways: either by something else, or by itself. By "by something else" we mean the way things are moved that are in motion because they are in something that is in motion — as sailors are, for though they are not moved in the same way as the ship (since the ship is moved by itself but the sailors because they are in a moving thing). This is clear if we consider their limbs: the proper motion of the feet is walking, and this is also a motion of human beings, but it does not belong to the sailors at that time. Since "being moved" is said in two ways, let us now consider whether the soul is moved by itself, and whether it participates in motion.

Since there are four kinds of motion — locomotion, alteration, decrease, and increase — the soul would be moved either by one of these or by several or by all. And if it is moved not incidentally, then motion would belong to it by nature, and if motion belongs to it by nature, then place does as well — for all these motions are in a place. But if the being of the soul is to move itself, its being moved would not be incidental to it, as it is for a thing that is white or three cubits tall: these too are moved, but incidentally, since it is the body they belong to that is moved. That is why they have no place of their own. But the soul will have a place, if it naturally shares in motion.

Moreover, if the soul moves itself by nature, then it could also be moved by force; and if by force, then also by nature. The same holds for rest: the thing to which a thing naturally moves, there it also naturally rests; and similarly, the thing to which a thing moves by force, there it also rests by force. But what the forced motions and forced rests of the soul might be is not easy to give an account of, even for those who wish to fabricate one.

Further, if the soul moves upward, it will be fire; if downward, earth. For these are the motions of these bodies. And the same argument applies to the intermediate states. Again, since the soul is observed to move the body, it is reasonable that the soul produces the same motions that it itself is moved by. And conversely, if this is so, then it is also true to say that the motion the body is moved by is the same motion the soul is moved by. But the body is moved by locomotion, so the soul too would change in respect of place, shifting about either as a whole or in its parts. And if this is possible, then it would also be possible for the soul to go out of the body and re-enter it, and from this it would follow that dead animals could come back to life.

But the soul could be moved incidentally by something else — for an animal can be pushed by force. But a thing that is moved by itself essentially need not be moved by something else, except incidentally, just as what is good in itself or on its own account need not be good for the sake of something else. One might say that if anything moves the soul, it is the objects of perception that move it most of all. But also, if the soul does move itself, then the soul itself would be moved, so that if every motion is a displacement of the thing moved qua moved, the soul would be displaced from its own nature — if its motion is not incidental to it, but motion belongs to it in its own right and by its own nature.

Some say that the soul moves the body it is in, in the way that it itself is moved. Such was the view of Democritus, who spoke rather like Philippus the comic poet, who said that Daedalus made the wooden Aphrodite move by pouring quicksilver into it. Democritus speaks similarly: he says the indivisible spheres are in motion because it is their nature never to stand still, and they pull along and move the whole body with them. We shall ask whether it is by the same means that the soul also produces rest. How it would do so by means of atoms is difficult or impossible to say. And in general, it does not appear that the soul moves the animal in this way, but rather through some kind of choice and thought.

In the same way, the *Timaeus* too gives a physical account of how the soul moves the body: by being interwoven with the body, the soul moves it and consequently moves itself as well. Having composed the soul from the elements and divided it according to the harmonic numbers, so that it would have an innate perception of harmony and the universe would move in harmonious revolutions, Plato bent the straight line into a circle, and then divided the one circle into two circles joined at two points, and again divided one of these into seven circles — the idea being that the motions of the soul are the revolutions of the heavens.

But in the first place, it is not right to say the soul is a magnitude. For the soul of "the whole" is clearly meant by Plato to be of the same kind as what is called mind or intellect — not, that is, like the perceptive soul or the desiring soul, since the motion of these is not revolution. But mind is one and continuous in the way that thinking is, and thinking is identical with the thoughts that compose it, and these are one in succession, like a number, and not like a magnitude. For this reason, mind too is not continuous in that way, but is either without parts or continuous in some way other than the way a magnitude is continuous. For how would it think if it were a magnitude? Would it think with any one of its parts? And would this part be a part in the sense of a magnitude, or in the sense of a point — if one should call a point a part? If in the sense of a point, and points are infinite in number, it is clear that mind would never traverse them. If in the sense of a magnitude, mind would think the same thing many times over — indeed, infinitely many times. But it seems possible to think a thing once. And if it is sufficient for mind to make contact with its object by any one of its parts, what need is there for it to move at all, let alone to move in a circle? If, on the other hand, thinking requires contact of the whole mind with its object, what is the meaning of the contact of the parts? Further, how will what has parts think what has no parts, or what has no parts think what has parts? But mind must be this circle, for it is the revolution of a circle that is thinking, and if the revolution is thinking, then the circle whose revolution constitutes that thinking must be mind.

And what will mind always be thinking? For it must think something always, if the revolution is eternal. Practical kinds of thinking have limits — for all practical thought is for the sake of something other than itself — and theoretical acts of thinking are similarly limited by their accounts. Every account is either a definition or a demonstration. Demonstrations both proceed from a starting point and have in a way an end — namely, the conclusion or what is demonstrated. Even if they do not come to an end, they at least do not circle back to the beginning; instead they proceed by always taking a further middle or extreme term and go forward in a straight line. But revolution goes back to the beginning. And all definitions are finite. Moreover, if the same revolution recurs many times, mind will have to think the same thing many times. Further, thinking seems to be more like a kind of coming to rest and stopping than like a motion. And the same applies to the syllogism.

But further, what is not easy but forced cannot be blessed. And if the motion of the soul is not its essence, the soul would be moved contrary to its nature. It must also be troublesome to be mixed with the body and unable to escape — indeed, it is something to be avoided, if it is in fact better for mind not to be together with body, as is commonly said and as many agree.

The cause of the heaven's revolving is also left unclear. For it is not the essence of the soul that is the cause of the revolving motion — the soul moves in this way only incidentally — nor is the body the cause, but rather the soul would be. And it is not even stated that such motion is better. Yet the reason why god made the soul revolve ought to be that it is better for the soul to move than to be at rest, and to move in this way rather than in some other. But since this kind of investigation belongs rather to other discussions, let us set it aside for now.

An absurd consequence befalls this account and most accounts about soul: they attach the soul to a body and place it in a body without additionally determining the reason why or the condition of the body. Yet this would seem to be necessary. For it is because of their association that the one acts and the other is acted upon, that the one moves and the other is moved, and none of these reciprocal relations holds between any random things. But these thinkers try only to say what sort of thing the soul is, while about the body that receives it they add no further determination — as if it were possible, in accordance with the Pythagorean stories, for any random soul to clothe itself in any random body. For each body seems to have its own distinctive form and shape. What they say is rather like saying that carpentry can clothe itself in flutes. For the craft must use its tools, and the soul must use its body.

### **Chapter 4**

Yet another opinion about the soul has been handed down. Some say the soul is a harmony. For a harmony, they say, is a blending and composition of opposites, and the body is composed of opposites. But a harmony is a certain ratio or composition of the things mixed, and the soul cannot be either of these. Moreover, producing movement does not belong to a harmony, but practically everyone assigns this to the soul above all. It is more fitting to say "health" is a harmony than to say the soul is, and in general the bodily excellences, as will become quite clear if one tries to assign the affections and functions of the soul to some harmony. For it is difficult to fit them together. Further, if we use "harmony" in its most proper sense, there are two uses: most properly, it is the composition of magnitudes that have motion and position when they fit together so well that nothing of the same kind can be admitted beside them; and derivatively, it is the ratio of the things mixed. In neither of these senses is it reasonable to speak of the soul as a harmony. That the soul is a composition of the parts of the body can be examined quite easily. For the compositions of the parts are many and various: of what and how is mind a composition? And of what is the perceptive or desiring part a composition? It is equally absurd to identify the soul with the ratio of the mixture. For the mixture of the elements is not in the same ratio for flesh as for bone. It would follow, then, that one has many souls throughout the whole body, if all the bodily parts are composed of the elements mixed in a certain ratio and the ratio of the mixture is harmony and soul.

One might also demand of Empedocles: since he says each of the parts exists by some ratio, is the soul the ratio, or is it rather something different that comes to be in the parts? And further, is Love the cause of any random mixture, or only of the one that is according to the right ratio? And is Love itself the ratio, or something beyond the ratio? These questions, then, raise such puzzles. But if the soul is different from the mixture, why is it that when the flesh and the other parts of the animal are destroyed, the soul too is destroyed? And in addition, if each of the parts does not have a soul — given that the soul is not the ratio of the mixture — what is it that perishes when the soul departs?

From what has been said, it is clear that the soul cannot be a harmony, nor can it revolve in a circle. It is possible, however, as we have said, for the soul to be moved incidentally, and even to move itself in a sense — namely, the thing the soul is in can be moved, and it can be moved by the soul. But there is no other way in which the soul could be moved in place.

One might more reasonably raise puzzles about the soul's being moved if one considers the following. We say the soul is pained and pleased, is confident and afraid, and again that it is angry and perceives and thinks. All of these seem to be motions. One might therefore suppose the soul is moved. But this does not follow necessarily. For even if being pained and being pleased and thinking are very much motions — that is, cases of being moved — and each of them is a being-moved, still the being moved is brought about by the soul — as for instance, being angry or fearing is the heart's being moved in such-and-such a way, while thinking is perhaps either this or something else being so moved. Some of these come about by locomotion of certain parts, others by alteration — which parts and how is another discussion. But to say that the soul is angry is as if one were to say that the soul weaves or builds. For it is perhaps better to say not that the soul pities or learns or thinks, but that the human being does these things with or by means of the soul. And this not because the motion is in the soul, but because the motion sometimes extends to the soul and sometimes starts from it — perception, for instance, coming from the objects and proceeding to the soul, while recollection starts from the soul and extends to the motions or traces in the sense-organs.

Mind, however, seems to come to be in us as a kind of substance and not to be destroyed. For it would be destroyed, if at all, by the feebleness of old age, but in fact what happens is much the same as what happens with the sense-organs. For if an old person could obtain the right kind of eye, he would see just as well as the young person. So old age is not due to the soul's having been affected in some way, but to the body that contains it having been affected — just as in drunkenness and disease. Thinking and contemplating decay because something else within is being destroyed, but the intellect itself is impassible. Thinking, loving, and hating are not affections of mind but of the thing that has it, insofar as it has it. That is also why, when this thing perishes, the mind neither remembers nor loves. For these did not belong to the mind, but to the composite thing that has perished. But mind is surely something more divine and is impassible.

That the soul cannot be moved is evident from these considerations. And if it is not moved at all, it is clear that it cannot be moved by itself.

### **Chapter 5**

By far the most unreasonable of the views put forward is the claim that the soul is a self-moving number. For those who maintain it, there follow impossibilities both from its being moved, as has been said, and specifically from their calling it a number. For how is one to think of a unit that is moved? By what agency, and in what manner, if it is without parts and undifferentiated? For if it is both productive of motion and capable of being moved, it must have differentiation. Further, since they say a line in motion produces a surface, and a point in motion produces a line, the motions of the units too will be lines. For a point is a unit having position, and the number of the soul is already somewhere and has position. Again, if one subtracts a number or a unit from a number, a different number remains. But plants, and many animals, go on living when divided, and each part seems to have the same kind of soul.

It would seem to make no difference whether we speak of units or tiny corpuscles. For even if Democritus's spherical atoms were to become points, with only quantity remaining, there will still be within the collection something that moves and something that is moved, just as there is in a continuum. For what happens does not depend on a difference in size — whether large or small — but on the fact that the thing in question has quantity. That is why there must be something to move the units. But if, in the animal, what produces motion is the soul, then it is so in the number as well, so that the soul is not both the mover and the thing moved, but the mover only. But how is it possible for this to be a unit? For it must have some difference from the other units. But what difference can a point-unit have other than position? If, then, the units in the body are different from the points in the body, the units will be in the same place as the points, for each will occupy the space of a point. Yet if two things can be in the same place, why not infinitely many? For things whose place is indivisible are themselves indivisible. But if the points in the body are the number of the soul — or if the number of the soul is the points in the body — why do not all bodies have souls? For there seem to be points in all bodies, and infinitely many of them.

Further, how is it possible for the units to be separated and released from the bodies, if lines cannot be divided into points? The result, as we said, is in one respect the same as the view that makes the soul consist of fine-bodied particles. For there is the peculiar absurdity that attaches to saying, in the manner of Democritus, that the soul moves the body — for if the soul is in the whole body that perceives, and if the soul is a kind of body, then two bodies would be in the same place. And for those who call the soul a number, there would be many points in a single point, or every body would have a soul — unless a number different from the points in the body, a different kind of number, comes to be in it. It would also follow that the animal is moved by number, in the way that we said Democritus moved it. For what difference does it make whether one speaks of small spheres or large units, or in general of units in motion? In either case, it is necessary that these should move the animal by being moved.

Three ways of defining the soul have been handed down: some declared it to be the most movement-producing thing by virtue of its moving itself; others, that it is the finest-bodied or the most incorporeal of all things; and still others, that it is composed of the elements. We have gone through roughly all the difficulties and objections these views involve. It remains to examine the claim that the soul is composed of the elements. They say this so that the soul may perceive and know each of the things that are. But many impossible consequences necessarily follow. For they posit that like is known by like, as though thereby positing that the soul is identical with the things it knows. But the elements are not the only things there are: there are many other things as well — or rather, the things composed of the elements are perhaps unlimited in number. Let us grant, then, that the soul knows and perceives the things of which each element is a constituent. But by what will it know or perceive the composite whole — for instance, what god is, or a human being, or flesh, or bone? And similarly for any other composite thing. For each of these is not merely its elements in any random arrangement, but in a certain ratio and mode of composition, as Empedocles says of bone: "The kindly earth in her broad-bosomed moulds received two parts of gleaming Water out of eight, and four of Fire; and white bones were formed." It is of no use, then, for the elements to be in the soul, unless the ratios and the modes of composition are present as well. For each element will know its like, but nothing will know bone or a human being, unless these too are in the soul. And that this is impossible hardly needs to be said. For who would puzzle over whether a stone or a human being is in the soul? The same holds for the good and for what is not good. And similarly for everything else.

Further, since "being" is said in many ways — for it signifies a "this something," or a quantity, or a quality, or any of the other categories that have been distinguished — will the soul be composed of all of them, or not? But there do not seem to be elements common to all the categories. Is the soul composed only of those elements that belong to substance? If so, how does it know each of the others as well? Or will they say that each genus has its own proper elements and principles, and that the soul is composed of all of these? Then the soul will be both a quantity and a quality and a substance. But it is impossible for what is composed of the elements of quantity to be a substance and not a quantity. These and other similar consequences follow for those who say the soul is composed of all the elements.

It is absurd, too, to say both that like is impassible to like and that like perceives like or that like knows like. But they do assign perceiving as a kind of being-affected and being-moved, and likewise for thinking and knowing. That there are many difficulties and puzzles involved in saying, as Empedocles does, that each thing is known by means of its corporeal elements and by reference to what is like in the soul — the further evidence we are now considering also confirms. For whatever parts of an animal's body consist simply of earth — bones, sinews, hair — seem to perceive nothing at all, and therefore not even their like. And yet they ought to perceive their like if the principle holds. Again, each of the principles will have more ignorance than knowledge. For each will know one thing but be ignorant of many, since it will be ignorant of all the other elements.

In Empedocles's case at least, it follows also that god is the least intelligent being. For god alone will not know one of the elements — strife — while mortal beings, being composed of all the elements, will know all of them. And in general, why do not all beings have a soul, since each thing is either an element or composed of one or more or all the elements? Each would have to know some one or some several or all things. One might also raise the problem of what it is that unifies the soul. For the soul is like the elements as matter, and what holds them together is the most important factor, whatever it might be. But it is impossible for there to be something superior to the soul that rules over it — and still more impossible for there to be something superior to mind. For it is reasonable that mind is the most primordial and authoritative thing by nature, yet they say the elements are the primary beings.

None of those who say the soul is composed of the elements — on the grounds that it knows and perceives the things that are — and none of those who say the soul is the most movement-producing thing, are speaking about every kind of soul. For not all things that perceive are capable of locomotion. Some animals evidently remain fixed in place. Yet this seems to be the only motion that the soul imparts to the animal, according to those thinkers. Similarly, those who construct mind and the perceptive faculty out of the elements do not speak about every kind of soul. For plants seem to live without sharing in locomotion or perception, and many animals lack thought. But even if one were to set these cases aside and accept that mind is a part of the soul, and likewise the perceptive part, even so they would not be speaking about the whole of soul nor about any whole soul.

The same objection also applies to the account given in the so-called Orphic verses. For they say the soul enters from the whole universe in the course of breathing, being carried by the winds. Now this cannot happen in the case of plants, nor even in the case of some animals, if not all of them breathe — a fact that has escaped the notice of the proponents of this view. Even if we should concede that the soul must be composed of the elements, there is no need for it to be composed of all of them: one member of each pair of contraries is sufficient to judge both itself and its opposite. For by the straight we know both the straight and the curved, since the ruler is a judge of both, whereas the curved is a judge neither of itself nor of the straight.

Some also say that the soul is mixed in the whole universe, which is perhaps why Thales thought all things are full of gods. But this view raises certain puzzles. For why does the soul, when it is in air or fire, not produce an animal, but only when it is in the mixtures — and that, though it is supposedly better when it is in the pure elements? One might also ask why the soul in the air is better and more immortal than the soul in animals. Either way the result is absurd and paradoxical. For calling fire or air an animal is among the more paradoxical claims, and not calling them animals when they have a soul in them is absurd. They seem to suppose that soul is in these elements because the whole is of the same kind as its parts. So they are compelled to say that the soul too is of the same kind as its parts — that is, of the same kind as the elements — if the soul comes to be in animals through something of the surrounding medium being taken in. But if the portion of air that is taken in is of the same kind as the whole, while the soul has parts that are unlike, then clearly some part of the soul will be present but another part will not.

It is therefore necessary either that the soul have parts and not be of the same kind throughout, or that it not be present in every part of the whole. From what has been said, it is clear that neither does knowing belong to the soul because it is composed of the elements, nor is it correct or true to say that it is moved. Since knowing, perceiving, opining, and further desiring and wishing and the appetites in general, belong to the soul — and since locomotion is produced in animals by the soul, and further growth, maturity, and decay — does each of these belong to the whole soul, and do we think, perceive, move, and do and undergo each of the rest with the whole soul, or do we do different things with different parts? And does life itself reside in some one of these parts, or in more than one, or in all of them — or does it have some further, different cause? Some say the soul is divisible, and that one part thinks while another desires. What is it, then, that holds the soul together, if it is naturally divisible? It is certainly not the body: on the contrary, the soul seems rather to hold the body together. At any rate, when the soul departs, the body disperses and decays. If, then, some other thing makes the soul one, that thing would most properly be the soul. But then we will have to ask about that thing in turn — whether it is one or has many parts. If it is one, why not say at once that the soul is one? If it has parts, again the argument will demand what holds those parts together, and so on to infinity.

One might also raise a puzzle about the parts of the soul — what power does each have in the body? For if the whole soul holds the whole body together, each part of the soul ought to hold together some part of the body. But this seems impossible: it is hard even to imagine what sort of part the intellect would hold together, or how it would do so. It is also observed that plants continue to live when divided, and certain insects do as well — the implication being that the separated segments have a soul that is the same in kind, even if not the same numerically. For each of the two segments retains perception and locomotion for a time. That they do not continue is not surprising, since they lack the organs needed to maintain their nature. Nonetheless, all the parts of the soul are in each of the two segments — parts that are of the same kind as one another and as the whole soul — the parts being inseparable from one another even if the whole soul is divisible. It seems, too, that the principle in plants is a kind of soul. For this alone is shared by both animals and plants. And this principle is separable from the perceptive principle, but nothing has perception without also having it.

## **Book II**

### **Chapter 1**

The things that have been said about the soul by our predecessors have now been surveyed. Let us return, as though from the beginning, and try to determine what the soul is and what would be its most common account. We say that one genus of the things that are is substance, and that under substance we include, first, matter — that which in itself is not a this something — and, second, the shape or form in virtue of which it is already called a this something, and, third, the composite of the two. Matter is potentiality, while form is actuality — and actuality in two senses, corresponding on the one hand to knowledge as a possessed state, and on the other to the active exercise of knowledge, the actual contemplating.

Bodies seem to be substances above all, and among bodies especially natural bodies, since these are the principles of the others. Of natural bodies, some have life and some do not. By "life" Aristotle means self-nourishment, growth, and decay. So every natural body that shares in life would be a substance — substance in the sense of a composite. But since it is also a body of a definite kind, namely one that has life, the soul would not be the body. For the body is not something said of a subject; it is rather itself a subject and matter. The soul must therefore be substance in the sense of the form of a natural body that has life potentially. But substance in this sense is actuality. The soul, then, is the actuality of a body of this kind.

Actuality, however, is said in two ways: one corresponding to knowledge as a state, and the other to the active exercise of knowledge, the actual contemplating. The soul is actuality in the first sense — actuality as knowledge is. For the presence of the soul is compatible with both sleep and waking, and waking corresponds to the active exercise of knowledge, sleep to possessing knowledge without exercising it. In the order of coming to be in a given individual, knowledge as a state is prior. The soul is therefore the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially. Such a body would be one that is organic — equipped with organs. Even the parts of plants are organs, though extremely simple ones: the leaf is a covering for the pod, and the pod for the fruit; and the roots are analogous to the mouth, since both draw in nourishment.

If, then, something general must be said about every soul, the soul would be the first actuality of a natural organic body. That is why there is no need to inquire whether the soul and the body are one, any more than whether the wax and the shape impressed upon it are one, or in general whether the matter of each thing and that of which it is the matter are one. For although "one" and "being" are said in many ways, what is most properly meant is the actuality.

It has been stated in general terms, then, what the soul is: it is substance in the sense of the account — that is, the what-it-was-to-be of a body of a definite kind. Suppose some tool, such as an axe, were a natural body. Then the being of the axe — what it is to be an axe — would be its soul, and if this were removed, it would no longer be an axe except in name. As things stand, however, it is just an axe, since the soul is not the what-it-was-to-be and the account of this kind of body, but of a natural body of a definite kind that has in itself a source of motion and rest.

One must also consider what has been said in connection with the parts of the body. For if the eye were an animal, sight would be its soul. For sight is the substance of the eye in the sense of the account — the eye being the matter of sight. And when sight fails, the eye is no longer an eye, except in name — just as an eye of stone or a painted eye is not really an eye. What holds of the part must be applied to the whole living body. For as the particular sense stands to the particular sense-organ, so the whole of perception stands to the whole perceptive body as such. It is not the body that has lost the soul that is potentially alive, but the one that has it. The seed and the fruit are potentially bodies of this sort.

Waking, then, is actuality in the way that cutting and seeing are; the soul is actuality in the way that sight and the capacity of the tool are. The body is what is in potentiality. And just as the eye is the pupil together with sight, so the living thing is the soul together with the body. That the soul is not separable from the body — or that certain parts of it are not, if it naturally has parts — is not unclear. For in some cases the actuality is the actuality of the parts themselves. Not that anything prevents at least some parts from being separable, on account of their not being the actuality of any body. Further, it is unclear whether the soul is the actuality of the body in the way the sailor is of the ship.

Let this serve as a sketch and outline of what the soul is.

### **Chapter 2**

Since what is clear and more knowable by reason emerges from what is unclear but more obvious to us, we must try to approach the soul afresh in this way. For the defining account should not merely show the fact, as most definitions do, but should contain and display the cause. As things stand, the statements of definitions are like conclusions. For example, what is squaring? The construction of an equilateral rectangle equal in area to a given oblong. But such a definition is a statement of the conclusion. The one who says that squaring is the finding of a mean proportional states the cause of the thing.

We say, then, making a beginning of the investigation, that the ensouled is distinguished from the unensouled by living. And "living" is said in many ways — and even if only one of the following belongs to a thing, we say it is alive: intellect, perception, locomotion and rest, and further the motion involved in nourishment and decay and growth. This is why all plants, too, are thought to be alive. For they evidently have in themselves a capacity and a principle through which they grow and decay in opposite directions. For they do not grow upward without growing downward, but they grow equally in both directions — indeed, in all directions — and they do this as long as they are able to receive nourishment, and they continue to live as long as they can do this. This capacity can be separated from the others, but the others cannot be separated from it, in the case of mortal beings. This is evident in plants, for no other capacity of the soul belongs to them.

It is, then, on account of this principle that living belongs to living things. But it is on account of perception that they are first called animals. For things that do not move and do not change their place, but that do have perception, we call animals and not merely living things. Of the senses, touch belongs first of all to all animals. And just as the nutritive capacity can be separated from touch and from all perception, so touch can exist apart from the other senses. By "nutritive" we mean the part of the soul in which even plants share. All animals are observed to have the sense of touch. Why each of these is so will be stated later. For now, let it suffice to say that the soul is the principle of these capacities and is defined by them — by the nutritive, the perceptive, the thinking, and by motion.

Whether each of these is a soul or a part of a soul — and if a part, whether the kind of part that is separable only in account or also separable in place — is in some cases not hard to see, though some cases raise difficulties. For just as in the case of plants, some parts clearly continue to live when divided and separated from each other — the soul in such plants being one in actuality in each whole plant but many in potentiality — so we see the same happening with other varieties of soul in the case of insects that have been cut in two. For each of the segments has perception and locomotion. And if they have perception, they also have imagination and appetite. For where there is perception, there is also pain and pleasure, and where these are, there is necessarily also desire.

About the intellect and the contemplative capacity, nothing is yet clear — but it seems to be a different genus of soul, and it alone is capable of being separated from the body, as the eternal from the perishable. It is clear from these things that the remaining parts of the soul are not separable, as some maintain — though it is plain that they are different in account. For the being of the perceptive and the being of the opinative are different, if perceiving and opining are indeed different; and the same holds for each of the other capacities mentioned.

Further, some animals have all of these, others have some of them, and still others have only one — and this is what produces the differences among animals. What the cause of this is must be investigated later. Very much the same holds for the senses too: some animals have all of them, others have some, and others have only the one that is most necessary — touch.

"That by which we live and perceive" is said in two ways — just as "that by which we know" is said in two ways: in one sense it means knowledge, in another the soul, since we say we know by each of these. Similarly "that by which we are healthy" means in one sense health, in another some part of the body or even the whole body. Of these, knowledge and health are the shape and a kind of form and account and a kind of actuality of what is receptive — in the one case, of what is capable of knowing, in the other, of what is capable of being healed. For the actuality of what is capable of acting seems to come about in the thing that is affected and disposed to receive it. The soul, then, is that by which we primarily live, perceive, and think. So it would be a kind of account and form, not matter and the underlying subject. For substance is said in three ways, as we said: form, matter, and the composite. Of these, matter is potentiality, form actuality. Since the composite of the two is the ensouled thing, the body is not the actuality of the soul, but the soul is the actuality of a certain kind of body. For this reason those who think the soul is neither without body nor a kind of body are right. For it is not a body, but it is something of the body — and that is why it is in a body, and in a body of a definite kind. Not just in any body, as previous thinkers supposed when they fitted the soul into a body without any further specification of what kind of body or what quality of body — even though it is evident that the reception of one thing by another does not happen at random. And it comes about in this way by reason too: the actuality of each thing comes about naturally in what is already that thing in potentiality — that is, in the appropriate matter.

From all of this, then, it is clear that the soul is a certain actuality and account of that which has the potentiality of being of such-and-such a kind.

### **Chapter 3**

Of the capacities of the soul that have been mentioned, all belong to some living things, as we said, while to others some of them belong, and to still others only one. The capacities we mentioned are the nutritive, the perceptive, the appetitive, the locomotive, and the thinking. Plants have only the nutritive. Other things have both the nutritive and the perceptive. And if they have the perceptive, they also have the appetitive. For appetite comprises desire, spiritedness, and wish. All animals have at least one of the senses — touch. And that which has perception also experiences pleasure and pain, and what is pleasant and painful. And things that have these also have desire, since desire is appetite for the pleasant.

Further, animals have a perception of food — for touch is the sense concerned with food. For all living things are nourished by what is dry and moist and hot and cold, and touch is the sense of these. Animals are nourished by other perceptible qualities only incidentally. For neither sound nor color nor smell contributes anything to nourishment, and flavor is one of the tangible qualities. Hunger and thirst are desires — hunger for the dry and hot, thirst for the cold and moist — and flavor is a kind of seasoning of these. We must clarify these matters later. For now, let it suffice to say that those living things that have touch also have appetite. The situation with regard to imagination is unclear and must be examined later. Some living things have, in addition to these, the locomotive capacity, and others have also the thinking capacity and intellect — for example, human beings, and any other being there may be of a similar or superior kind.

It is clear, then, that soul would have a single account in the same way as figure does. For in the case of figures, there is no figure apart from the triangle and the figures that follow upon it; and similarly in the case of soul, there is no soul apart from the ones mentioned. Even in the case of figures, a common account could be formulated that would fit all of them, but would be proper to none of them in particular. The same holds for the souls mentioned. It is therefore ridiculous, in these cases and in others, to seek a common account that will not be the proper account of any of the things that are, and not to look instead for such a proper account corresponding to the indivisible species — letting go of the common account. The facts about soul are much like the facts about figures. For in both cases, the prior is always present potentially in what succeeds it — as the triangle is in the quadrilateral and the nutritive in the perceptive. One must therefore inquire, case by case, what is the soul of each — of a plant, of a human being, of an animal. Why they are arranged in this successive fashion must be considered. For the perceptive does not exist without the nutritive, yet the nutritive is separated from the perceptive in plants. Again, without touch, none of the other senses exists, yet touch exists without the others — for many animals have neither sight nor hearing nor a sense of smell. And of those that perceive, some have the locomotive capacity and others do not. Last and most rarely, some have reasoning and thought. Those perishable beings that have reasoning have all the other capacities too, but not all of those that have each of the others have reasoning. Some do not even have imagination, while others live by imagination alone. The theoretical intellect requires a separate discussion. That the account of each of these capacities is the most proper account of soul is clear.

### **Chapter 4**

For anyone who intends to carry out an investigation of these capacities, it is necessary to grasp what each of them is, and then to inquire into the next things and the rest. And if one must say what each of them is — for instance, what the thinking part is, or the perceptive part, or the nutritive part — one must first say what thinking is and what perceiving is. For actualities and actions are, in account, prior to potentialities. And if this is so, and if the objects corresponding to the actualities should have been studied before them, then one must for the same reason first determine about the objects in question — food, the perceptible, and the intelligible.

First, then, about nourishment and reproduction. For the nutritive soul belongs to the other living things as well, and is the first and most common capacity of the soul, the one in virtue of which living belongs to all. Its functions are reproduction and the use of food. For the most natural function of any living thing — such as are complete and not deformed, and do not come into being spontaneously — is to produce another like itself, an animal producing an animal, a plant a plant, in order that they may share in the eternal and the divine so far as they can. For that is what all things desire, and it is for the sake of that that they do whatever they do by nature. "That for the sake of which" has two senses: the purpose and the beneficiary. Since, then, living things cannot share in the eternal and the divine by continuous existence, because nothing perishable can persist as the same individual, each shares in it to the extent that it can — some more, some less. What persists is not the individual itself, but something like itself — not one in number, but one in species.

The soul is the cause and principle of the living body. And these terms — cause and principle — are used in many senses. The soul is a cause in three of the ways distinguished. For it is that from which motion originates, it is that for the sake of which the body exists, and it is the substance — the formal cause — of ensouled bodies. That it is substance as the formal cause is clear: the cause of being is, for all things, their substance, and for living things, being is living, and the soul is the cause and principle of this. Moreover, the actuality is the account of what exists potentially.

It is evident, too, that the soul is a cause in the sense of that for the sake of which. For just as mind acts for the sake of something, in the same way nature does too, and this something is its end. In living things, the soul is naturally such an end. For all natural bodies are instruments of the soul, and this is so for the bodies of plants just as much as for those of animals, showing that they exist for the sake of the soul. "That for the sake of which" is of two kinds: purpose and beneficiary.

Moreover, the soul is also that from which locomotion originates — though this capacity does not belong to all living things. Alteration and growth also come about through the soul. For perception seems to be a kind of alteration, and nothing perceives that does not share in soul. The same holds for growth and decay: nothing decays or grows naturally without being nourished, and nothing is nourished that does not share in life.

Empedocles was wrong to add that growth in plants occurs downward because earth naturally moves in that direction, and upward because fire does. For he does not understand "upward" and "downward" correctly. For up and down are not the same for all things and for the universe: the roots of plants correspond to the head of animals, if organs are to be identified as different or the same by their functions. Besides, what is it that holds together the fire and the earth, which move in opposite directions? For they will be torn apart unless there is something to prevent it. And if there is such a thing, it is the soul, and it is the cause of growth and nourishment.

Some think the nature of fire is the cause without qualification of nourishment and growth. For fire appears to be the only body, or the only element, that nourishes itself and grows. One might therefore suppose that it is fire that accomplishes this function in plants and animals as well. But fire is in a way a contributing cause, though not the cause without qualification. Rather, it is the soul that is the cause. For the growth of fire is without limit, so long as there is something combustible, but all things that are naturally constituted have a limit and a ratio both of size and of growth. And this belongs to soul rather than to fire — to the account rather than to the matter.

Since the same capacity of the soul is both nutritive and reproductive, it is necessary first to determine the facts about nourishment. For the nutritive soul is distinguished from the other capacities by this function. It is thought that the contrary is nourished by the contrary — not in every case, but in those cases where the contraries not only come to be from one another but also grow by one another. For many things come to be from each other that are not even quantities — as the healthy comes from the sick. And even those contraries that do grow do not seem to be nourished by each other in the same way: water is nourishment for fire, but fire does not nourish water. It seems, then, that it is especially among the simple bodies that one is the nourishment and the other the thing nourished.

But there is a puzzle here. Some say that like is nourished by like, just as they say like grows by like. But others, as we said, think the opposite — that contrary is nourished by contrary — on the ground that like is impassible to like, and nourishment must change and be digested, and change is always into the opposite or the intermediate. Further, nourishment is affected by what is nourished, but what is nourished is not affected by the nourishment — just as the carpenter is not affected by the matter, but the matter is affected by the carpenter. The carpenter merely changes from inactivity to activity.

It makes a difference whether nourishment is the last thing added or the first. If both, then nourishment would be both — as undigested, it would be contrary nourishing contrary; as digested, like nourishing like. So it is clear that both parties are in a way right and in a way wrong.

Since nothing is nourished that does not share in life, what is nourished would be the ensouled body qua ensouled. Nourishment, then, is relative to the ensouled thing, and not incidentally so. But being nourishment and being productive of growth are different in being. Insofar as the ensouled thing is a quantity, nourishment is productive of growth. But insofar as it is a this-something and a substance, nourishment is nourishment. For the ensouled thing preserves its substance and exists as long as it is nourished, and it is productive of the generation not of the thing that is nourished but of something like the thing that is nourished. For the substance of the thing already exists, and nothing generates itself but only maintains itself. Therefore this principle of the soul is a capacity for maintaining the thing that has it, qua having it, and nourishment prepares it for activity. That is why, if deprived of nourishment, it cannot exist.

Since there are three things — the thing nourished, that by which it is nourished, and what does the nourishing — what does the nourishing is the first soul, what is nourished is the body that has the soul, and that by which it is nourished is the food. Since it is right to name all things by their end, and the end here is to generate another like itself, the first soul would be the soul that is generative of another like itself. "That by which it is nourished" has two senses — just as "that by which one steers" means both the hand and the rudder. The one both moves and is moved, the other is only moved. All nourishment must be capable of being digested, and what produces digestion is heat. That is why every ensouled thing has heat.

What nourishment is has been stated in outline. It must be clarified later in the discussions proper to it.

### **Chapter 5**

Now that these matters have been determined, let us speak in general about all perception. Perception consists in being moved and affected, as has been said, for it is thought to be a kind of alteration. Some also say that like is affected by like. In what way this is possible or impossible has been stated in the general discussions concerning acting and being affected.

There is a puzzle about why we do not have perception of the senses themselves, and why they produce no perception without external objects, even though fire, earth, and the other elements are present in them — elements whose perception, whether in themselves or in their incidental properties, constitutes what the senses perceive. It is clear, then, that the perceptive capacity is not in actuality but only in potentiality. That is why it does not perceive on its own — just as the combustible does not burn on its own without something to set it alight. For if it did, it would burn itself and would have no need of actual fire.

Since "perceiving" is said in two ways — for we say both that something that potentially sees and hears does see and hear, even if it happens to be asleep, and also that something that is actually seeing and hearing sees and hears — "perception" too would be said in two ways: one as potential, the other as actual. Similarly "to perceive" means both the potential and the actual. Let us first proceed as though being affected, being moved, and being in actuality are the same. For motion is a kind of actuality, though an incomplete one, as has been stated elsewhere.

Everything is affected and moved by what is capable of acting and is in actuality. Hence a thing is affected in one sense by what is like it and in another by what is unlike it, as we said. For what is being affected is unlike, but once it has been affected it is like.

We must also make distinctions about potentiality and actuality, since just now we were speaking of them in an unqualified way. A thing can be a knower in one sense in the way that we would say a human being is a knower because a human being belongs to the class of things that know and have knowledge. In another sense, we call a knower someone who already has, say, grammatical knowledge. Each of these has a capacity, but not in the same way: the first has a capacity because his genus and matter are of a certain kind; the second because he is able to contemplate when he wishes, provided nothing external prevents him. And there is a third sense — the person who is already contemplating, who is in actuality and in the fullest sense knowing this particular A. Both of the first two are potentially knowers, but the one becomes a knower in actuality by being altered through learning and by frequent changes from the contrary state, while the other — the one who has arithmetical or grammatical knowledge but is not exercising it — passes to being in actuality in a different way.

Being affected is not a single thing either. In one sense, it is a kind of destruction by the contrary. In another sense, it is rather a preservation of what exists potentially by what exists in actuality and is like it, in the way that potentiality stands to actuality. For the one who has knowledge comes to contemplate, and this is either not a case of being altered — since the development is into itself and into actuality — or it is a different genus of alteration. That is why it is not right to say that the thinker is "altered" when he thinks, any more than the builder is altered when he builds. What leads from potential to actual knowing and thinking should not, then, be called "teaching" without qualification, but should have some other name. And the one who starts from potential knowledge and learns and acquires knowledge from the teacher who is actually knowing should either not be said to be "affected" at all, or we should say there are two kinds of alteration — one a change to conditions of privation, the other a change to a thing's positive states and its nature.

The first change in the perceiver is produced by the parent, and at birth the perceiver already has perception in the way that the first sense of knowledge was described. Actual perception corresponds to the exercise of knowledge. But there is a difference: in the case of perception, the things that produce the actuality are external — the visible, the audible, and so on for the other senses. The reason is that actual perception is of particulars, while knowledge is of universals, and universals are in a way in the soul itself. That is why thinking is up to the person — one can think whenever one wishes — but perceiving is not up to the person, since the perceptible object must be present. The same holds even for the sciences of perceptible things, and for the same reason — namely, that the perceptible objects are particular and external.

But there will be an opportunity to clarify these matters later. For now, let it suffice to have distinguished that "potentiality" is not said in one way only, but in one sense as when we say a child is potentially a general, and in another as when we say the adult is potentially a general. The same holds for the perceptive capacity. Since the difference has no name — though it has been determined that the two are different and in what way they are different — we must use "being affected" and "being altered" as if they were the proper names. The perceptive faculty is potentially what the perceptible object already is actually, as has been said. It is affected, then, insofar as it is not like the object, but once it has been affected, it has been made like and is such as the object is.

### **Chapter 6**

Concerning each of the senses, we must speak first of the perceptible objects. "Perceptible" is said in three ways. Two of these we call perceptible in themselves, and one incidentally. Of the first two, one is proper to each sense, the other common to all. By "proper" I mean what cannot be perceived by any other sense, and about which it is impossible to be deceived — as sight is of color, hearing of sound, and taste of flavor. Touch indeed has many varieties of object. But each sense judges about its proper objects and is not deceived about the fact that there is a color or a sound, but only about what or where the colored thing is, or what or where the sounding thing is. These, then, are called proper to each sense.

Common perceptibles are motion, rest, number, shape, and magnitude. These are not proper to any one sense but are common to all — for a certain movement is perceptible both by touch and by sight.

Something is said to be "incidentally perceptible" when, for instance, the white thing is the son of Diares. This is perceived incidentally, because the quality of being the son of Diares is incidental to the white thing that is perceived. That is why the perceiver is not affected at all by the perceptible object as such — that is, insofar as it is incidentally perceptible. Of the perceptibles that are in themselves perceptible, the proper perceptibles are perceptible in the primary sense, and it is to these that the substance of each sense is naturally relative.

### **Chapter 7**

The object of sight is the visible. The visible is, first, color, and second, something that can be described in words but happens to have no name — what it is will become clear as we proceed. The visible, then, is color. And color is what lies upon what is visible in itself — and "in itself" here means not in the account of the thing but because the thing has in itself the cause of its being visible. Every color is capable of setting in motion what is actually transparent, and this is its nature. That is why color is not visible without light, and the color of each thing is always seen in light. Hence we must first say what light is.

There is, then, something transparent. By "transparent" I mean what is visible not in itself, strictly speaking, but on account of the color of something else. Of this kind are air, water, and many solid things. It is not qua water or qua air that these are transparent, but because there is a certain nature in them that is the same as that in the eternal upper body. Light is the actuality of this — of the transparent qua transparent. Where this nature is present potentially, there darkness is also present. Light is, as it were, the color of the transparent, when the transparent is made actually transparent by fire or by something of the kind of the upper body — for this too has something that is one and the same as fire.

What the transparent is and what light is has been stated — that it is neither fire nor body at all nor an effluence from any body (for even then it would be a kind of body), but the presence of fire or something of that kind in the transparent. For two bodies cannot be in the same place at the same time. Light is thought to be the contrary of darkness. But darkness is the privation of this condition from the transparent, so that evidently the presence of this condition is light. Empedocles — and whoever else said the same — was wrong to say that light travels and at some time extends between the earth and what surrounds it, but escapes our notice. For this is contrary both to the evidence of reason and to the observed facts. Over a small distance it might escape notice, but that it should do so from east to west is too great a claim.

What is colorless is receptive of color, and what is soundless is receptive of sound. The colorless is the transparent and the invisible — or the barely visible, which is what "dark" seems to be. The transparent is indeed of this kind, though not when it is actually transparent, but when it is potentially so. For the same nature is sometimes darkness and sometimes light.

Not all things are visible in light. Rather, only the proper color of each thing is visible in light. For some things are not seen in light but produce perception in darkness — for example, things that appear fiery and shining (there is no single name for these), such as fungus, horn, the heads, scales, and eyes of fish. But the proper color of none of these is seen in darkness. Why these are seen must be discussed elsewhere. For now, this much is clear: what is seen in light is color. That is why color is not seen without light. For this was just what it was to be a color — to be capable of setting in motion what is actually transparent. And the actuality of the transparent is light. Evidence of this is clear: if one puts the colored thing right on the eye, it will not be seen. Rather, the color sets the transparent in motion — for instance, the air — and the sense-organ is set in motion by the air, which is continuous with it.

Democritus is wrong to think that if the intervening medium became void, one could see distinctly even an ant in the heavens. For this is impossible. Seeing occurs when the perceptive part is affected in some way. It is impossible for it to be affected by the color itself that is seen. It remains, then, that it is affected by the intervening medium, so that some medium must exist. If the intervening space were void, not only would seeing not be distinct — nothing would be seen at all.

The reason why color must be seen in light has been stated. Fire, on the other hand, is seen in both — in darkness and in light — and necessarily so, since it is through fire that the transparent becomes transparent.

The same account holds for sound and smell. Nothing produces perception by touching the sense-organ directly — rather, the medium is set in motion by sound and smell, and the sense-organ is set in motion by the medium. When someone places the sounding or smelling thing directly on the sense-organ, it produces no perception. The same holds for touch and taste, though it does not seem to — the reason why will be clear later.

The medium for sound is air, that for smell has no name. For there is a certain common affection in air and water — something present in both that stands to what has smell as the transparent stands to color. For aquatic animals too seem to have a sense of smell. But a human being and any other land animal that breathes cannot smell without breathing. The cause of this, too, must be discussed later.

### **Chapter 8**

Now let us first make some distinctions about sound and hearing. Sound is of two kinds: one in actuality, the other in potentiality. For some things we say do not have a sound, like sponge and wool, while others we say do, like bronze and things that are solid and smooth — because they are able to produce a sound, that is, to produce an actual sound in the medium between the object and the organ of hearing.

Actual sound is always produced by something, against something, and in something. For it is a blow that produces it. That is why it is impossible for a sound to be produced when there is only one thing — for the striker and the thing struck are different. So what sounds does so against something, and sound does not occur without locomotion. But, as we said, it is not the blow of just any random things that constitutes sound. For wool makes no sound when struck, but bronze does, and things that are smooth and hollow — bronze because it is smooth, and hollow things because by reverberation they produce many blows after the first, since what is set in motion cannot get out.

Further, sound is heard in air, and also in water, though less well. But it is not air or water that is principally responsible for sound. Rather, solid things must strike against each other and against the air. This happens when the air that is struck stands firm and does not disperse. That is why, if the blow is quick and forceful, it makes a sound. For the motion of the striker must outrun the dispersal of the air — just as one could strike a pile or a swirl of sand as it is being carried along rapidly.

An echo occurs when, the air having been made one and bounded by the vessel that contains it and prevents it from dispersing, the air bounces back like a ball. It seems that an echo always occurs, though not always clearly, since what happens with sound is the same as what happens with light. For light is always reflected — otherwise there would not be light everywhere, but darkness outside the area directly illuminated by the sun — but it is not reflected in the way it is reflected from water, bronze, or any other smooth surface, so as to produce a shadow, by which we define light.

Void is rightly said to be the principal condition of hearing. For air seems to be void, and it is air that produces hearing — when it is set in motion as one continuous mass. But because air breaks up easily, it produces no sound unless the thing struck is smooth. In that case, the air becomes one simultaneously on account of the surface — for the surface of a smooth thing is one.

That, then, is what produces sound — what is capable of setting in motion a single mass of air continuously all the way to the organ of hearing. The organ of hearing has air naturally attached to it. And because it is in air, when the outside air is moved, the air inside is moved too. That is why the animal does not hear with every part of its body, and the air does not pass through every part. For it is not every part that has the air that is going to be moved and that is ensouled. Air itself is soundless, because it breaks up easily. But when it is prevented from breaking up, its motion constitutes sound. The air in the ears is walled in so as to be immovable, in order that the animal may perceive accurately all the variations of the motion.

That is also why we hear in water too — because the water does not penetrate to the air that is naturally contained in the ear, nor even into the ear at all, because of its convolutions. When this does happen, one does not hear — nor does one hear when the eardrum is damaged, just as one does not see when the membrane over the pupil is damaged. An indication of whether one hears or not is whether the ear is always resonating, like a horn. For the air in the ear is always moving with a motion of its own. But sound is something external and not the ear's own. That is why they say we hear by means of what is void and resonant — because we hear by means of what has air enclosed in it.

Is it the striker or the thing struck that produces the sound? Or both, but in different ways? For sound is a motion of what is capable of being moved in the way that things are that bounce off smooth surfaces when someone strikes them. Not everything that strikes or is struck produces a sound, as we said. For if one thing strikes another that has no resistance, no sound is produced — for instance, a needle striking a needle. Rather, the thing struck must be smooth, so that the air can bounce off and vibrate as one mass.

The differences between things that sound are made clear in actual sound. For just as without light colors are not seen, so without sound the sharp and the flat are not heard. These terms — sharp and flat — are transferred from the sense of touch. What is sharp in sound moves the sense a great deal in a short time, and what is flat moves it a little in a long time. Not that the sharp is fast and the flat slow, but the one motion is what it is because of its speed, the other because of its slowness. There seems to be an analogy with what is sharp and blunt in the case of touch. For the sharp stabs, as it were, while the blunt pushes — because the one produces its effect in a short time, the other in a long time. And so the one turns out to be fast and the other slow.

Let these distinctions suffice concerning sound. Voice is a certain sound produced by something ensouled. Nothing without a soul has a voice, though some are said by a likeness to have a voice — such as the flute and the lyre, and anything else among soulless things that has range, melody, and articulation. The likeness is apt, since voice too has these features. Many animals do not have a voice — for instance, the bloodless ones, and among the blooded ones, fish. And this is reasonable, if sound is in fact a motion of air. Those fish that are said to have a voice — such as those in the river Achelous — make a sound with their gills or some other such part.

Voice is a sound made by an animal, and not with just any part of its body. Since everything that sounds does so by something striking something else in something — namely, air — it is reasonable that only those things have a voice that take in air. For nature uses the air that is breathed in for two functions — just as it uses the tongue for both tasting and articulation, and of these, taste is necessary (which is why it belongs to more animals) while the ability to express meaning is for the sake of well-being — so nature uses breath both for inner warmth, as something necessary (the cause of this will be discussed elsewhere), and for voice, so that there may be well-being. The organ of breathing is the windpipe, and the part for the sake of which this exists is the lung. For it is by means of this part that land animals have more heat than other animals. The area around the heart is the first to need breath. That is why the air must be taken in when one breathes.

So voice is the striking of the air breathed in against the so-called windpipe, by the agency of the soul in these parts. For not every sound made by an animal is voice, as we said — for it is possible to make a sound with the tongue, or as in coughing. Rather, what does the striking must be ensouled and must do so with a certain kind of imagination. For voice is a significant sound, and not merely a sound of air that is breathed in, as a cough is. Rather, with this air the animal strikes the air in the windpipe against the windpipe itself. An indication of this is that one cannot produce voice while breathing in or breathing out, but only while holding one's breath. For the person holding their breath produces the motion with this air.

It is also clear why fish are voiceless. They have no windpipe. And they lack this part because they do not take in air and do not breathe. The reason for this belongs to another discussion.

### **Chapter 9**

Concerning smell and the object of smell, it is harder to give a precise determination than for the objects we have already discussed. For what sort of thing odor is, is not as clear as what sound or color is. The reason is that this sense is not precise in us, but is inferior to that of many animals. For a human being smells poorly, and perceives none of the objects of smell without the accompaniment of pain or pleasure, which shows that the sense-organ is not precise.

It is reasonable that hard-eyed animals perceive colors in the same imprecise way, and that differences of color are not clear to them except in terms of what is frightening and what is not. This is how the human race stands with respect to smells. For smell seems to bear an analogy to taste — and the species of flavor correspond to the species of smell — but our sense of taste is more precise because it is a form of touch, and touch is the most precise sense in human beings. For in the other senses humans are inferior to many animals, but in touch they surpass all other animals in precision. That is why the human is the most intelligent of animals. An indication of this is that among human beings themselves, it is by this sense-organ and no other that the naturally gifted differ from the naturally ungifted. For those with hard flesh are dull in intellect, while those with soft flesh are well-endowed.

Just as flavors are sweet and bitter, so are odors. In some things, the odor and the flavor correspond — for instance, the odor is sweet and the flavor is sweet — and in others the correspondence is reversed. Similarly, an odor can be pungent, harsh, acidic, or oily. But because smells are not as clear as their corresponding flavors, as we said, they have taken their names from the flavors by way of a likeness. For sweet odor goes with saffron and honey, pungent with thyme and things of that sort, and the same for the rest. Just as hearing and each of the other senses is concerned with the audible and the inaudible, and sight with the visible and the invisible, so smell is concerned with the odorous and the odorless. "Odorless" applies either to what is incapable of having a smell at all, or to what has a small or feeble one. The same holds for the word "tasteless."

Smelling, too, takes place through a medium — such as air or water. For aquatic animals, both those with and those without blood, seem to perceive smell, just as animals in the air do. For some of these animals come from great distances toward food, being guided by scent. That is why there is a puzzle. All animals smell in the same way, but a human being smells only when breathing in — when not breathing in but breathing out, or holding one's breath, one does not smell, whether the object is far away or near or even placed on the nostril itself. That what is placed directly on the sense-organ should be imperceptible is common to all animals, but not to perceive without breathing in is peculiar to human beings. This is clear from experience. Thus bloodless animals, since they do not breathe, would seem to have some further sense beyond those enumerated. But this is impossible, if it is odor they perceive. For the perception of odor — whether pleasant or foul — is smelling. Further, these animals appear to be destroyed by the same strong odors that destroy humans — such as bitumen, sulphur, and the like. They must, then, smell, though not by breathing.

This sense-organ seems to differ in humans from that in other animals, just as human eyes differ from those of hard-eyed animals. For human eyes have as a covering and guard the eyelids, which must be moved and drawn back before one can see. Hard-eyed animals have nothing of this kind, but immediately see whatever occurs in the transparent. Similarly, then, the organ of smell in some animals is uncovered, like the eye in hard-eyed animals, while in those that take in air it has a covering that is drawn back when one breathes in — as the veins and pores are dilated. That is why animals that breathe do not smell in water, since they must breathe in order to smell, and it is impossible to breathe in water.

Odor belongs to what is dry, as flavor belongs to what is moist. And the organ of smell is potentially of this kind.

### **Chapter 10**

The object of taste is a kind of tangible. And this is why it is not perceived through a medium that is an external body. Touch is the same in this regard. And the body in which the flavor resides — the object of taste — is in the moist as its matter. And the moist is a kind of tangible.

That is why, even if we lived in water, we would perceive anything sweet that was thrown into the water — but our perception of it would not come through the medium but through the thing's being mixed with the moisture, as in the case of a drink. Color is not perceived in this way, by being mixed, nor by effluences. There is therefore no medium at all in the case of taste. Rather, as the object of sight is color, so the object of taste is flavor. But nothing produces the perception of flavor without moisture: it must have moisture in actuality or in potentiality — as what is salty does, for this is easily dissolved and has a dissolving effect on the tongue.

Sight is of both the visible and the invisible (for darkness is invisible, but sight also judges darkness), and also of what is excessively bright (for this too is invisible, though in a different way from darkness). Similarly hearing is of both sound and silence, the one being audible and the other inaudible, and also of loud sound in the way that sight is of the bright. For just as a small sound is inaudible, in a way so too is a loud and violent one. "Invisible" in one sense means what is completely invisible — as in other cases what is called "impossible" — and in another sense, what is naturally of a kind to have the quality but either does not have it or has it only to a weak degree, like the footless or the seedless. In the same way taste is of the tasteable and the tasteless — the tasteless being what has a small or feeble flavor or is destructive of taste. The starting point seems to be the drinkable and the undrinkable. For both of these are a kind of flavor, but the one is poor and destructive of taste, while the other is natural to it. The drinkable is common to touch and taste.

Since the tasteable is moist, the sense-organ for it must be neither actually moist nor incapable of being moistened. For tasting is affected in some way by the tasteable qua tasteable. It is therefore necessary for the organ of taste to be moistened — the part that is capable of being moistened while yet remaining not actually moist — and then be capable of being moistened by the tasteable. An indication of this is that the tongue perceives neither when it is very dry nor when it is excessively moist. For in the latter case, contact is with the pre-existing moisture — as when someone with a previous taste in his mouth then tastes another flavor — and in the former case, the excessive dryness prevents contact. Rather, just as the object of sight is color, so the object of taste is flavor. And none of these produces the perception of flavor without moisture — it must have moisture in actuality or in potentiality.

### **Chapter 11**

Concerning the tangible, the same account holds. For touch is not one sense but several. This must be considered. The question arises what the body of this sense-organ is. Is it the flesh, and what is analogous to flesh in other animals? Or is the flesh rather the medium, while the primary sense-organ is something internal?

For every sense seems to be concerned with a single contrariety: sight with white and black, hearing with high and low pitch, taste with bitter and sweet. But in the tangible there are many contrarieties: hot and cold, dry and moist, hard and soft, and the rest of this kind. There is a partial solution to this puzzle, namely, that the other senses too perceive more than one contrariety. For instance, in voice there is not only high and low but also loud and soft, smooth and rough, and so on. There are other similar differences in the case of color too.

But what the one underlying subject is for touch — corresponding to what sound is for hearing — is not clear. Is it internal — is the sense-organ the flesh, or the heart, or whatever the corresponding organ might be? Or is it not internal, and is the flesh the immediate medium? But every sense seems to relate to a single contrariety, and touch appears to relate to several. It does not seem possible for them all to be grasped by one sense-organ.

There is also this consideration. Every sense perceives through a medium, but in the case of touch, this appears to go unnoticed. We do not perceive the tangible through any medium — or if we do, the medium is the flesh itself, like a membrane grown around us. If so, the flesh is the medium of touch, and we perceive through it, just as if, while an object was perceived through a membrane, we did not notice the membrane.

We should still investigate what makes it the case that the body of the tangible sense-organ is different from the body that serves as medium. For a sense is a ratio, and the sense-organ qua sensing is a ratio of the tangible qualities. And the organ of touch is the first sensitive part. Just as the part that perceives is so related to the perceptible as to be proportionate to it, the tangible perceptible must be such as to be perceived by a mean between the contraries. That is why we do not perceive what is equally warm or cold as our own body, but only the excesses — the sense being like a mean between the contraries in the tangible. And that is why it judges them. For the mean is a judge — since relative to each extreme, it becomes the other. And just as what is going to perceive white and black must be neither of them in actuality but both in potentiality — and similarly for the other senses — so in the case of touch, the organ must be neither hot nor cold.

Further, just as sight was in a way of the visible and the invisible, and the other senses similarly of their opposites, so too touch is of the tangible and the intangible. The intangible is either what has to a very small degree the distinctive quality of the tangible — as air does — or what has an excess of tangible quality that is destructive, such as extreme heat or cold.

So much, then, for an outline of each of the senses.

### **Chapter 12**

In general, concerning all perception, one must grasp that the sense is what is receptive of the forms of perceptible things without the matter, as wax receives the imprint of the signet ring without the iron or the gold. It takes the golden or the brazen imprint, but not qua gold or bronze. Similarly, each sense is affected by the thing that has color or flavor or sound, but not insofar as each of those things is said to be the particular thing it is, but insofar as it has the relevant quality and in accordance with the ratio.

The primary sense-organ is that in which this kind of capacity resides. These — the capacity and the organ — are then the same in subject, but their being is different. For what perceives is a certain extended magnitude, but neither the being of what perceives nor the perception is a magnitude — rather, each is a certain ratio and capacity of the organ.

From these things it is also clear why excesses in the perceptible objects destroy the sense-organs. For if the motion of the sense-organ is too strong, the ratio is broken down — and the ratio is the sense — just as the harmony and the pitch of strings are broken when they are struck too violently. It is also clear why plants do not perceive, although they have a part of soul and are affected in some way by tangible things — for they become cold and hot. The reason is that they do not have a mean, nor a principle of a kind to receive the forms of perceptible things. Rather, they are affected along with the matter.

One might raise the puzzle whether what is incapable of smelling could be affected by odor, or what is incapable of seeing by color — and similarly for the others. If the object of smell is odor, then if odor produces anything, it produces smelling, so nothing incapable of smelling can be affected by odor (and the same argument applies to the others), nor can any of the things that are capable of perceiving be affected, except insofar as each is a perceiver. This is also clear from the following. Light and darkness, sound and silence, do not affect bodies at all. Rather, it is the things in which they are present that affect bodies — for instance, it is the air that comes with thunder that splits the wood. But the tangible qualities and flavors do affect bodies. For if they did not, by what would soulless things be affected and altered? Will the other perceptible qualities also affect such things? Or is it the case that not every body is affected by odor and sound, and that the things that are affected are indeterminate and do not stand firm — like air, for the air takes on a smell, as though affected?

What, then, is smelling, beyond being affected? Or is smelling also perceiving, whereas air, when it is affected, quickly becomes a perceptible object rather than a perceiver?

## **Book III**

### **Chapter 1**

That there is no further sense beyond the five — I mean sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch — one may be convinced from the following considerations. If we in fact have perception of everything for which touch is the sense (for all the qualities of the tangible qua tangible are perceptible to us through touch), and if any sense we lack must mean that we also lack a certain sense-organ, and if all the things we perceive by touching them are perceptible by the touch that we do in fact have, while all the things we perceive through media and not by direct contact are perceptible through the simple elements — air and water — then the following must hold. If several distinct perceptible qualities are perceived through a single medium, then whoever possesses a sense-organ made of that medium must be able to perceive both sorts of quality, as for instance air is the medium for both color and sound. If the same quality is perceived through more than one medium — as color is perceived through both air and water — then whoever possesses either medium will perceive the quality in question. Now sense-organs are made only of these two elements — air and water. The pupil is made of water, the organ of hearing of air, and the organ of smell of one or the other. Fire belongs to none of the senses or to all in common, since nothing perceives without warmth, and earth belongs to none or is mixed in most in the sense of touch — so the sense-organ would be made of nothing besides water and air. And these, in fact, are what the sense-organs of certain animals are actually made of. All the senses, then, are possessed by animals that are neither incomplete nor maimed. For even the mole is observed to have eyes beneath the skin. So unless there is some further body or some quality that belongs to none of the bodies in this world, no sense would be missing.

Nor can there be a special sense for the common perceptibles — the qualities we perceive incidentally by each sense, such as motion, rest, shape, magnitude, number, and unity. For we perceive all of these by motion. For example, magnitude is perceived by motion, and therefore so is shape, since shape is a kind of magnitude. What is at rest is perceived by the absence of motion. Number is perceived by the negation of continuity, and also by the proper sensibles, since each sense perceives one kind of thing. So it is clearly impossible for there to be a special sense for any of these — for instance, for motion. For if there were, it would be the same as our perception of the sweet by sight. This we can do because we happen to have a perception of both, by which we recognize them when they occur together. Otherwise, we would only perceive them incidentally — as we perceive the son of Cleon, not because he is the son of Cleon, but because he is white, and the white thing happens to be the son of Cleon. But we have a common sense of the common sensibles, and our perception of them is not incidental. There is therefore no special sense for them, for then we would not perceive them in any way other than the way we now perceive the son of Cleon.

The senses perceive each other's proper objects incidentally — not insofar as they are the particular senses they are, but insofar as they form a unity, whenever the perception of the same object occurs simultaneously — as when we perceive that bile is bitter and yellow. For it is certainly not the function of either sense alone to say that both are one. That is why one can be deceived and, if something is yellow, think it is bile. One might ask why we have several senses and not just one. Is it in order that the common perceptibles that accompany the proper perceptibles — motion, magnitude, number — should be less likely to escape our notice? For if sight were the only sense, and its object were white, the common perceptibles would more likely escape notice, and all qualities would seem to be the same, because color and magnitude always accompany each other. But as things stand, since the common perceptibles are present in the objects of another sense too, this makes it clear that each of them is something distinct.

### **Chapter 2**

Since we perceive that we see and hear, it must be either by sight that we perceive that we see, or by some other sense. But then the same sense will be of sight and of the color that is the object of sight, so that either there will be two senses of the same thing, or the sense will be of itself. Further, if the sense that perceives sight were a different sense, either this will go on to infinity, or some sense will be of itself. So we may as well attribute this to the first sense.

But there is a difficulty. If perceiving by sight is seeing, and what is seen is color or what has color, then if one is to see the thing that sees, the thing that sees in the first instance will also have color. It is clear, then, that perceiving by sight is not one thing only. For even when we do not see, we distinguish darkness and light by sight — though not in the same way. Moreover, what sees is in a way colored. For each sense-organ is receptive of the perceptible object without the matter. That is also why perceptions and imaginings remain in the sense-organs even when the perceptible objects are gone.

The actuality of the perceptible object and of the sense are one and the same, though their being is not the same. I mean, for instance, actual sound and actual hearing. For it is possible for one who has hearing not to hear, and what has sound is not always sounding. But when what is capable of hearing is actively hearing and what is capable of sounding is sounding, then the actual hearing and the actual sound occur at the same time. One might call the one a hearing and the other a sounding.

If, then, the motion — both the acting and the being affected — is in what is acted upon, then both the actual sound and the actual hearing must be in what is potentially hearing. For the actuality of what is capable of acting comes about in what is affected. That is why what causes motion need not itself be moved. The actuality of what is capable of producing sound is sound or sounding, and the actuality of what is capable of hearing is hearing or hearkening. For "hearing" is twofold, as is "sound." The same account holds for the other senses and their objects. For just as the acting and the being affected are in what is affected and not in what acts, so the actuality of the perceptible object and of the perceptive faculty are in the perceiver. In some cases both have a name — for example, sounding and hearing — but in others one or the other has no name. For the actuality of sight is called "seeing," but the actuality of color has no name. And the actuality of taste is called "tasting," but the actuality of flavor has no name.

Since the actuality of the perceptible object and of the perceptive faculty is one — though their being is different — the hearing and sound spoken of in terms of actuality must be destroyed or preserved together. And likewise for the others. But when spoken of in terms of potentiality, this is not necessarily so. The earlier natural philosophers were wrong about this. They thought there was neither white nor black without sight, and no flavor without taste. In one sense they were right, in another wrong. For "sense" and "perceptible object" are said in two ways: in terms of potentiality and in terms of actuality. In the case of the actual, what they said holds. But in the case of potentiality, it does not. They were speaking without distinction about things that are said with a distinction.

If voice is a kind of harmony, and voice and hearing are in a way one — and if harmony is a ratio — then hearing too must be a kind of ratio. And this is why each excessive perceptible — both the high and the low in pitch — destroys hearing. Similarly, in the case of flavors, excess destroys taste, and in the case of colors, the excessively bright and the excessively dark destroy sight, and strong odor destroys smell — the implication being that the sense is a kind of ratio. That is also why the perceptible objects, when they are pure and unmixed, are pleasant — such as the sharp, the sweet, or the salty — for they are pleasant at that stage. And in general, what is mixed is more a harmony than what is high-pitched or low-pitched, and to the touch what is capable of being heated or cooled is more pleasant than either extreme. The sense is the ratio, and the excesses pain or destroy it.

Each sense, then, is concerned with its underlying sense-object, being present in the sense-organ qua sense-organ, and it judges the differences of its underlying sense-object — for example, sight judges white and black, taste sweet and bitter, and so for the others. Since we also distinguish white from sweet, and indeed each perceptible quality from every other, by what do we perceive that they differ? It must be by perception — for they are perceptible objects. This also makes it clear that the flesh is not the ultimate sense-organ. For if it were, the judging faculty would have to judge by touching the object.

Nor is it possible to judge by separate faculties that sweet is different from white. Both must be evident to some one faculty. For otherwise, the situation would be the same as if you perceived one and I perceived the other and it were then evident that the two are different. One and the same faculty must state that they are different. For the sweet is different from the white. It is the same faculty, then, that states this. And as it states, so it thinks and perceives. That it is not possible to judge separate objects by separate faculties is clear. And that it is not possible to judge them in separate times is also clear from the following. For just as the same faculty states that good and bad are different, so also when it states the one is different, it also states the other is different — and the "when" is not incidental, by which I mean that it does not say (as I now say that something is different) without saying that it is different now. Rather, it says both now and that it is now. Therefore simultaneously. So the faculty is a single undivided thing, active in an undivided time.

But perhaps it is impossible for the same thing, insofar as it is undivided, to be moved simultaneously with contrary motions. For if the object is sweet, it moves perception or thought in one way, while the bitter moves it in the contrary way, and the white in yet another. Is the judging faculty, then, simultaneously both undivided and inseparable in time, yet divided in its being? In a way, then, it is what is divided that perceives divided objects, but in another way it does so as undivided. For it is divided in its being but undivided in place and in number. Or is this not possible? For the same thing, insofar as it is undivided, can be both contraries potentially but not in its being. Rather, it becomes divided in its activation — and it cannot be white and black at the same time. So neither can it be affected by the forms of these — if perceiving and thinking are of this sort. Rather, the situation is like what some call a point, which insofar as it is one or two can be treated as both undivided and divided. Insofar as it is undivided, the judging faculty is one and judges at one time. But insofar as it is divided, it uses the same mark twice at the same time. Insofar as it treats the boundary as two, it judges two distinct things, and they are in a way separate. But insofar as it is one, it judges with one faculty and at one time.

Concerning the principle in virtue of which we say the animal is capable of perception, let this suffice.

### **Chapter 3**

There are two features by which the soul is defined above all: motion with respect to place, and thinking, judging, and perceiving. Both thinking and exercising practical intelligence are held to be like a kind of perceiving. For in both cases the soul judges and has knowledge of something that is. And the ancients at any rate say that exercising practical intelligence and perceiving are the same. Empedocles said "Wisdom grows for humans in relation to what is present," and in another place "Whence it befalls them always to think different thoughts." Homer's phrase "such is the mind" means the same. For all of these suppose that thinking is something corporeal, like perceiving, and that like perceives and thinks like, as we explained at the beginning. Yet they ought also to have discussed error at the same time. For error is more natural to animals than truth, and the soul spends more time in error. From this it must follow either that — as some say — all appearances are true, or that error is contact with what is unlike, since this is the contrary of knowing like by like. But it seems that the same capacity knows contraries, and error regarding contraries is the same error.

That perceiving and exercising practical intelligence are not the same is clear. For the former belongs to all animals, the latter to few. Nor is thinking — by which I mean that in which there are the right and the not-right: the right being practical wisdom, knowledge, and true opinion, the not-right being the contraries of these — the same as perceiving either. For the perception of the proper sensibles is always true and belongs to all animals, but it is possible to think falsely, and thinking does not belong to any animal that does not also have reason. For imagination is different from both perception and thought; and imagination does not occur without perception, and without imagination there is no belief. That imagination is not the same as belief is clear. For imagination is up to us whenever we wish — we can place something before our eyes, as those who set up images in mnemonic systems do — but forming opinions is not up to us, since the opinion must be either false or true. Further, when we form the opinion that something is terrible or fearful, we are immediately affected, and likewise with what is encouraging. But in the case of imagination, we are in the same state as those who contemplate terrible or encouraging things in a painting.

There are also differences within belief itself: knowledge, opinion, practical wisdom, and their contraries. The differences among these must be the subject of another discussion. As for thinking, since it is different from perceiving and is thought to comprise both imagination and judgment, we must first determine what imagination is and then discuss judgment.

If, then, imagination is that in virtue of which we say an image occurs to us — and we are not using the word metaphorically — is it one of those capacities or states in virtue of which we judge and are right or wrong? Such are perception, opinion, knowledge, and intellect.

That imagination is not perception is clear from the following. Perception is either a potentiality — like sight — or an activity — like seeing. But something can appear to us even when neither of these is present, as in dreams. Further, perception is always present, imagination is not. If they were the same in actuality, imagination would be available to all animals, but it does not seem to be — for instance, it does not belong to the ant or the bee or the grub. Further, perceptions are always true, but imaginings are for the most part false. Also, we do not say, when we are perceiving an object precisely, "this appears to be a man." Rather, we say this when we do not perceive clearly — and then the appearance may be true or false. And, as we said before, visions appear to people even when their eyes are closed.

Nor can imagination be any of the states that are always true — such as knowledge or intellect. For imagination can also be false. It remains, then, to see whether it is opinion. For opinion can be both true and false. But opinion is attended by conviction — for one cannot hold opinions one does not believe — and no beast has conviction, though many have imagination. Further, every opinion involves conviction, conviction involves having been persuaded, and persuasion involves reason. But while some beasts have imagination, none have reason.

It is clear, then, that imagination is neither opinion combined with perception, nor opinion through perception, nor a blend of opinion and perception — both from the arguments given and because the opinion would have to be of nothing else but the object of the perception, the blend of the opinion of white and the perception of white being the imagination of white. For surely it would not be the imagination of white that comes from the opinion of good and the perception of white. To imagine is then to have an opinion about the very thing one perceives, and not incidentally.

But false appearances do occur of things about which one simultaneously has a true supposition — as the sun appears to be a foot across, though one believes it to be larger than the inhabited earth. What follows, then, is that either one has thrown away one's true opinion, which one had, while the thing remains unchanged and one has neither forgotten nor been persuaded otherwise; or, if one still holds the opinion, then the same opinion is simultaneously true and false. But the opinion could only have become false if the thing changed without one's noticing it. Imagination is therefore neither one of these things nor composed of them.

But since it is possible, when one thing is moved, for another to be moved by it, and since imagination seems to be a kind of motion and not to occur without perception but only in perceivers and concerning the objects of perception, and since it is possible for motion to come about as a result of actual perception, and since this motion must be like the perception — this motion cannot exist without perception and cannot belong to things that do not perceive, and the thing that has it can do and undergo many things in virtue of it, and this motion can be both true and false. The reason for this last point is as follows. The perception of proper sensibles is true, or involves the least possible amount of falsehood. Second comes the perception that the incidental attributes accompany the sensible objects — and here one can already be in error. That the white thing is white one is not in error about, but whether the white thing is this or that, one can err. Third is the perception of the common sensibles — those that accompany the incidental attributes to which the proper sensibles belong, such as motion and magnitude. It is about these especially that one can be deceived in sense-perception.

The motion that comes about from the activity of perception will differ depending on which of these three kinds of perception it comes from. The first kind is true as long as the perception is present. The others can be false whether the perception is present or absent — and especially when the perceptible object is far away.

If, then, nothing else has the stated features apart from imagination, and imagination is what has been described, then imagination would be a motion produced by perception in actuality. Since sight is the sense par excellence, imagination has derived its name from light, because without light it is not possible to see. And because imaginings persist and are similar to perceptions, animals do many things in accordance with them — some because they lack intellect, namely beasts, and some because their intellect is sometimes obscured by passion or disease or sleep, namely humans.

Concerning what imagination is, then — what it is and why — let this suffice.

### **Chapter 4**

Concerning the part of the soul by which the soul knows and exercises practical wisdom — whether this is separable, or not separable in magnitude but separable in account — we must consider what differentiating feature it has and how thinking ever comes about.

If thinking is like perceiving, it would consist in being affected in some way by the intelligible, or in something else of a similar kind. This part of the soul, then, must be impassible, but receptive of the form, and must be potentially like the form but not identical with it. And as the perceptive faculty is related to perceptible objects, so must intellect be related to intelligible objects.

It is therefore necessary that the intellect — since it thinks all things — be unmixed, as Anaxagoras says, in order to master them, that is, in order to know them. For the intrusion of anything foreign hinders and obstructs it. And so intellect has no nature of its own other than this — that it is a capacity. The so-called intellect of the soul — I mean the intellect by which the soul thinks and forms judgments — is actually none of the things that are, before it thinks. That is also why it is reasonable that it should not be mixed with the body. For in that case it would come to have some quality — cold or hot — or it would have an organ, as the perceptive faculty does. But as things stand, it has none.

Those who say the soul is the "place of forms" speak well, except that it is not the whole soul but only the thinking part, and it is the forms not in actuality but in potentiality.

That the impassibility of the perceptive faculty is not the same as that of the thinking faculty is clear from the sense-organs and from perception. For the sense, after perceiving something strongly perceptible, cannot perceive — for instance, after a loud sound one cannot hear, and after strong colors or odors one cannot see or smell. But when the intellect thinks something that is highly intelligible, it does not think less well about lesser things but rather better. For the perceptive faculty does not exist without the body, but the intellect is separable.

When the intellect has become each of the intelligible objects, as the knower is said to when he is actually knowing — and this happens when he can exercise his knowledge by himself — even then the intellect is in a way potential, though not in the same way as before learning or discovering. And then it is able to think by itself.

Since magnitude is different from the being of magnitude, and water from the being of water — and so too for many other things, though not all, since for some the thing and its being are the same — we judge flesh and the being of flesh either by different faculties or by the same faculty differently disposed. For flesh does not exist without its matter, but is like the snub — a this in a this. It is, then, by the perceptive faculty that we judge the hot and the cold and the things of which flesh is a kind of ratio. But it is by a different faculty — either one that is separate from the perceptive or one that is related to it as the bent line is to the same line when straightened — that we judge the being of flesh. And again, in the case of things that exist by abstraction, the straight is like the snub, since it is always accompanied by extension. But its what-it-was-to-be, if the being of the straight and the straight are different, is something else. Let us say it is twoness. We judge it, then, by a different faculty, or by the same faculty in a different disposition. And in general, as things are separable from matter, so are the faculties of the intellect.

One might raise the puzzle: if the intellect is simple and impassible, and has nothing in common with anything, as Anaxagoras says, how will it think, if thinking is a kind of being affected? For it is insofar as two things have something in common that the one seems to act and the other to be affected. Further, is the intellect itself intelligible? For either intellect will belong to all other things — if it is intelligible not by virtue of something else and if the intelligible is one in form — or it will have something mixed in it, which makes it intelligible in the same way as the others.

The distinction we drew earlier about being affected in virtue of something common must serve here: the intellect is in a way potentially the intelligible objects, but is actually none of them before it thinks. It must be like a tablet on which nothing has actually been written. This is exactly what happens with the intellect. And it is itself intelligible in the same way as its intelligible objects. For in the case of things without matter, what thinks and what is thought are the same. For contemplative knowledge and its object are the same. We must consider why the intellect does not always think. In the case of things that have matter, each of the intelligible objects is present only potentially. So intellect will not be present in those things — for the intellect is a potentiality for being such things without the matter — but the intellect will be intelligible.

### **Chapter 5**

Since in the whole of nature, for each genus there is something that serves as matter — that which is potentially all the members of the genus — and something else that is the cause and the productive factor, by virtue of producing them all — the relation between them being like that of an art to its material — it is necessary that these differences also exist in the soul. And indeed there is, on the one hand, an intellect that is such by becoming all things, and on the other hand an intellect that is such by making all things, like a kind of positive state such as light. For in a way light too makes potential colors into actual colors.

This intellect is separable, impassible, and unmixed, being in its substance an actuality. For what acts is always superior to what is acted upon, and the principle is superior to the matter. Actual knowledge is identical with its object. Potential knowledge is prior in time in the individual, but in general it is not prior even in time. This intellect does not sometimes think and at other times not think. When separated, it is just what it is, and this alone is immortal and eternal. But we do not remember because this intellect is impassible, whereas the passive intellect is perishable. And without this, nothing thinks.

### **Chapter 6**

The thinking of indivisible objects takes place among things about which there can be no falsehood. Where there is falsehood as well as truth, there is already a combination of thoughts that form a unity — as Empedocles said, "Where the heads of many grew without necks," and were then combined by Love. In the same way, these thoughts, which were previously separate, are combined — for instance, the incommensurable and the diagonal. If the thinking is about things that have been or will be, the thinker combines and thinks of time in addition. For falsehood always involves a combination. Even when one thinks that the white is not white, one has combined "not-white." One might also call all of these cases division. But at any rate, falsehood and truth are not only about whether Cleon is white, but also about whether he was or will be. And what makes each of these a unity is the intellect.

Since "indivisible" has two senses — potentially indivisible and actually indivisible — nothing prevents the intellect from thinking the indivisible when it thinks a length (for the length is actually undivided), and from doing so in an undivided time. For the time is divided and undivided in the same way as the length. It is not possible, then, to say what the intellect was thinking in each half of the time. For the half does not exist, except potentially, unless the whole is divided. In thinking each of the two halves separately, the intellect simultaneously divides the time as well. And then the lengths are treated as two. But if the intellect thinks the length as composed of two halves, it also thinks in a time that covers both halves.

What is indivisible not in quantity but in form, the intellect thinks in an undivided time and with an undivided act of soul. But incidentally, and not in the way in which the time and the object of thought are indivisible, the thinking is divisible — the actuality being indivisible in a different sense. For in these too there is something indivisible, though perhaps not separable, that makes the time and the length one. And this is equally present in every continuous thing, both time and length.

The point, and every dividing mark, and whatever is indivisible in this way, is made known in the same manner as a privation. The same account applies by analogy to other cases as well — for example, how the intellect knows evil or black. For it knows them in a way by their contraries. What knows must be potentially its object and must contain the contrary in itself. But if there is any cause that has no contrary, then it knows itself and is in actuality and is separable.

Assertion is about something said of something, and is in every case either true or false. But not every act of intellect is of this kind. The thinking of the what-it-was-to-be in the sense of the what-it-is is true, and is not about something said of something. But just as the seeing of the proper object is true, while seeing that the white thing is a man or is not a man is not always true, so it is with whatever is without matter.

### **Chapter 7**

Actual knowledge is identical with its object. Potential knowledge is prior in time in the individual, but in the whole it is not prior even in time. For all things that come to be are derived from what is in actuality.

The perceptible object makes the perceptive faculty actual out of being potential. But the faculty is not affected and is not altered. This is therefore a different kind of change. For motion was the actuality of what is incomplete, while actuality in the unqualified sense — the actuality of what has been completed — is a different thing.

Perceiving, then, is like bare assertion and thinking. When the object is pleasant or painful, the soul, as if asserting or denying, pursues or avoids it. And to feel pleasure or pain is to be active with the sensitive mean in relation to what is good or bad as such. Avoidance and appetite, when actual, are the same, and the appetitive faculty is not different from the faculty of avoidance — nor are these different from each other or from the perceptive faculty. Rather, their being is different.

For the thinking soul, images serve in place of perceptions. When it asserts or denies good or bad, it avoids or pursues. That is why the soul never thinks without an image. Just as the air affects the pupil in a certain way and the pupil affects something else, and similarly for hearing, but the last thing is one — a single mean, though its being is many.

We have already said by what faculty the soul distinguishes that the sweet differs from the hot, but we must state it again as follows. It is something that is one, and one in the way a boundary is. And these — the sweet and the hot — being one by analogy and in number, stand to each other as their respective qualities stand to each other. For what difference does it make whether we ask how the soul distinguishes things that are not of the same genus, or how it distinguishes contraries like white and black? Let A — white — stand to B — black — as C stands to D; then alternating, A stands to C as B stands to D. If, then, C and D belong to one thing, they will be related the same way as A and B, which are the same and one though their being is not one — and similarly for the other pair. The same account would hold if A were the sweet and B the white.

The thinking faculty, then, thinks the forms in the images, and just as what is to be pursued and avoided is determined for it in the sensory field, so also when it is apart from perception and is engaged with images, it is moved. For example, perceiving that the torch is fire, one recognizes by the common sense, seeing it move, that an enemy approaches. At other times, one calculates and deliberates about the future by means of images or thoughts in the soul, as though seeing them, and when one says that something there is pleasant or painful, one avoids or pursues it here — and in general in the sphere of action. And what is apart from action — the true and the false — is in the same genus as the good and the bad. They differ, however, in that the one pair is unqualified and the other is relative to someone.

What are called abstractions — such as mathematical objects — the intellect thinks as if it were thinking of the snub, not qua snub, but (if anyone were actually thinking it) qua curved, without the flesh in which the curved is found. So when it thinks the mathematical objects, it thinks them as separable, though they are not in fact separable. And in general, the intellect when active is identical with its objects. Whether or not the intellect, not being itself separate from magnitude, can think anything that is separate — this must be considered later.

### **Chapter 8**

Summing up what has been said about the soul, let us say again that the soul is in a way all the things that are. For the things that are are either perceptible or intelligible, and knowledge is in a way the things that are known, and perception is in a way the things perceived.

We must inquire in what way this is so. Knowledge and perception are divided to correspond to the things — potential knowledge and perception to potential objects, actual knowledge and perception to actual objects. The perceptive and the cognitive faculties of the soul are potentially these things — the one the object of knowledge, the other the object of perception. They must be either the things themselves or their forms. The things themselves they are not — for it is not the stone that is in the soul, but the form of the stone. The soul is like the hand. For the hand is the tool of tools, and the intellect is the form of forms, and perception the form of perceptible things.

Since there seems to be no thing that exists apart from perceptible magnitudes — as it appears — the intelligible objects are in the perceptible forms: both the things called abstractions and all the states and affections of perceptible things. And that is why, if one perceived nothing, one would learn and understand nothing. And when one contemplates, one must simultaneously contemplate an image. For images are like perceptions, except that they are without matter.

Imagination is different from assertion and denial. For truth and falsehood involve a combination of thoughts. But what distinguishes the first thoughts from images? Surely not even these other thoughts are images, though they do not occur without images.

### **Chapter 9**

The soul of animals has been defined by two capacities: the capacity for judging, which is the function of thought and perception together, and the capacity for producing locomotion. Enough has been said about perception and intellect. We must now investigate what it is in the soul that produces motion. Is it one part of the soul, separable either in magnitude or in account? Or is it the soul as a whole? And if it is some part, is it some special part beyond those usually spoken of and those we have mentioned? Or is it one of them?

The difficulty arises at once of the sense in which we should speak of parts of the soul and how many there are. For in one way they seem to be unlimited and not confined to those that some thinkers distinguish — the rational, the spirited, and the appetitive — or those that others distinguish — the rational and the irrational. For if one considers the differentiating features by which these thinkers divide them, other parts will appear as well, having greater differences than these — parts we have in fact already discussed: the nutritive, which belongs to plants and to all animals alike; the perceptive, which cannot easily be classified as either rational or irrational; further, the imaginative, which is different in being from all the others, though it is very puzzling to say which of the other parts it is the same as or different from, if one posits separate parts of the soul; and in addition to these, the appetitive, which would seem to be different from all of them both in account and in capacity. And it is absurd to split this apart. For wish arises in the rational part, while desire and spiritedness arise in the irrational part. And if the soul is in three parts, appetite will be in all three.

To turn to the question now before us: what is it that moves the animal with respect to place? The motion of growth and decay, being present in all animals, would seem to be produced by the part that belongs to all — the generative and nutritive faculty. Breathing in and breathing out, sleep and waking, must be examined later, since these too raise many difficulties. But about locomotion, we must consider what it is that moves the animal with progressive motion.

It is clear that it is not the nutritive faculty. For this motion is always for the sake of something and is accompanied by imagination or appetite. For nothing moves except what desires or avoids something — except by force. Furthermore, plants would then be capable of locomotion and would have some instrumental part for this motion. Similarly, it is not the perceptive faculty. For there are many animals that have perception but remain stationary and immobile throughout their lives. If, then, nature does nothing in vain and omits nothing necessary — except in cases that are deformed or incomplete — and if such animals are complete and not deformed (the evidence being that they reproduce and have maturity and decay), then they would also have the organs of progressive locomotion. Nor is it the thinking faculty or what is called intellect that produces the motion. For the theoretical intellect thinks nothing that concerns action, and says nothing about what is to be avoided or pursued, while motion is always the motion of something that is avoiding or pursuing something. Even when it does think of such a thing, it does not immediately command avoidance or pursuit. For instance, the mind often thinks of something frightening or pleasant, but does not command fear — though the heart is moved, or in the case of something pleasant, some other part.

Further, even when the intellect commands and thought tells us to avoid or pursue something, one is not moved but acts according to appetite — as in the case of the person lacking self-control. And in general we see that the person who has the medical art does not necessarily heal, the implication being that it is something other than knowledge that is responsible for acting in accordance with knowledge — and not knowledge itself. Nor is it appetite that is responsible for this motion. For those who have self-control, even though they have an appetite and desire something, do not do the things they have an appetite for but follow intellect.

### **Chapter 10**

It appears, then, that it is two things that produce motion: appetite and intellect — if one counts imagination as a kind of thinking. For many people follow their imaginations against their knowledge, and in the other animals there is no thinking or reasoning, only imagination. Both of these, then — intellect and appetite — are capable of producing locomotion. The intellect in question is the one that calculates for the sake of something — practical intellect. It differs from theoretical intellect by its end. And all appetite is for the sake of something. For the object of appetite is the starting point of the practical intellect, and the last step in the practical intellect's deliberation is the starting point of action.

So these two seem reasonably identified as the causes of motion: appetite and practical thought. For the object of appetite produces motion, and thought produces motion because the object of appetite is its starting point. And when imagination produces motion, it does not do so without appetite.

What produces motion is therefore one thing: the faculty of appetite. For if two things produced motion — intellect and appetite — they would do so by virtue of a common form. But as things stand, intellect does not appear to produce motion without appetite — for wish is a form of appetite, and when someone is moved in accordance with reasoning, they are also moved in accordance with wish. But appetite does produce motion contrary to reasoning, since desire is a form of appetite. Now, intellect is always right, but appetite and imagination may be either right or wrong. That is why what produces motion is always the object of appetite — but this is either the good or the apparent good. Not every good, but only the practicable good. And the practicable good is what is capable of being otherwise than it is.

That, then, it is this kind of capacity in the soul — what is called appetite — that produces motion, is clear. Those who divide the soul into parts, if they divide it according to the capacities and separate them, arrive at very many: the nutritive, the perceptive, the thinking, the deliberative, and further the appetitive. For these differ from one another more than the desiring and the spirited do.

Since contrary appetites arise — and this happens when reason and desires are contrary, and it occurs in beings that have a perception of time (for the intellect commands holding back because of the future, but desire commands action because of the immediate; for what is immediately pleasant appears both pleasant without qualification and good without qualification, because of the failure to see the future) — the mover would be one in form, the faculty of appetite as such, and first of all the object of appetite (for this produces motion without being moved, by being thought of or imagined), but the things that produce motion are many in number.

There are three factors in motion: first, what produces the motion; second, the means by which it produces it; and third, what is moved. What produces the motion is twofold: what is unmoved, and what produces motion while also being moved. What is unmoved is the practicable good. What produces motion while being moved is the appetitive faculty — for what desires is moved insofar as it desires, and appetite when active is a kind of motion. And what is moved is the animal. The instrument by which appetite produces motion is something bodily, and so it must be examined among the functions common to body and soul.

To summarize for now: what produces motion instrumentally is located where beginning and end coincide, as in a hinge joint. For there the convex and the concave are respectively the end and the beginning — which is why the one is at rest and the other is moved. They are different in account but inseparable in magnitude. For everything is moved by pushing and pulling. That is why, as in a circle, something must remain at rest, and the motion must originate from there.

### **Chapter 11**

In general, then, as has been said, it is insofar as the animal is capable of appetite that it is capable of moving itself. And it is not capable of appetite without imagination. All imagination is either rational or perceptive. In the latter, then, the other animals share too.

We must also consider what it is that produces motion in the case of imperfect animals — those to which only touch belongs, such as worms and many insects. Do these have imagination and desire, or not? For pleasure and pain plainly appear in them. And if these are present, desire must be present too. But how would imagination be present in them? Perhaps, just as they are moved indeterminately, so these capacities are present in them, but present indeterminately.

Perceptive imagination, then, as we said, is found in the other animals too, but deliberative imagination is found only in rational animals. For whether to do this or that is already a work of reasoning. And one must measure by a single standard, for one pursues what is greater. So one must be able to make a single image out of several. And the reason why imagination is not thought to involve opinion is this: it does not involve opinion from reasoning, though opinion involves imagination. That is why appetite does not contain the deliberative faculty. But sometimes it overcomes and moves wish, and at other times wish overcomes and moves appetite, like one sphere moving another — appetite overcomes appetite. But by nature the higher appetite is always more authoritative and produces motion. And so three kinds of locomotion are already at work.

The faculty of knowledge is not moved but stays at rest. Since one premise or judgment is universal and the other concerns the particular — the first saying that a person of such-and-such a kind should do such-and-such, and the second that this is a thing of such-and-such a kind and I am a person of such-and-such a kind — it is the latter opinion that produces motion, not the universal. Or rather, both do, but the universal is more at rest, while the particular is not.

### **Chapter 12**

Every living thing, then, must have the nutritive soul, and in fact has a soul from its birth until its death. For what has been born must have growth, maturity, and decay, and these cannot occur without nourishment. The nutritive capacity is therefore necessarily present in all things that grow and decay.

But perception need not be present in all living things. For those whose body is simple cannot have touch — and without touch, no animal can exist. Nor can those that are incapable of receiving forms without matter have perception. An animal, however, must have perception, if nature does nothing in vain. For all things in nature exist for the sake of something, or are concomitants of things that exist for the sake of something. Every body capable of progressive motion would, if it lacked perception, be destroyed and would not reach its end, which is the function of nature. For how would it be nourished? Things that are stationary have their nourishment from the place where they originated. But it is not possible for a body that is not stationary but comes into being by birth to have a soul and a discerning intellect without also having perception. Nor indeed even if it were ungenerated. For why would it not have perception? Either it would be better for the soul, or better for the body. But in fact neither is the case: the soul will not think better, and the body will be no better off, without perception. No body, then, that is not stationary has a soul without also having perception.

But if a thing has perception, the body must be either simple or mixed. It cannot be simple, for then it would not have touch, and without touch it cannot exist. This is clear from the following. Since the animal is an ensouled body, and every body is tangible, and the tangible is what is perceptible by touch, it is necessary that the body of the animal must also be capable of touch, if the animal is to be preserved. For the other senses perceive through something else — through media — but what touches is destroyed by direct contact with objects that exceed it in the relevant quality. For touch is the sense not only of tangible things but of things that would destroy the animal. If an animal does not have this sense, it cannot avoid some things and take others, and if it cannot do this, it will be impossible for the animal to be preserved. That is why taste too seems to be a kind of touch: for it is concerned with nourishment, and nourishment is a tangible body. Sound, color, and smell do not nourish, nor do they produce growth or decay. So taste must be a kind of touch, being the sense of what is nourishing and tangible.

Both of these senses, then, are necessary for the animal, and it is clear that without touch it is impossible for an animal to exist. The other senses, on the other hand, exist for the sake of well-being and belong not to just any genus of animal but to certain ones — for instance, they must belong to whatever is capable of progressive motion. For if such an animal is to survive, it must perceive not only by contact but also from a distance. This will be the case if it can perceive through a medium, with the medium being affected and moved by the perceptible object, and the animal by the medium. For just as what produces locomotion causes a change up to a certain point, and what gives a push makes something else push so that the motion proceeds through a medium — the first mover pushing without being pushed, the last thing being only pushed and not pushing, while the medium both pushes and is pushed (and there may be many media) — so too in the case of alteration, except that the thing is altered without changing place. So if someone dips something in wax, the wax is moved to the extent that the dipping goes — a stone would not be moved at all, and water would be moved far. Air is moved the most and acts and is affected the most, as long as it remains one and continuous. That is why, in the case of reflection, it is better to say not that the sight goes out from the eye and is reflected, but that the air is affected by the shape and color as long as it is one. On a smooth surface it is one — that is why the air in turn moves the organ of sight, as if the seal on a signet ring were pressed through the whole depth of the wax.

### **Chapter 13**

That the body of the animal cannot be simple — by which I mean, for instance, all fire or all air — is clear. For without touch it cannot have any other sense, since every ensouled body is capable of touch, as has been said. The other elements besides earth could become sense-organs, but they all produce perception by perceiving through something else — that is, through media. Touch, by contrast, operates by direct contact with its objects, and that is why it has the name it does. The other sense-organs, too, perceive by touch, but through a medium. Touch alone seems to perceive through itself.

So none of these elements — fire, air, water — could constitute the animal body, nor could earth. Touch is the sense of none of these but of the tangible qualities — hot and cold, dry and moist. And the organ of touch must be potentially such as its objects. For perceiving is a kind of being affected, so what acts makes what is potentially such actually such. That is why we do not perceive what is equally hot or cold or hard or soft, but only the excesses — implying that the sense is a kind of mean between the contraries in the tangible. That is why it judges the tangible: for the mean is a judge — since relative to each extreme it becomes the other.

And just as what is going to perceive white and black must be neither in actuality but both in potentiality — and similarly for the other senses — so too for touch: the organ must be neither hot nor cold. Further, just as sight is in a way of the visible and the invisible, and the other senses similarly of their contraries, so touch is of the tangible and the intangible. The intangible is what has a very small degree of tangible quality — like air — or what has an excess of the tangible that is destructive.

Each of the senses has now been treated in outline. In general, concerning all perception, one must grasp that the sense is what is receptive of perceptible forms without the matter, as wax receives the imprint of the ring without the iron or the gold. It takes the golden or brazen imprint, but not qua gold or bronze. In the same way, each sense is affected by the thing that has color, flavor, or sound, but not insofar as each of these is said to be the individual thing it is, but insofar as it has a certain quality and in accordance with the ratio.

The primary sense-organ is that in which such a capacity resides. The organ and the capacity are the same in subject but different in being. For what perceives is a certain extended magnitude, though the being of what perceives — the perceptive faculty — is not a magnitude but a form and a capacity of the organ. From this it is also clear why excesses of perceptible objects destroy the sense-organs. For if the motion of the sense-organ is too strong, the ratio is broken down — and the ratio is the sense — just as harmony and pitch are broken down when the strings are struck too violently.

It is also clear why plants do not perceive, even though they have a part of soul and are affected by tangible things — they become cold and hot, after all. The reason is that they have no mean, and no principle of the kind that can receive the forms of perceptible objects. They are affected, but together with the matter.

One might puzzle about whether something that cannot smell can be affected by odor, or something that cannot see can be affected by color, and so for the other senses. If the object of smell is odor, then what odor produces, if anything, is smelling, and so what is incapable of smelling cannot be affected by odor — and the same reasoning applies to the others. Nor can perceivers be affected except insofar as each is a perceiver. This becomes clear from the following as well. Neither light nor darkness, neither sound nor silence, affect bodies at all — rather, the things in which they are present do so. It is the air that comes with the thunder that splits the wood. But the tangible qualities and flavors do affect bodies. Otherwise, what would affect soulless things and alter them? Will the other perceptible qualities do so too? Or does not every body admit of being affected by odor and sound? And those that are affected are indeterminate and shifting things — like air, for air becomes odorous, as if affected. What then is smelling, beyond being affected? Smelling is also perceiving, whereas the air, when affected, quickly becomes something perceptible.

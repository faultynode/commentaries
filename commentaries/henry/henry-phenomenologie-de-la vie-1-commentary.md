# Michel Henry, *Phénoménologie de la vie I: De la phénoménologie* — Commentary

Citations give the page of the PUF "Épiméthée" edition (2003). Quotations are in Henry's French. The volume gathers eleven essays spanning 1965 to the late 1990s; the editor's foreword is not treated. Several confusions recur in the reception of these texts and are guarded against throughout. Auto-affection is not reflexive self-relation, not a turning-back of consciousness upon itself, and not a closed-off "autism": it is the condition of any relation to the world. Immanence is not the psychologist's interior space or Kant's inner sense, both of which Henry places on the side of exteriority. The invisible is not the unconscious in the representational sense, nor a lack of appearing, but a mode of appearing in its own right. Life is not the object of biology. Affectivity is not one psychic region among others (emotion as opposed to cognition) but the phenomenological substance of self-givenness. Finally, Henry's critique of intentionality does not deny that consciousness is directed at objects; it denies that this directedness can be the first or only mode of manifestation.

## I. Does the Concept of the Soul Have a Meaning?

### 1

Henry asks whether the old metaphysical concept of the soul can still mean anything for us, and fixes at once what "meaning" requires: reference to a reality that corresponds to it. The question is therefore not semantic but ontological. A source note matters for reading the whole essay: the text records two public lectures given in Brussels in November 1965, published in 1969; Henry wished to include it only in substantially revised form and managed, before his death, to rewrite only the first part. The two halves accordingly differ in style and, to some degree, in doctrine. The second half belongs to the period of *L'essence de la manifestation* and *Philosophie et phénoménologie du corps*; the first is late Henry rewriting his own early argument.

What stands between us and the traditional doctrine — the soul as a simple, spiritual, self-identical, imperishable substance distinct from body — is the eighteenth-century critique, above all Kant's, which claimed to be radical and therefore final. Its radicality lies in subordinating special metaphysics (whose objects are soul, world and God) to general metaphysics, which in Kant becomes the inquiry into the conditions of possible experience, that is, into the being of any being whatever. Henry endorses Heidegger's reading of the *Critique* as a laying of the foundation of ontology. The endorsement is strategic: if Kant's critique is ontological, it must be met on ontological ground.

The consequence for the soul follows directly. Its problem is the problem of the being of the self, and that being depends on the structure of being in general. Whatever holds of being in general — that it cannot be known a priori by pure thought, that it cannot be known as it is in itself — must hold of the self's being. The Transcendental Dialectic says exactly this: the being of the ego can neither be posited by pure thought nor grasped as it is in itself. Kant dismisses the concept of the soul because the soul names precisely that real, in-itself being of the self. Henry's reconstruction makes Kant's rejection of the soul a corollary of a general ontology rather than a series of local logical corrections. That is what makes it vulnerable.

To speak of the soul again, then, one must first reject the Kantian critique — and Henry already holds the guiding thread. The critique of Kant's critique of the paralogisms must take the form of an "ontological destruction," a laying bare of the structures of being that Kant presupposes. The single question is: what idea of being leads Kant to refuse being to the cogito as such? Henry will not contest the Dialectic's propositions one by one; he goes back to the understanding of being they all share, the horizon within which *cogito ergo sum* can look like a paralogism. The term "destruction" is Heidegger's (*Destruktion*), turned here against the ontology Heidegger himself shares with Kant — a reversal that the essay's second part makes explicit.

Kant's ontology rests on a phenomenological premise, and Henry grants it: we know only phenomena; nothing exists for us except within experience. The decisive limitation lies in what Kant takes a phenomenon to be — what is given to sensibility and thought by the understanding. Sensibility is the power of intuition, and intuition does not create its object but finds it. Consciousness therefore relates through intuition to something it is not and did not make, something transcendent to it. It can do so only by projecting a pure medium, an horizon, in which the being can show itself. This projection of a pure field of encounter is pure intuition, and it is "l'objectivité même dans son surgissement premier" (p. 11): space, but more fundamentally time. Henry reads the Transcendental Aesthetic through Heidegger's *Kantbuch*, where pure intuition is the formation of an horizon of objectivity.

Transcendence thus acquires its true sense. It no longer means only that a being is metaphysically other than consciousness; it means that being is phenomenologically exterior, set at a distance within a field of visibility. Henry's formula for Kant's ontology is brief: "Être, pour Kant, c'est être donné comme représenté, posé là devant un champ de visibilité" (p. 11). Everything in the essay hangs on this identification of being with being-placed-before. It is the first statement of what Henry elsewhere calls "ontological monism": the thesis, common to Kant, Husserl and Heidegger, that there is only one mode of manifestation, the opening of exteriority (the target of the opening sections of *L'essence de la manifestation*).

The pure field, however, is empty. Pure intuition always operates together with empirical intuition, because a pure medium of space or time contains no actual existence. Kant assigns the delivery of existence to sensation: "La sensation [...] est ce qui désigne une réalité" (p. 12, citing A 374). Sensation thus plays something like the role of an origin, since experience is real only as experience of an existence that is essentially empirical. Henry registers here, without yet exploiting it, a tension he will later press hard: reality is entrusted to sensation, yet sensation is received in a form of exteriority. Whether sensation is a self-feeling impression or merely a content laid out in time becomes the hinge of essays II and IV.

The understanding confirms the point. Left to itself, it leads nowhere: Kant insists that the bare category shows the possibility of nothing and that an intuition must always be at hand to establish the objective reality of a pure concept (B 288), and that the category has only an empirical use (B 147). The intuition required is empirical. Both faculties of the transcendental power of knowledge thus point beyond themselves to sensation as the bearer of reality.

Henry sums up: real knowledge for Kant is synthetic knowledge. The problem of synthesis is not only Heidegger's problem of the original unity of pure intuition and understanding; it is the problem of what must be added to that transcendental power for knowledge to become knowledge of something. Sensation supplies the something, so that synthetic knowledge is empirical knowledge. The quiet correction of Heidegger matters: the *Kantbuch* concentrated on the pure synthesis of imagination and so passed over the question of where reality comes from.

Considered in itself, apart from the empirical element, the transcendental power of knowledge is marked by "une sorte d'indigence ontologique foncière" (p. 13): it lacks existence. Because it must find the element of reality outside itself, it is intuitive and receptive. Kant calls it a merely logical condition, and Henry specifies why: not because it is an understanding, but because it is cut off from what makes being actual. Hence his objection to calling this condition "subjectivity." A subjectivity that is not a life, only a logical entity, does not deserve the name. The objection contains Henry's whole programme in miniature: subjectivity must be defined by actual self-experience, not by its function as a condition of objects.

This poverty of pure thought shows in the concept. Because pure thought is separated from actual existence, the concept by itself yields no knowledge and answers no question; Kant says it "tourne toujours sur lui-même" (p. 13, A 398) and that every synthetic solution requires an intuition. Henry then extends the point beyond Kant's intention: the concept cannot even know itself. There is no immediate inner self-possession of the concept, "parce qu'il n'y a pas chez Kant de philosophie de la subjectivité" (p. 13). Kant's agnosticism about the self is thus traced not to the limits of knowledge but to the absence of any account of how thinking is given to itself.

With these premises in place, Kant's critique of rational psychology becomes transparent. Rational psychology claims real knowledge of the soul from pure thought alone, but knowledge without an empirical element is no knowledge. It merely develops transcendental predicates — substantiality, simplicity, identity, distinct existence — which determine the logical condition of knowledge in general. Those predicates may well be true of that condition; they say nothing about our actual being, which only empirical predicates, grounded in sensation, can determine. Henry restates Kant fairly, and in doing so isolates the premise he will attack: that the self's actual being, if it is knowable at all, is knowable only through sensation received in intuition.

Rational psychology also lacks a concept of the self. A genuine concept is more than a category: it is the concept of a determinate object, like "man," and determination requires relation to intuition, which rational psychology by hypothesis lacks. Further, the object would have to be precisely my self, and no such concept can be drawn from the general condition of knowledge, which, as Kant says, distinguishes no particular object (B 404). Rational psychology therefore lacks both the concept and the intuition of a self — everything Kant requires for an actual phenomenon. Henry here notes, without comment, that the self Kant cannot reach is singular. The singularity of the ego, its being *mine*, becomes the essay's decisive criterion.

How then is the being of the self to be known, if not by pure thought? Kant answers with his theory of inner experience, which simply reapplies the general premises of his ontology: the self is determined by being subjected to the conditions of being in general. To be, it must be given to sensibility and thought by the understanding: an empirical element exhibited in the pure intuition of time, the form of inner sense, and then determined by the categories in their empirical use, receiving a place in the system of experience.

Inner experience, so understood, is the conceptual determination of an impression received in inner sense: empirical knowledge of the empirical self, the only self we can know. The definition closes the exposition; the critique begins with the next unit.

Henry's argument is that the structure of being as Kant understands it is incompatible with the structure of the being of our self. He names this latter structure "the essence of ipseity" — selfhood in the strict sense of being oneself. The title echoes *L'essence de la manifestation*, where ipseity is identified with affectivity.

If the argument holds, two things must be shown: that Kant's inner experience cannot deliver our self, and that whenever Kant speaks of a self he presupposes the essence of ipseity without accounting for it. The two-pronged plan governs the rest of part 1: a negative demonstration against inner sense, then a diagnosis of the "I" in "I think."

Of the two components of inner experience, the conceptual one has already been disposed of by Kant himself: the paralogisms show that the self's being cannot be drawn from pure thought or pure concepts. Only intuition remains to be examined. Henry turns Kant against himself: the critique of rational psychology removes half of Kant's own account of self-knowledge.

Henry then states his fundamental thesis: "l'ego par principe n'est pas susceptible d'être intuitionné" (p. 16). The impossibility lies in the nature of intuition, which is inhabited by a primordial transcendence and so always opens onto something other than the power of intuiting. In inner experience that power is inner sense — and a sense, for Kant, always means affection by an alien content. What is given to inner sense is therefore, in principle, otherness itself, the not-self as such. How could selfhood arise in the dimension of radical otherness? The argument does not depend on any particular deficiency of inner sense; it follows from the definition of givenness as reception of something other. This is the essay's central move, and it is purely structural.

The content of inner sense is twofold, depending on whether its intuition is pure or empirical. If pure, the content is pure time, which, Kant says, "ne peut être perçu en lui-même" (p. 16, B 225); what cannot be perceived at all cannot be perceived as a self. Henry anticipates the Heideggerian reply: pure time is no being, a nothing, yet something — the three-dimensional horizon of future, present and past. But that horizon is not a self or the possibility of one; it is precisely the dimension of otherness into which intuition opens. The rebuttal targets the *Kantbuch*'s thesis that time as pure self-affection forms the essential structure of subjectivity (Heidegger, GA 3, § 34). For Henry, Heidegger's "self-affection" of time remains the self-affection of exteriority, and so is no self-affection at all.

If the intuition is empirical, the content of inner sense is the flow of impressions and sensations occurring in it. This is the last place left to look for the self.

The question whether sensation can give us the self, our own, is not to be posed in general but to Kant: can sensation in inner sense, within his own philosophy, exhibit a self? Henry confines the inquiry to Kant's concept of sensation so that the negative result will not be read as a claim about feeling as such — which Henry will defend as the self's very substance.

The answer is negative. Kantian sensation is blind, an opaque content unable to say what it is — "un je ne sais quoi qui contient une existence" (p. 17; the editors could not locate the phrase in Kant). If inner sensation is an unknown something, how could we know that the existence it contains is our self? Worse, sensation cannot be considered in the abstract but only as it is, and for Kant its being consists in being intuited, received in the horizon of pure time, a medium of exteriority and otherness. Inner sensation is therefore not only nameless; it is something for us only insofar as it emerges into the light of that medium "où elle se donne à nous comme autre que nous" (p. 17). Henry's objection is not that Kant ignores feeling but that he gives feeling a mode of appearing — appearing-as-other — that strips it of what would make it mine.

This failure explains attempts to tie the empirical content of inner sense to the self, so that its sensations are no longer an indifferent manifold but a content bound to the self in a specific way. There are two options: the sensation is produced by the self, as its effect; or it is received by the self, lived and undergone by it. The second option, "éprouvée par lui," is already the vocabulary of Henry's own solution, and he will show that it cannot be had within the Kantian frame.

Traces of both attempts appear in Kant himself — in the concept of self-affection introduced in the second edition of the *Critique* and in the *Opus postumum* — and two major commentators have stressed them (Jean Nabert's 1924 article "L'expérience interne chez Kant" and Pierre Lachièze-Rey's *L'idéalisme kantien*). On Nabert's reading, as the transcendental power of knowledge synthesizes the manifold of outer intuition and constructs the object under the categories, each act of determination strikes inner sense from within, like a recoil; that shock is the sensation of inner sense. Such a sensation is no longer arbitrary: its origin in the world-constructing self gives it a character of its own. Henry presents the most sophisticated French reading of Kantian self-affection, the one closest to his own concerns, before rejecting it.

The solution is illusory. It is not enough to assert that inner sensation is produced in us by the transcendental subject, or even undergone by it. That origin cannot remain a speculative hypothesis; the affection of inner sense by the transcendental subject would have to be a phenomenological datum, carrying the essence of ipseity within it. But then ipseity, and the existence of my self, would belong to "pure thought" — which is exactly the thesis of rational psychology. Nabert's solution either remains a hypothesis or reinstates what Kant refuted.

Not quite, Henry corrects. Rational psychology holds that the self's being belongs to pure thought and can be known absolutely from it; Kant denies that the "I" contained in "I think" can yield any real, synthetic, progressive knowledge of our being. Yet the two share an essential presupposition: that a self belongs to pure thought, and doubtless mine. Kant concedes it: "Le moi est bien dans toutes les pensées" (p. 19, A 350). Henry asks what obliges us to grant this "monumental" thesis in passing, only to declare it barren. Why does Kant write "I think" and not "thought thinks"? Even if "I think" yields no new knowledge, it carries a fundamental one: the knowledge of an I included in pure thought as such. The critique thus becomes more radical than Kant's: the paralogisms are not the problem; the unexamined attribution of an I to thought is. The question "Why not 'thought thinks'?" recalls Lichtenberg's objection to Descartes ("es denkt"), which Henry will cite in part 2 (p. 36).

Kant could not wholly evade the question, and his answer is "at once derisory and singularly illuminating": the "I think" is an intellectual representation — the only expression Kant uses for it without immediately correcting himself. That the "I think" is an intellectual representation means that it is the content of a representation from which every empirical element, every sensation, is absent. The adjective "illuminating" is earned in the next units: the formula reveals the phenomenological assumption that governs Kant's whole treatment of the self.

To say that "I think" is the content of a pure representation means that when I say "I think," I represent to myself that I think. The I and its thinking appear only on the side of what is represented, as content, "devant nous, car c'est cela être représenté, c'est être présenté devant" (p. 20). Henry exploits the etymology (*re-praesentatio*, setting-before) to tie representation to the ek-static structure of exteriority already identified with Kantian being.

Kant thus substitutes for the original cogito, the radically subjective ego we are, the mere representation of that ego. The texts confirm it: Kant never speaks of the original "I think" but always of the "proposition" "I think," which "expresses" or "represents" the spontaneity of thought without being it — its replica in a first dimension of objectivity, its image. The point anticipates the Cartesian analysis of *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*, where Henry distinguishes the *videre videor* of the cogito, a self-appearing prior to all seeing, from its later reduction to representation.

Why the substitution? Because the original ego can be spoken of only if it shows itself, only if it is a phenomenon, and Kant knew only one dimension of phenomenality: representation. To appear, and so to be, the "I think" had to be represented. The diagnosis is the essay's key: Kant's error is not epistemological but phenomenological — a single, unquestioned concept of appearing.

Is this arbitrary? For Kant, the original "I think" is the unity of consciousness, the synthetic unity that gathers the manifold into one representation, the form of apperception. Is that form a phenomenon? Kant explicitly denies it: "La forme de l'aperception est inhérente à toute expérience, mais elle n'est pas elle-même une expérience" (p. 20, A 354). For the condition of all unity to become conscious of itself, it would have to be represented — and Kant says just that: self-consciousness is the representation of what is the condition of all unity (A 405). The texts confirm that, for Kant, self-consciousness can only be a representation of the self, never the self's own appearing.

Kant thus replaces the original ego with its representation. Henry now generalizes his earlier thesis: the real being of the ego cannot be intuited, and more broadly cannot be represented, so representing it must mean losing it. The representation "I think" must then exhibit a fundamental ontological poverty — which is exactly what Kant affirms, and rightly, once the "radical ontological misinterpretation" of seeking the ego on the plane of representation has been committed. The cogito reduced to a representation contains no *sum*. The critique of the paralogisms starts from this representation, which Kant calls "l'unique texte de la psychologie rationnelle" (p. 21, B 401). Henry concedes that Kant's analysis of the paralogisms is an admirable description of the poverty of the represented ego, and cites the paralogism of personality: nothing remains in the soul but the I, "qui n'est si simple que parce que cette représentation n'a aucun contenu" (p. 21, A 381). The Kantian critique is thus correct, but as a phenomenology of representation, not as a doctrine of the self. Its results confirm Henry's thesis rather than refuting the soul.

Because the representation "I think" is empty, anything can be placed under it: the I that constructs the universe, the determining and the determinable self, the empirical self, the transcendental object underlying inner sense, the unknown noumenal self, the author and addressee of the moral law. Like clouds that take every shape because they have none, the represented self — "l'ego transcendantal dont parlera à son tour la phénoménologie contemporaine" — is "un fantôme conciliant" (p. 22). The aside extends the charge to Husserl: the transcendental ego of phenomenology inherits Kant's representational emptiness. Henry's later readings of Husserl (essays IV, V and X) develop the charge in detail.

Yet however poor, the representation "I think" is too rich: it is still the representation of a self, indeed of a particular self, as Kant recognizes when he calls the representation "I am" singular and "individuelle à tous égards" (p. 22, A 405). Where then is this always particular self that the representation represents? Where is the ipseity it presupposes? Kant's own text testifies to a singularity that representation cannot produce and can only presuppose — the second prong of Henry's plan, now completed.

The result is not merely negative. We have learned that the self's being is incompatible with Kant's structure of being, which is phenomenological — the structure of representation. If the essence of ipseity cannot be built in the unfolding of exteriority, nor any self appear there, the task of a new phenomenology is laid out: to put the question of phenomenality radically in question and, freeing it from its traditional and Kantian limits, to disclose "un mode de révélation propre à l'essence de l'ipséité" (p. 22). The sentence states the programme of Henry's entire oeuvre: a second mode of appearing, proper to the living self.

The last question of part 1 — whether twentieth-century "classical" phenomenology was ever capable of such an undertaking — is rhetorical. It bridges to part 2, which examines that phenomenology in Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty. Readers should note the change of idiom at the join: the unrevised second part speaks of "interiority," a term the late Henry largely replaces with "immanence" and "auto-affection," and it argues from Maine de Biran's body rather than from a general theory of Life.

### 2

The critique of Kant tied the existence of our original self to a dimension of radical interiority. Has that helped the problem of the soul? Henry doubts it. Interiority looks anachronistic, a concept modern philosophy has disposed of. Many philosophers still cherish the idea of an intimate personal life and try to reach it by reflection — the French reflexive tradition, still alive — or by a Bergsonian intuition. Henry distances himself from both allies in advance: reflection and intuition are modes of seeing, and so belong to the very structure he will contest.

Modern philosophy proper, the one that began with Husserl and reached remarkable results, forbids talk of interiority because it has submitted the concept to a phenomenological critique. Its principle is the demand to show the reality one speaks of — to bring it into the field of visibility, the horizon of light that Heidegger calls the world. But the world is a medium of radical exteriority; exteriority is visibility itself, and so "l'extériorité est la loi de l'être, elle est l'être lui-même" (p. 23). Henry reconstructs the opponent's best argument: the very demand for evidence, which defines phenomenology, seems to rule interiority out, since to show is to exteriorize.

Take the wall of the lecture hall. Common sense does not distinguish the wall from its being, but for the philosopher the being of the wall is rather its negation: the wall held in radical exteriority to itself, without which it would not be given. The gaze that reaches the wall is no psychological process but this self-exteriority of the wall itself. The same holds of bricks, atoms and everything that is: each is only by being negated, immersed in the nothingness that lets it appear. I too am only insofar as I appear, and so am traversed by universal exteriority: "Je suis un autre" (p. 24). For the interior to be, it would have to enter the light of being — to exteriorize, objectify and destroy itself. "L'intérieur n'existe pas." The language of negation and nothingness compounds Heidegger with Sartre and Hegel; Henry presents them as one ontology, the ontology of ek-stasis, which *L'essence de la manifestation* calls the "horizon of being." Rimbaud's formula ("Je est un autre") becomes the thesis of that ontology about the self.

Knowledge offers a second example. The naive picture of a knowledge first shut up in the subject, which must leap miraculously out of an inner sphere to reach its object, has to go. Heidegger notes that a silence reigns over what the inside of immanence positively means, and that Dasein, in grasping something, does not first leave an inner sphere but, by its primary way of being, is "toujours déjà dehors" (p. 24, citing *Sein und Zeit*, pp. 60, 62). Henry accepts Heidegger's demolition of the inner-sphere picture. His objection will be that the "silence" Heidegger notes is not an argument: the positive meaning of immanence was never sought.

Action and language follow the same pattern. I do not speak or act to express an intimate reality prior to the act; I speak and act so that reality may be, coming to being by exteriorizing itself. Expression, on this ontology, does not convey an inner content but makes being happen. The thesis is broadly Hegelian and is shared by Merleau-Ponty's theory of expression; Henry will reverse it in essay X, where language is said to presuppose a self-revelation that no word produces.

Henry will contest these powerful and universal ontological presuppositions — which modern philosophy has brought to conceptual clarity and which develop Kant's — not in general but through a single example: the body, my body. The choice is methodical. The body is where exteriority seems most at home; if interiority can be found there, it can be found anywhere.

It may seem paradoxical to defend interiority, and so the soul, by appeal to the body. The paradox fades once the body is understood as subjective — no longer one-sidedly as an object but as a subject, perhaps the true one, the source of sensible knowledge, itself understood as the ground of all knowledge. The analysis of the body in its original subjectivity then seems to lead toward the interiority sought. The hypothesis of the "subjective body" belongs to the 1965 context: *Philosophie et phénoménologie du corps*, written in 1948–49 and published that year, makes Biran its discoverer.

Yet the idea of a subjective body stays undetermined so long as subjectivity does. Everything depends on what subjectivity is — and modern philosophy does not reject the concept but claims to give it its ontological sense. It no longer conceives the subject as a being shut up in itself, nor as a super-being dominating others, but grasps its pure subjectivity as the ontological element of transcendental truth. Subjectivity is then no something or someone; it is unveiling itself, exteriority and its opening, "l'ekstase originelle qui fait régner un monde," being-in-the-world, existence as transcendance (pp. 25–26). The decisive point is that the phenomenological tradition did not abolish subjectivity; it redefined it as transcendence. That redefinition, not the denial of the subject, is Henry's target.

If subjectivity is existence and transcendence — intentional, or rather the ground of all intentionality, the original ek-static reference to an horizon, Kant's pure relation to an object — then to call the body subjective is to call it essentially intentional, a bursting-out toward things. This is Merleau-Ponty's thesis: "Mon corps [...] est ce qui m'ouvre au monde et m'y met en situation" (p. 26). Better to speak, as he often does, of "bodily existence," which "me fait continuellement la proposition de vivre," continually throwing me into the world. The chain from Kant's pure relation to an object through Heidegger's transcendence to Merleau-Ponty's body-subject puts the *Phenomenology of Perception* squarely inside the Kantian ontology criticized in part 1.

The body's relation to the world may seem to break off in illness, when the patient loses interest and withdraws into himself — a possibility that is also everyone's, realized provisionally in daily life. Merleau-Ponty describes it: I can close my eyes, lie down, listen to my blood beating, dissolve into a pleasure or a pain, shut myself into "cette vie anonyme qui sous-tend ma vie personnelle" (p. 26). Yet even then bodily existence does not stop being existence: it still surpasses itself, projects toward a future, waits — if only for the next heartbeat or some inner state. Merleau-Ponty insists that even absorbed in the solitude of sensation I cannot suppress all reference of my life to a world; some intention always springs forth, toward surrounding objects or toward the instants that arrive and push what I have just lived into the past. Henry stresses this last point: the reference to time concerns inner sensations and lived experiences themselves, which are thus also subject to a temporal horizon — "le temps est précisément l'extériorité originelle" — so that coenesthesia itself takes the form of a world (p. 27). To turn from the world toward so-called inner bodily life does not interrupt the reign of exteriority. Merleau-Ponty: I can withdraw from the human world only to find in my body "la même puissance, cette fois sans nom," and "l'existence corporelle ne repose jamais en elle-même" (p. 27). Merleau-Ponty's "anonymous life" is the closest the tradition comes to Henry's subject matter, and Henry shows that it is still interpreted by temporal ek-stasis. The phrase "never rests in itself" is exactly what Henry will deny of life.

These descriptions are accurate in their way and faithful to experience, yet they leave an essential problem in the dark. If the body opens us to the world and so defines our original knowledge of it, how does this body that knows a world know itself? How is the body that is present to the world — "le véhicule de notre présence au monde" (Henry quotes from memory; p. 27) — first present to itself? The question is logically and ontologically prior, because the power that opens us to the world can do so only if it is first ours, in our possession, if we coincide with it, so that this first coincidence, the radical unity of ourselves and our body, is itself a knowledge and the ground of our knowledge of the world. The argument has the form of a transcendental regress: every intentional power presupposes a non-intentional possession of that power. The whole of Henry's philosophy of the body follows from this priority.

Put otherwise: the body is said to be the set of our powers over the world, our motor and sensory intentionalities. But how are these powers first in our power? How can we reach them, actually exercise them, and through them reach things? The pun on *pouvoir* ("power," "can") is substantive: a capacity that I could not first "have" without mediation would not be mine to exercise.

Modern philosophy, which denies interiority, does not answer. We must turn to a philosopher who made interiority the centre of his reflection: Maine de Biran, discoverer of the subjective body, who knew the novelty of his view — he speaks in the *Essai sur les fondements de la psychologie* of "le point de vue tout à fait nouveau sous lequel je considère la connaissance du corps propre" (p. 28). Henry stresses "knowledge of one's own body": for Biran, the subjective body means something quite different from what it will mean in contemporary phenomenology. Biran did not ignore the body's transcendence, its capacity to make a world known, but he posed a prior and unavoidable problem — the knowledge of the body that knows the world. Biran is Henry's hero throughout *Philosophie et phénoménologie du corps*; here he is used against Merleau-Ponty, who knew Biran and in Henry's view read him only as a precursor of the body-subject.

Two questions must therefore be radically separated: the body's knowledge of the world, primitive only relative to the world, and the more truly primitive knowledge of our own body, the body that knows. The distinction emerges in Biran's critique of Condillac's account of how we know our body. In Condillac's sensualism, man is the site of manifold sensations, most of which carry nothing that would lead us beyond them to an external world. What drives us to posit a real being behind them is the sensation of solidity, whose organ is the hand.

Applied to the body: how do we know it? By the hand, which, moving over the parts of my body and constantly feeling solidity, reveals their real being and traces their form. My hand is thus the principle of my knowledge of my body — a remarkably modern thesis, since it makes the body, or one of its parts, a principle of knowledge. Henry credits Condillac with anticipating phenomenology's body-subject; the credit sets up the same critique for both.

The thesis is illusory. The original body is not the body traced and recognized by the hand as it moves, but the moving hand itself. The problem of original knowledge of the body is therefore not that of the parts successively circumscribed by the hand, but that of the hand's movement, of the knowing body — a problem Condillac simply forgets to pose. The distinction between the body known and the body knowing is the hinge of Henry's whole phenomenology of the body, from the subjective, organic and objective body of *Philosophie et phénoménologie du corps* to the distinction between body and flesh in *Incarnation* (and essay IX here).

Biran makes exactly this objection: Condillac's hand is the instrument by which I know my body, but "Cet instrument lui-même, comment est-il connu d'abord ?" (p. 30, citing the *Mémoire sur la décomposition de la pensée*). Contemporary phenomenology, Merleau-Ponty especially, does not ask it either. It describes admirably our access to the world through motor and sensory intentionalities — an access that is not the act of a pure understanding or an inspection of the mind but a looking with the eyes — yet it says nothing about the knowledge of the knowing body, of the hand as it moves and touches itself, of our inner, original knowledge of the power of grasping. Henry's mention of the hand that "touches itself" is pointed: Merleau-Ponty's reversibility of touching and touched will later be Henry's chief target in *Incarnation*, since it reduces self-touching to a relation between two exterior aspects of the body.

Here the basic theses of contemporary ontology are challenged and perhaps reversed, and interiority, "si hâtivement et si dogmatiquement écarté," regains its rights (p. 30). Our original knowledge of the grasping power is no longer intentional: no ek-stasis, nothing laid out before it, no exteriority. It cannot be intentional, because all intentionality rests on transcendence and unfolds an horizon, the medium of otherness. Whatever is reached across an horizon is given as other, foreign; what is given through ek-static relation is thereby withdrawn from us — what we fail to be, what we are only in the mode of not being it. Intentional knowledge sets an insurmountable distance between us and what it joins us to, because givenness takes place precisely in that interval. Henry inverts the usual valuation of distance: the interval that makes objects visible is the very thing that would make a power not mine.

If the power of grasping were given through some intentionality, it would be external to us, other than us, not ours — the very thing we are deprived of, what we cannot do. Entering the medium of exteriority, "qu'on s'obstine à considérer comme le seul milieu de l'être," the power becomes dialectically its contrary, a non-power, alienated in the radical sense (p. 31). "Alienation" carries its Hegelian-Marxian weight: Henry's later *Marx* (1976) will use exactly this structure — living praxis versus its objectified representation.

Biran said it in one of the weightiest sentences of the tradition: "Il n'y a pas de force étrangère absolue" (p. 31; Biran actually wrote that there is no possible *notion* of an absolute foreign force). An absolute force, an efficient causality, a power actual in its exercise cannot lie in the medium of exteriority, separated from itself, foreign to itself. Every true power is first given the power to be itself, to take hold of itself, to coincide with itself in a primal cohesion that excludes all distance — in the immanence of radical interiority. The editors' correction of the quotation is meaning-bearing: Biran's claim is epistemic (no notion), Henry's ontological (no such force). Henry reads the epistemic statement as the sign of a structure of being.

Is there such an absolute force, an efficient causality — which traditional metaphysics either denied or reserved for God — a true "I can" in its actual exercise? Yes: my body. It is the absolute, irrefutable power by which I expand or contract my chest, open or close my fingers, rise and walk. "Mon corps est le mouvement qui se prouve en marchant" — the movement that attests itself inwardly, my action as I live it in an immediate experience "qui défie tout commentaire et à plus forte raison toute contestation," the freedom "qui se moque des paralogismes" and of science (pp. 31–32). Henry turns Hume's and Malebranche's denial of experienced causality on its head: the felt "I can" is the paradigm of causality, not its absence. The lyrical close of the paragraph also redeems part 1: what Kant's paralogisms could not reach is lived in each gesture.

The philosophy of the subjective body (the printed "objectif" is evidently a slip) aimed to account for this power that I coincide with and am placed inside, this "I can" that I am. It can do so only with a concept of subjectivity radically different from modern philosophy's — one that no longer identifies subjectivity with transcendence but defines the inner structure of subjectivity, and of the body, by excluding all intentional relation. If the first relation of the self to its body were intentional, the self would be separated from its powers and could no longer reach them, and the old problem of the soul's action on the body would return with urgency. Using the hand would become unintelligible, a matter of magic, since the hand would be given to me only across intentional difference. The body would become an instrument of my action on the world — but how could the soul use an instrument it no longer is and is separated from forever? Henry turns the classic objection against dualism back on phenomenology: Merleau-Ponty's body-subject, by locating subjectivity in transcendence, reproduces the Cartesian gap between the soul and its instrument.

If instead the self's first relation to its body is not intentional, we understand our corporeity and can clear up a serious confusion: the customary link between corporeity and sensibility. Is the body not the totality of our senses? So Heidegger, when he offers a transcendental theory of the body, defines its possibility as that of sensibility: man as finite rational being can "have" a body in a transcendental sense only because transcendence as such is sensible a priori (p. 33, citing *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics*). Transcendence indeed structures the senses; *Sein und Zeit* says that sight and hearing are senses of distance only on the ground of Dasein in them (Henry condenses the original: Heidegger says they reach far not by their range but because Dasein, as de-distancing, dwells chiefly in them, § 23). That is why, as Kant said, a sense always means affection by a foreign content: transcendence inhabits it. Henry's lineage is precise: Kant's sense as affection by the other, Heidegger's transcendence as sensible a priori, Merleau-Ponty's sensing body — one thesis.

What is questioned is not the rooting of sensibility in transcendence but the identification of sensibility with the original phenomenon of the body. Sensibility concerns only the secondary phenomenon of our bodily knowledge of the world. Original corporeity — the self's relation to its own knowing body — excludes all transcendence and does not occur through sense, any sense. "Nous ne sentons pas notre corps originel et nous ne pouvons le sentir" (p. 33). The paradox is precise: the original body is not felt, because feeling (as sensing) is directed outward; it is undergone in a self-experience that is not a sense. Later Henry will call this self-experience "auto-impressionality" and rescue the vocabulary of impression from the vocabulary of sensation (essay IX).

Return to the movement of the hand. Is it known by a sensation? Classical psychology and philosophy say yes: by kinaesthetic sensations — a view still present, not by chance, in Sartre's *L'imaginaire*. The phrase "not by chance" implies that Sartre's consciousness, wholly outside itself, can relate to its own movement only as to an object.

Biran showed that my action is not known by a sensation. Kinaesthetic sensation is muscular sensation; it reports the modification in my muscles when I act. But it can be the same when I am not acting, when an external cause modifies my body so as to produce it. Muscular sensation therefore cannot make my own action known, since it can occur when I do not act. The argument is an argument from indiscernibility: what cannot distinguish my acting from my being acted upon cannot be what reveals my acting.

Take two wrestlers, and put yourself inside one of them. His muscular sensations result either from his own effort or from his opponent's hold. The sensation is silent about its origin; it can be referred to my own action, and lived as its effect, only if that action, which is mine, first reveals itself in itself. The example makes the regress concrete: the felt sensation can be mine only if my effort is already given to itself independently of it.

How could the original body be known by a sensation anyway? It is composed of intentionalities — motor ones, for example. Would an intentionality be grasped by a sensation? "Je n'éprouve pas plus mon action que mon doute, ou que mon vouloir, par la médiation d'un sens quelconque" (p. 34). The comparison with doubt and will places the body on the side of the Cartesian *cogitationes*: Henry's reading of Descartes's "thought" as everything that is felt immediately (essay IV; *Généalogie*) is here applied to movement.

Movement is therefore known by nothing other than itself; it is not apprehended by a power of sensing whose intentional content it would be. The original relation of my body to itself is not ek-static and does not take place in sensing. But that relation is my corporeity. Original corporeity excludes all transcendence; no exteriority yet unfolds in it. "La corporéité est une intériorité radicale" (p. 34). The conclusion completes the regress begun at p. 27: the body's self-knowledge is not a second act of knowing aimed at the body but the body's very being.

We have spoken of the body's original relation to itself, and earlier of the self's original relation to its body; in general, we seek to elucidate knowledge of one's own body. What does the intervention of a self mean here? What grounds the "special right," in Descartes's phrase (*Meditation VI*), by which I call this body mine? The question of ownership of the body is the question of ipseity, and it will be answered by the same structure that answered the question of the body's self-knowledge.

The subjective relation of the self to its body is nothing other than the original relation of the body to itself. There is not a self on one side and a body with its powers on the other; the being of each power consists in the auto-affection by which it is present to itself without distance. Such an affection — not by the content a movement or a sense unfolds in exteriority, a foreign content, but by itself — "est l'ipséité" (p. 35). The two questions collapse into one: self-knowledge, ownership and selfhood are the same auto-affection. This is the first appearance of "auto-affection" in the volume as a positive term, and its sense differs from Kant's and Heidegger's "self-affection" of time criticized in part 1: here the affecting and the affected are identical, with no distance.

For the power, the agent, the sensing, the knowing, there is thus an original affection whose content is the agent itself as acting, the sensing itself as sensing, the knowing itself as knowing. In such an affection no content is dead, intentional or separate; everything is alive. That is why we are living beings, why there is a place in us where everything happens "sur le mode du participe présent" and nothing presents itself as foreign: "la structure de l'ipséité est la structure même de la vie" (p. 35). The grammatical image is exact: the present participle names the doing in its doing, never its result. The equation of ipseity with life announces the "phenomenology of life" of the later essays; in 1965 it remains a conclusion about the body, not yet a doctrine of absolute Life.

The body, in its original non-ek-static relation to itself, therefore necessarily produces itself as a Self. It is not by virtue of particular properties, nor as the sum of its parts, the totality of functions it synthesizes, nor as the totality of our senses; it is by its very ipseity. Hence a self inhabits it originally: there is a Self of movement and a Self of sensing. Henry reverses the order of explanation: ipseity is not added to a body already constituted; the body exists only as a self.

The body is originally through its ipseity, its interiority: this first internal cohesion, this dwelling that we are and in which we are living beings, is the body, our body — "Et c'est cela aussi peut-être qu'on appelle notre âme" (p. 35). The answer to the title question is given here, in the conditional mode: the soul is not a substance opposed to the body but the body's own self-givenness.

Because the body's ipseity is identically its interiority, the hesitations and contradictions of the philosophies of transcendence on this issue become intelligible — visible in Merleau-Ponty. Having identified the I and the body, having said "Je suis mon corps," he must separate them. Deprived of its interiority, the body becomes mere existence, transcendence, its being merged with the anonymous advent of a world. So Merleau-Ponty writes that to render perceptual experience exactly I should say that "on perçoit en moi" and not that I perceive, and that perception always takes place in the mode of the "one" (p. 36). The I appears only on the plane of clear thought and the classical cogito, named — as in certain texts of Husserl — in connection with deliberate acts by which thought turns toward a prior sensible datum. But what grounds this appearance of the I on the plane of thought? Kant could not legitimate the I of "I think," and Descartes's cogito was charged with an ego it should not carry: should one not say "it is thought," "one thinks in me"? Yet what grounds the "in me"? Attributing the ego to pure thought is as gratuitous in Kant as expelling it from bodily life is in Merleau-Ponty. When Merleau-Ponty speaks of "l'existence corporelle qui fuse à travers moi sans ma complicité" and of a current of existence about which we never know whether its forces are its own or ours, the truth of these sentences may rest on presuppositions quite different from his. The fundamental, insurmountable passivity encountered there is not the light flight of a world, the free projection of an horizon, negativity, imagination, possibility; nor is it, as Merleau-Ponty thinks, foreign to ipseity. It sinks into ipseity and defines it. For ipseity is precisely "cette passivité radicale à l'égard de soi-même," being riveted to oneself without distance or retreat, being one's own living content, one's own life, inexorably — unable to escape, assume, refuse or even accept it (pp. 36–37). This is the essay's deepest move: Henry takes over Merleau-Ponty's best description, the anonymity and passivity of bodily life, and reassigns it. Passivity is not the absence of a self; it is the self's very structure, its being given to itself without having given itself. This doctrine of the self's radical passivity becomes, in the later essays, the self's generation in Life (essays III, VI, IX).

The radical interiority of the body, its ipseity, alone grounds one of its most essential and visible characters: its situation. Situation is not fully explained by transcendence. As Dasein, the body is there, situated in a world; existence understands itself in a given situation by projecting itself toward the possibility it has chosen. Only because I plan to climb a rock can I face an unclimbable rock (Sartre's example in *Being and Nothingness*); more generally, my situation is that of a being delivered over to the world to die because my ultimate possibility is my death. The ek-static relation thus seems to contain the principle and the true features of every situation. Henry presents the Heideggerian and Sartrean account of situation fairly, as derived from projection.

Yet the original question of situation is not how existence understands its situation, returning ek-statically to itself from a finite horizon, but how the existence that unfolds ek-stasis is first delivered to itself in its first coincidence with itself, such that, in this radical passivity toward itself, it is what it is and cannot escape its being or what that being does. The ultimate situation lies not in transcendence but in "le Soi de la transcendance" (p. 37): what situates us is not being in the world but, more originally, our ipseity, the fact that we are a self, this self from which we can never escape. Heidegger's thrownness (*Geworfenheit*) is reinterpreted: what we are thrown into is first ourselves, not the world. The theme returns in Henry's analyses of suffering and despair (essay VII), where the impossibility of escaping oneself is shown in affectivity.

In Kafka's *Amerika*, Karl, after many vain attempts to find a place, a situation, comes upon a circus recruitment poster, and one sentence especially pleases him: "Ici il y a une place pour chacun" (p. 37). For Henry it expresses the metaphysical condition of being. Contemporary ontology easily accounts for "here," "there is," "a place" — but what does the "each one" that surges up at the end of the sentence mean? Is ipseity a contingent addition to the anonymous, impersonal advent of being in exteriority, an accidental limitation of its universality? Or is it the condition of being, its original embrace with itself "dans une lumière qui n'est plus celle du monde," the first givenness that is ipseity itself (p. 38)? Being has a monadic structure, which Henry calls "interiority" for lack of a better word. The concept of the soul has a meaning if it refers not only to a reality but to the fundamental structure of every possible reality. The conclusion turns the essay's question around: the soul is not one being whose existence must be proven, but the form of reality as such. The Heideggerian "there is" (*es gibt*) is met with the "each one" it cannot generate, and the Leibnizian word "monadic" announces the treatment of intersubjectivity in essay XI, where community is a sharing of Life among selves, not a relation across the world. The phrase "faute de mieux" concedes the inadequacy of "interiority"; the later Henry will replace it with "immanence" and "auto-affection."

## II. What Is This That We Call Life?

Henry begins from the objection that "life" is vague: it covers nutrition and reproduction in any minimally organized being, the everyday activity of human beings, and their highest spiritual experiences. Perhaps the word's praise-laden aura, and the prestige of Romantic philosophies of vital expansion, rest on this confusion; perhaps "life" is merely what is invoked against mechanism, logic and reason, a flight from ideal abstraction into the instinctive, the unconscious or the mystical. Yet a rigorous account of these meanings would find in each the same mysterious essence: what makes us, too, living beings. That is why the Gospel's "Je suis la Voie, la Vérité et la Vie," Kierkegaard's truth "pour quoi on voudrait vivre ou mourir," and Marx's thesis that "ce n'est pas la conscience des hommes qui détermine leur vie, mais leur vie qui détermine leur conscience" still shake us to the core, despite the advances of linguistic analysis (pp. 39–40). The lecture was given at the Université du Québec à Trois-Rivières in 1977, between *Marx* (1976) and *Généalogie de la psychanalyse* (1985), and the triple citation maps the work of that decade: Christianity, Kierkegaard's affective existence, Marx's living praxis. The jab at "linguistic analysis" sets the address against the Anglophone and structuralist climate of the moment.

"Vivre signifie être" (p. 40). The concept of life loses its apparent vagueness once it marks out the field and task of ontology, that is, of philosophy itself. If life means being, it cannot be identified with specific phenomena studied by biology or mysticism, which presuppose it like everything that is. But does life not lose its concreteness — what moves us in its very name — if we strip from it eating, sexuality and the ordinary activities of everyone's life? Nourishing oneself, dressing, meeting and embracing another belong to life as its first and undeniable manifestations. What must be understood is why and how they are living, on the basis of what essence within them. Kafka points the way: "Avec chaque bouchée du visible, une invisible bouchée nous est tendue, avec chaque vêtement visible un invisible vêtement" (p. 40, from the *Diaries*). The equation of life with being is not an abstraction away from concrete life but a claim about where its concreteness lies: in the invisible doubling of every visible act.

Life means being — but being must be understood so that it means life. Western philosophy, from its Greek beginning up to and including Heidegger, whether explicitly ontological or moved by an implicit ontology, presupposes a concept of being that excludes the essence of life insurmountably. Life is suspect to philosophy not because it is vague or doubtful — it is the most certain thing — but because philosophy has been unable to think it. Life is constituted in its inmost being as a radical interiority that can scarcely be thought, whereas Western being is defined by exteriority. The wall of this room is a particular reality, different from the table and still more from its being, which makes it and the table be. That being, Fichte says in a sentence that "contient le destin de la métaphysique occidentale," is "son être en dehors de son être" (p. 41, from *Die Anweisung zum seligen Leben*). The wall's being is the wall in the infinite difference that separates it from itself, so that it reaches itself and its identity only in that difference. The example of the wall repeats essay I almost verbatim (p. 24), now with Fichte as the source of the formula. Henry's use of Fichte is double: the same *Anweisung* contains a doctrine of blessed life, the self-enjoyment of absolute life, that Henry's own thought is close to.

Why does exteriority define being? Because to be is to appear, to show oneself, and the unfolding of exteriority is the substance of appearance — the pure phenomenality of what appears, the appearing of the wall but first of appearing itself, the field in which appearing reaches intuition of itself, "le devenir visible de la visibilité, la lumière dans l'effectivité de son acte de luire" (p. 41). Exteriority shows itself in a self-showing that is nothing but its being-outside. "L'extériorité est en soi le pour soi." Henry grants the tradition its own form of reflexivity: exteriority is self-manifesting — but only as the light that shows itself by shining. The phrase "in itself, for itself" names the Hegelian absolute as the self-manifestation of exteriority; the whole of Henry's critique lies in denying that this self-showing is a true self-givenness.

That being as exteriority is no mere spatialization, a naive view of common consciousness, shows in the fact that space itself appears only within a transcendental horizon, which is the original departure of being out of itself, its first ek-stasis. As Kant says, space is itself in time, understood as the condition — that is, the phenomenality — of all phenomena. And time? Heidegger: "La temporalité est l'extériorité originelle en soi et pour soi" (p. 42, *Sein und Zeit* p. 329: *das ursprüngliche "Außer-sich" an und für sich selbst*). The interpretation of spirit as time that has guided Western philosophy since Hegel only reaffirms the presuppositions that have always determined it. The identification of exteriority with time, not space, is the key to Henry's critique. It forbids any reply that interiority might be found by withdrawing from space into time — the move of Bergson, of Husserl's inner time-consciousness, and of Kant's inner sense.

Has classical philosophy not escaped this, as a philosophy of consciousness since Descartes — consciousness as a subject opposed to the object, as or inhabited by a self, a subjective interior opposed to the world? Henry denies it, beginning with Descartes himself, in whom the moment of the cogito is fragile and fleeting. On the ego's belonging to the consciousness that says "I think, I doubt, I will," the *Second Meditation* merely asserts that it is so evident it needs no explanation. The absence of any serious inquiry into the ego's ipseity, at the very moment it is set at the centre of modern thought, explains modern thought's endless wavering: the self is included in consciousness or excluded from it with equal ease; Hamelin says "it is thought" instead of "I think," Merleau-Ponty "one feels" instead of "I feel," and today the subject is abruptly "evacuated from the problematic" and buried with pomp, though no one any longer knows whose funeral it is. Often a single author — Husserl, Sartre — asserts in turn the immanence and the transcendence of the ego to the field of consciousness. The sarcasm targets the structuralist "death of the subject" of the 1960s and 1970s; Henry's diagnosis is that the subject was never properly born, since its selfhood was never thematized. The point continues essay I (p. 36), where the same Hamelin/Merleau-Ponty pairing appears in Lichtenberg's form.

The being of the field of consciousness itself is equivocal. One must indeed set aside the illusion that consciousness is a particular reality, a subject opposed to an object. Understood in its pure concept, consciousness is not something that also has the property of being conscious but the fact of being conscious, the phenomenal condition — pure phenomenality, what Heidegger calls being. But the philosophy of consciousness presupposes, implicitly or explicitly, the same concept of being as Western thought generally and Heidegger in particular: being as exteriority. Fichte identifies the being of consciousness with being in general, the being of the wall: its self-exteriority, its opposition to itself, its representation — and consciousness is that representation. The subject is the phenomenal condition of the object, its objectivity. "La subjectivité du sujet n'est en Occident que l'objectivité de l'objet" (p. 43). The formula is the volume's sharpest statement of ontological monism. It also explains why Henry insists against Heidegger that Heidegger has not overcome the metaphysics of subjectivity: he has completed it, since he too defines the subject's being as the opening of exteriority.

Historically, the identification of subjectivity with exteriority passes through phases. In Descartes, the grasp of subjectivity as lived experience, a moment of life — legible in the decisive claim that "sentir, c'est encore penser" (p. 43, *Meditation II*; *Principles* I, § 9) — does not detain the philosopher long. As with ipseity, Descartes does not ask about the inner structure of subjectivity insofar as it may be identical with life. From the *Third Meditation* on, interest shifts to the relation of consciousness to its correlate, from cogito to cogitatum; consciousness is progressively reduced to a "movement toward," the opening of exteriority. The idea of God as intentional content, the cogitatum in general, its presentation in the clear and distinct idea and in evidence — in objectivity — becomes the goal, which aims at establishing the world's existence and grounding a theory of knowledge and science. Henry splits Descartes into two: the beginning (*Meditation II*, where feeling counts as thought, the cogito as self-feeling) and the beginning's betrayal (*Meditation III*, where evidence, i.e. seeing, takes over). The split governs *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*.

Husserl's grand resumption of the Cartesian project shows the same shift — despite reservations that concern only some transcendent constructions Descartes retained — from the material of consciousness, briefly considered as *hylé*, to intentionality, "l'irruption triomphante de l'extériorité" (p. 44). Husserl speaks of consciousness as a life; experience is what it lives, the *Erlebnis*. The lectures on inner time-consciousness try to grasp the substance of this life as a field of primal presence, the living present. But this present exceeds the abstract instant only because retentions and protentions are continually bound to it — and these, as intentionalities, are nothing but the first bursting-out of being beyond itself and its indefinite repetition. Husserl sees brilliantly that these intentionalities of original passive synthesis cannot give us life: offered to retention, life would be a life in the past; in protention, a life to come. The first upsurge of presence, life, must precede the constant sliding of the impression into the past; it lies in the impression itself as primal impression (*Urimpression*). Yet Husserl's thought "vient précisément mourir devant cette impression" (p. 44): he cannot grasp its inner essence, which is life's. The impression may well, as already in Kant and Hume, contain existence, but it presents itself as an opaque datum, uncomprehended in its being — the very opposite of life. For life is truth; it is living only as self-revelation, constituted through and through by that revelation. And remarkably, when Husserl wants to treat life by phenomenology's own prescriptions, he can entrust this revelation to no power but intentionality. The flow of consciousness becomes manifest to itself only through the longitudinal intentionalities running along it, which hold each phase in being and so give the flow to itself at every instant. The flow's marvellous being-for-itself is its constant intentional reference to itself; the first givenness, as self-givenness, has no site other than "le creux de l'extériorité" (p. 45). Phenomenology thus turns exclusively to problems of constitution, of transcendent being, which is always an intentional correlate, a cogitatum, and Husserl rejoins the paths, or the dead ends, of idealism and classical rationalism. This is the compact version of Henry's reading of the *Lectures on the Phenomenology of Inner Time-Consciousness*, worked out fully in *Phénoménologie matérielle* (1990). Its crux is the *Längsintentionalität* of § 39 of the lectures, by which the flow appears to itself: Husserl had the primal impression in hand but made its self-manifestation depend on retention, so that self-givenness became a modification of exteriority. On this reading Husserl's "genius" is to have glimpsed that life cannot be given by retention; his failure is to have had no other phenomenological means to give it.

Kant, already mentioned, shows the limits of this thought most clearly. His inability to grasp life, or even to anticipate its essence, appears in the critique of the paralogisms, which strips the concept of the soul — in fact identical with that of life — of all legitimacy. The *Critique* claims to take from the self its own real being on the ground that we know only phenomena and our self is one. But the demand for phenomenality that gives Kant's argument its force remains bound to the Western concept of being: to be a phenomenon is to be given to intuition and thought by the understanding, and both are representation, ek-static projection of an horizon of visibility. That the self's real being cannot be exhibited in an intuition and has no concept shows that it is unrepresentable — that ipseity is irreducible to exteriority, that Kant's premises are closed to the being of life. Kant also cannot ground even the phenomenal existence of the self: the content of inner sense is a blind impression that nothing links to a self, to this self rather than that one, or to a foreign being; its form is the empty form of representation, and nothing shows why an I must accompany it, especially since that representation, Kant admits, has "aucun contenu" (p. 46, A 381). Henry condenses essay I's argument and adds one equation: the soul is life. The phrase "nothing links it to this self rather than that one" makes singularity, again, the touchstone.

"Une forme vide, un contenu mort" (p. 46): this is the metaphysical situation of Western thought. Since being unfolds its essence through ek-stasis, it presents itself as a being without interior, uninhabited, offering only its outside, a surface without depth on which the gaze slides and dies. Every deepening of knowledge yields only a new objective appearance, a new outside, and being is the ek-static succession of appearances among which it scatters, each referring to another in an endless transcendence that is inseparable from the tireless accomplishment of exteriority, that is, from time. It is not only the spatial thing that by its own necessity is given through an open series of adumbrations, its being an ideal pole beyond them; every possible being obeys "la loi de la dispersion." In the world all existence is alienated, broken, indifferent, opaque, contingent, absurd. It is broken when it exists only outside itself as its own image, a representation — the ground of idealism; lost when what makes it actual lies outside it, in its exteriority to itself; alienated when the law of its development is no longer its own but a foreign spirit's; contingent when it no longer builds itself from within. And my body, once it is no longer the self-united upsurge of movement and desire — what I am and do — and resolves, outside itself, into a strange juxtaposition of parts and an unjustifiable assembly of functions, offers consciousness only "le paradoxe d'une configuration imbécile" (p. 47). But the abandonment of what shows itself in the light of ek-stasis refers back to that light and to its mode of unveiling. "Toute transcendance est le principe d'une facticité insurmontable, l'objectivité est pour la vie le plus grand ennemi." Henry turns the existentialist catalogue of absurdity — Sartre's *Nausea* is audible in "contingent, absurd" and in the body as an "imbecile configuration" — into a symptom of a mode of appearing. Facticity and absurdity are not features of existence as such but effects of the ek-stasis in which existence is shown. The adumbration doctrine of Husserl's *Ideas I* is generalized: not only things but every being given in exteriority is given in profiles, so that dispersion is the law of the world's phenomenality.

"L'être objectif dénué de raison est l'unique texte du rationalisme" (p. 47) — an echo of Kant's "unique text of rational psychology" (essay I, p. 21). Rationalism seeks evidence and proof everywhere, which means bringing what must be established into evidence. Since all certainty comes from objectification, Heidegger could think that modern technology fulfils the teleology of rationalism and discloses its nature: the will to subjugate beings by making them objects, an action of which scientific theory is a prime form. If this action ends in the devastation of the earth, it is because technology is blind to the essence in which beings rest. But that essence is exteriority itself, which makes beings objects; the devastation of the world in the age of modern theory follows from Greek theory, and technology belongs to the history of being. Henry accepts Heidegger's genealogy of technology (from Greek *theoria* to the *Gestell*) and turns it against Heidegger: if the devastation follows from the essence of being as unveiling, then Heidegger's own thought of being belongs to the history it diagnoses. *La barbarie* (1987) develops this into a critique of technoscience as life's self-negation.

The attempt to reject rationalism fails as long as it rests on the same ontological premises. So it went, mid-century, with the philosophy of existence. Replacing triangles and axioms with historical and bodily existence, relation to others, anguish and death changes less than it seems if historicity is only the accomplishment of ek-stasis and death its correlate, if the body is defined by intentionality, if anguish remains uncomprehended in its innermost possibility, the affectivity of life within it. The dereliction of existence delivered to the world, the dehiscence of a present gnawed by nothingness, the "je ne suis pas ce que je suis" — "tout ce pseudo-pathos" would not have aged so soon if it had expressed something other than the old reign of exteriority and found the way to life (p. 47). The targets are named by their formulas: Heidegger's thrownness, Sartre's being-for-itself ("is what it is not and is not what it is"), Merleau-Ponty's "dehiscence." The term "pseudo-pathos" is precise: their affective vocabulary is borrowed from an affectivity their ontology cannot ground, since anguish, as an affect, presupposes life's self-feeling.

"Car la vie demeure en elle-même" (p. 47). Life has no outside; no face of its being offers itself to a theoretical or sensible gaze or as object for any action. "Nul n'a jamais vu la vie et ne la verra jamais" (p. 48). Life is a dimension of radical immanence, which, insofar as we can think it, excludes all exteriority, the transcendental horizon of visibility called the world. Life is invisible. But "invisible" is adequate only if absolutely distinguished from an invisible that is a limit-mode of the visible, a degree within the system of consciousness. Thus the Husserlian impression, after its retentional modification, runs through ever deeper temporal positions with diminishing clarity, finally sinking into the "unconscious"; thus too the temporal flow reproduced in imagination or memory keeps its phenomenological differences, though, given only in the mode of the "quasi," it suffers a further deficit specific to reproduction. These are degrees of consciousness, not life. To dissociate life's invisible from the declining modes of the world's phenomenality, one criterion suffices: any mode of objectivity or consciousness can turn into another; confused, obscure or marginal consciousness can become clear, distinct, finally evident. Every process of elucidation, every becoming-conscious, "qu'elle soit celle de l'intellectualisme ou de la psychanalyse," rests on this possibility. What belongs to life as invisible, by contrast, can in principle never become a determination of the visible. "La vie n'est ni consciente, ni subconsciente, ni inconsciente et elle n'est pas non plus susceptible de le devenir" (p. 48). The criterion of non-convertibility is the decisive conceptual tool of the essay. It separates Henry's invisible from Husserl's horizon of the "unconscious" (the end of the retentional chain), from the Freudian unconscious understood as representation that has not yet become conscious, and from any "degree" of appearing. *Généalogie de la psychanalyse* will reread Freud's unconscious through this criterion: the true unconscious is life, which never becomes conscious because it never needs to appear in representation.

For the same reason, life's invisible has nothing to do with an original non-truth underlying all truth. If unveiling arises from and presupposes the unveiled, both belong to a world and must be understood from it. Still less is the invisible the mere negation of the visible, the hypostasis of a negative term passing for being. "Si la vie n'a pas de visage, elle n'est pas rien, elle n'est pas la simple privation de la phénoménalité" (p. 49). The target is Heidegger's *lethe* as the concealment at the heart of *aletheia* — the self-withdrawal of being in which unconcealment originates. For Henry, Heidegger's concealment is only the shadow of the world's light, still measured by unveiling; life's invisibility is a positive, full mode of phenomenality.

"La vie se sent, s'éprouve elle-même" (p. 49). Not as something that also has the property of feeling itself: that is its essence, pure self-experience, and the essence of life lies in auto-affection. Because auto-affection is the concept of life, it must be thought rigorously — and rigour is lost when it designates inner sense, as in Kant and then Heidegger. With inner sense, the inmost being of subjectivity, what makes it life, was indeed in question. But both authors went astray, as shown by the fact that the affection occurring in inner sense is the affection of time itself by the three-dimensional horizon it projects ek-statically — an affection of time by itself, which is why Heidegger calls it, in Kantian terms, "self-affection." Yet this affection of time by the horizon it projects and receives is affection by the original medium of otherness, "la mise en image d'un monde," the world in its pure worldhood; transcendental exteriority is its only content. Such an affection is sensibility in its specific structure, since a sense always means affection by something foreign to the power that senses. Life, on the contrary, in its first affection, is affected by nothing other than itself; it constitutes the content it receives. It is no self-positing, no self-objectification; it does not set itself before itself to affect itself in a self-seeing or self-perceiving, as an object would be manifested. Life affects itself, is for itself, without setting itself before itself "dans l'objection de l'ekstase"; it feels itself without any sense, inner or otherwise (pp. 49–50). And this original auto-affection, in a truly radical sense — absolute immanence excluding all intentional rupture and transcendence — is no postulate of thought. We do not construct being logically or dialectically, nor seek its conditions by reflexive analysis; we do not say "it must be that" subjectivity is for itself, that life makes itself be as the simple and permanent, the One and the self-same, before anything else can affect it. Phenomenology has the means to confront the ultimate problems, and in a sense it alone can. What feels and experiences itself without the mediation of a sense is, in its essence, affectivity. "L'affectivité est l'essence originaire de la révélation" (p. 50), the phenomenological auto-affection of being and its first upsurge. That is why the invisible is not the antithetical, formal and empty concept of phenomenality but its actualization "dans l'effectivité du sentiment," and why it cannot turn into the visible or run through degrees of consciousness: where life reigns in the actuality of self-feeling there is not, and cannot be, any exteriority. This explains what psychologists, and a deep philosopher like Scheler, observe: attention disturbs the normal play of our feelings. Not because consciousness intervenes in our life, but because the intentional gaze is in principle unable to discover life in its reality, in the radical interiority of its affective auto-affection. "L'être, nous devons le dire avec force contre Heidegger, n'est pas ce qui doit être pensé, il ne peut l'être" (p. 50). No modification of thought, no new way of interpreting reality, can change anything in reality insofar as it lies in life. "Ce n'est pas la conscience des hommes qui détermine leur vie" — not because consciousness is imperfect but because the medium in which it moves, being laid out before it to be gathered in perception and offered to intelligibility, excludes the essence of life (pp. 50–51). One must therefore smile at rationalism's claim to change people's lives and history through a becoming-conscious, a gain in knowledge or objectivity. Real change can occur only where reality unfolds its essence, in life, through it; such change, arising in life as its own movement and set against the impotence of all theory and ideology, "nous le nommons praxis" (p. 51). The distinction between Heidegger's self-affection of time (affection by the horizon, thus hetero-affection) and Henry's auto-affection (identity of affecting and affected) is drawn with full precision; the *Kantbuch*'s § 34 is the explicit target, as in essay I. Henry also insists that auto-affection is not a transcendental condition inferred by regress ("it must be that…") but a phenomenon; the methodological claim distinguishes his project from Fichte's and from reflexive philosophy, and anticipates the debate over whether a "phenomenology of the invisible" is possible at all (the objection of Janicaud's *Le tournant théologique*, 1991). The anti-Heideggerian thesis that being cannot be thought — and that changing thought changes nothing real — joins Marx's sentence to Henry's 1976 reading of Marx: praxis is life's own self-transformation, and the "consciousness" Marx subordinates to life is representational consciousness. Scheler's observation on attention is redescribed: it is not that attention distorts feeling, but that feeling was never available to attention.

What is this movement of life, willed from life and tirelessly producing what it produces? First, it is in principle individual: it is the individual's own transformation and results from him as his own work. Because life finds its being-alive in affective auto-affection, it is monadic. Auto-affection is the essence of ipseity if the Self is the fact of feeling oneself, "l'identité de l'affectant et de l'affecté" (p. 51). Life is not like a river indifferent to the wheels it turns; being need not negate itself in its universality to give itself the moment of particularity. On the contrary, the particular is the essence of being, its innermost possibility, the unfolding of its positivity. A self differs from another not by natural, psychic or spiritual qualities — sensitivity, intelligence, place or time of birth — and the *principium individuationis* owes nothing to the categories of exteriority. A self differs because it is itself originally, in and through its auto-affection. This being-oneself in affectivity relates every life to itself, makes it life, and at the same time opposes it to every other "dans la suffisance absolue de son individualité radicale." The river image targets Schopenhauer's will and Hegel's universal (and, in the background, Heidegger's anonymous *es gibt*), where individuation is a negation or limitation of the universal. Henry inverts the hierarchy: individuality is not derived from life but is its essence. "Absolute sufficiency" is the formula that later Henry will qualify: from the mid-1980s, the living self is no longer self-sufficient but generated in an absolute Life it does not produce (essays III, VI, IX). The last paragraph of this essay already turns in that direction.

Does each individual life not depend on its environment and on the circumstances it traverses, chance events for it? Is it not affected at each instant by the world and determined by that affection? But affection of the individual by the manifold of sensibility presupposes affection by the world in which that manifold is felt, sensibility itself; and sensibility, the ek-stasis of the horizon, presupposes the auto-affection of the act that opens us to the world — life and its affectivity. Whatever affects us in the world can do so only insofar as its coming is first the coming of life into itself, its limitless experience in feeling. "Voilà pourquoi rien de visible ne nous advient qui ne soit aussi un invisible" (p. 52) — the gloss on Kafka's invisible mouthful. Because affectivity is the essence of affection, what we feel, determined each time by the affecting object, is overdetermined by life's actuality in us. Life ultimately accounts for what we undergo, that is, for itself. Superficial explanations must be refused: birth trauma, the vicissitudes of infantile or adult sexuality do not cause our anguish. Something like anguish, any affective tone, can arise only in a being originally constituted as auto-affection, finding its essence in life and affectivity. Many things in the world arouse our sufferings and joys, but only because suffering and joy can take shape in us as possibilities of our life and fundamental modes of its phenomenological actualization. The argument is a transcendental regress once more (affection presupposes sensibility, which presupposes self-affection), and it yields a strong thesis about causation: worldly causes occasion affects but do not produce their affectivity. The swipe at Otto Rank's birth trauma and at Freud anticipates *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*, where anguish is traced to life's own excess, its inability to escape itself.

How does life bear fundamental affective tones as its own modes? Experiencing itself in the radical immanence of auto-affection, life is essentially passive toward itself, bound to itself, unable to break that bond or take any distance from itself. That is what characterizes life: the impossibility of escaping itself, of keeping a fallback position behind itself into which it might withdraw from its own being and from what may be oppressive in it. Insofar as life is driven back against itself in the insurmountable passivity of an uninterruptible self-experience, it is a suffering, "le « se souffrir soi-même » dans et par lequel elle est livrée irrémédiablement à elle-même" (pp. 52–53). The verb *souffrir* carries its double sense here — to suffer, and to bear or endure (as in "souffrir quelque chose"). Suffering is not first an unpleasant feeling but the structure of undergoing oneself. The same passivity was the end-point of essay I (pp. 36–37); essay VII develops it fully.

Yet in this self-suffering, life experiences itself, reaches itself, is given to itself in the perfect adherence of being riveted to itself; it swells with its own content, enjoys itself, "elle est la jouissance, elle est la joie" (p. 53). The fundamental dichotomy of affectivity — the spontaneous division of affects into "positive" and "negative" tones — is no empirical curiosity or natural fact; it is rooted in life's essence and expresses it. We then understand the course of life, the possibility of each affect passing into the other. Joy follows pain not only because a favourable event follows an unfavourable one in the world, but first because joy can follow pain; and this possibility of passage is their common possibility, the essence from which both derive — as Kierkegaard found when he disclosed, at the bottom of despair, the essence of life identical with beatitude and leading to it. The polarity of suffering and joy is grounded in a single structure: the same self-adherence is undergone as weight and enjoyed as fullness. *L'essence de la manifestation* had worked out this "unity of suffering and joy"; the Kierkegaard of *The Sickness unto Death* supplies the template: despair is the self's failed attempt to be rid of itself, and its cure is the self's resting transparently in the power that posits it — which is exactly how this essay ends.

The passage from suffering to joy brings us before time. Life is temporality, but its temporality is hard to think. Modern philosophy greatly advanced the thought of time, yet produced not a phenomenology of life's temporality but only one of the consciousness of time — something quite different. A phenomenology of time-consciousness treats time as representation and finally as the very structure of representation, the original bursting of being into exteriority. Its ontological deficiency is that it moves in pure unreality. The pure positions of future and past are unreal, and so is what appears in them; unreal too the present itself, if defined as a consciousness of the present, an ek-static horizon, an exteriority. The real present, the living present, is the phenomenological actualization of auto-affection — "l'impression, si l'on veut," but grasped in its essence and innermost possibility, the radical immanence of its affectivity (pp. 53–54). The sliding of the impression into the past substitutes for life's immanent temporality a consciousness of time in which the substance of the present flows out of it — a flight of actuality into exteriority already at work, which will dominate post-Husserlian thought as it dominates modern thought generally. Henry reclaims Husserl's own phrase "living present" (*lebendige Gegenwart*) against Husserl: living, because it is the impression's self-affection, not because it is held between retention and protention.

What then is the real temporality phenomenology could not think — the temporality of the impression itself, its self-movement, which is not the departure out of itself and out of us of what was alive in us, not the leap into the unreal, death at every instant, but life's movement occurring within it as its very interiority and never ceasing to belong to it? Is the concept not contradictory? What can "passing" mean if everything is there and remains there in auto-affection's indissoluble bond to itself, if what passes does not separate from itself, if what passes is life remaining in itself? Henry formulates the paradox the rest of the essay must resolve: a movement without departure, a time without ek-stasis.

Consider my hand — not the objective hand, which touches nothing and has never done anything, but the subjective power of grasping, the fundamental "I can" whose auto-affection is our original corporeity. Since birth, this power has made many movements we now call past. Where are they? Have they fled like the Husserlian impression, sliding into an ever more obscure past and finally into the unconscious? If they return, will it be as memories, in the unreal mode of a representation that never gives the original? Or are all the acts of grasping we have made, and will make, always already there, nothing but the power's auto-affection and its potential actualization? When the actualization ends, the act does not fall outside us onto the fleeing line of time; it remains in us as the power's remaining in itself, as the latent tension that defines our being and is never abolished (pp. 54–55). What remains and is no longer conscious is not the material trace, the dead being "éclaté - juxtaposé - rassemblé" in the mythological unconscious of classical thought or its Freudian sequels. What remains, and is no longer conscious and never was, is life's remaining-in-itself in its hidden being. In it all of life's powers remain in themselves as actual powers, ready to unfold, constantly joining themselves, and I can join them — I who am only their being-given-to-themselves, their life. Being can no longer be thought in terms of exteriority. Everything that lives and acts proceeds from what remains in itself, as what remains, in the form of iteration and repetition. "Rien ne revient mais tout provient, dans le présent vivant, du pouvoir de la vie" (p. 55). This is Henry's alternative to retention as the model of memory: Biran's theory of habit and motor memory (bodily power as a repository of the past) reinterpreted as the self-retention of life. The opposition "nothing returns but everything proceeds" is aimed at the Husserlian model of recollection as return of a representation, and at the Freudian model of the "return of the repressed." The "unconscious" of powers that are always there but never represented is what *Généalogie* will call the true unconscious, coextensive with life.

In Kafka's parable "The Next Village," an old man on his doorstep watches people pass and thinks that if they knew how short life is, they would not even set out for the next village, for they would understand they lack the time. The text signifies the unreality of time. Looking back, our past life reduces to nothing; there is only this instant; the future too is nothing. If we tried to find ourselves in that time — to return, despite Kafka's warning, to the village of our childhood — we would find nothing that is ourselves, "comme les croisés devant le tombeau vide d'un dieu" (p. 55). Life is interiority, and no one will ever find it in exteriority. Kafka's parable is read as a reductio of ek-static time: measured in its own currency, a whole life has no extension. The crusaders at the empty tomb image a search for life in the world's places — the Gospel's question "Why do you seek the living among the dead?" (Luke 24:5) stands behind it, anticipating the Christological turn of the 1990s.

Foreign to time and exteriority, eternal — "l'éternité n'est autre que le lien indissoluble de l'auto-affection" — life nevertheless shows its own temporality, the real temporality whose concept we seek. It consists not only in the differentiated actualization of life's potentialities, nor only in the continual passage of tones into one another — a passage in which none disappears but remains in what "follows" it as its essence and possibility, so that through all these modes life never ceases to experience itself. Life's most original temporality must be understood from its fundamental passivity. Passivity toward itself means passivity toward its own ground. That life experiences itself means it has not posited the content of its self-affection; it undergoes it as what it did not posit but is given and constantly given to it, coming into it from what it is not. Nor has life posited its essence, the fact that it comes into itself and constantly experiences itself in self-enjoyment. Life is nothing but the passivity of this coming-into-itself, and the endless movement of this coming "c'est le temps." What comes does not come from the future: what comes is life's coming into itself, as life experiences it in experiencing itself, so that, plunging through the transparency of its affectivity, life "plonge dans la puissance qui la pose et ne cesse de la poser" (pp. 55–56). Henry's thought turns here within a single essay: the "absolute sufficiency" of individual life (p. 51) gives way to a life that has not posited itself and is constantly given to itself by a power it is not. The formula is Kierkegaard's (the self "grounded transparently in the power that established it," *The Sickness unto Death*), and it announces the doctrine of the 1990s: the living self generated in absolute Life (*C'est moi la vérité*, 1996; essays III, VI and IX here). Time is redefined as this coming of life into itself, not as an horizon: the future is replaced by advent. The identification of eternity with auto-affection's bond is Eckhartian, as in *L'essence de la manifestation*.

These reflections will seem metaphysical, outdated, foreign to the world science thematizes and whose objectivity seems to guarantee its reality. Is a philosophy of life not suspect to science, turning its back on our culture's effort at rationality? But what if reason itself were grounded in life? What if its concrete categories could not be deduced a priori from pure thought but were only the expression and representation of life's categories — if causality, for instance, which reason uses constantly but cannot account for, were nothing other than our original corporeity, the fundamental "I can" we are? What if the supreme proof, the ultimate "it is true that" presupposed by all knowledge and all statements, were the original revelation that resides in life and constitutes its essence? The causality thesis comes straight from Biran (essay I, pp. 31–32): the felt effort is the origin of the concept of cause. The deeper claim — that the "it is true that" of every judgement rests on life's self-revelation — anticipates Henry's distinction between the truth of the world and the truth of life (*C'est moi la vérité*) and, in *La barbarie*, his account of science as a mode of life that forgets its own ground.

And the objective world? Could it still rest on objectivity if the phenomena that form its texture were explained neither by themselves nor by the medium of their phenomenality — if, say, a radical critique of political economy showed that economic determinations, their variations and their persistence, derive not from economic causality but from the power of life, which is their true naturing source (*naturant*)? The economic universe, where everything happens on the surface and in daylight, would then be the unreal, arbitrary, impotent and finally fantastical double of what takes place elsewhere, in "le laboratoire secret de la production," as Marx says (p. 57, *Capital* I), in the invisible subjectivity of living individuals. The properties of that subjectivity, the laws of life, account for the laws of economics, and the economic realities serving as their objective equivalents are built from living subjectivity, though that equivalence and the rationality it grounds can never cease to be illusory. The world's great malaise will be dispelled not by scientific progress but by new forms of life. A philosophy of life is no survival of an objectless metaphysics; it alone can guide the transcendental gaze to an inner understanding of this otherwise enigmatic world, as it alone can open to each the path that leads to himself. The conclusion compresses Henry's *Marx*: living labour, subjective and individual, is the real source of value; economic objectivity is its "ideal double." The Spinozist term *naturant* (*natura naturans*) makes life the producing nature behind the produced economic world. The final sentence reconnects the social diagnosis to the self: the same invisible life that grounds the economy is the path each person takes to himself — a theme taken up in essay V's remarks on economics and in essay XI on community.

## III. Phenomenology of Life

The phenomenology of life belongs to the movement founded by Husserl at the end of the nineteenth century and continued by Heidegger and Scheler, still alive today, especially in France. Henry will show how it depends on that movement and where it departs from it. The lecture, given at the Munich Academy of Fine Arts in November 2000, two years before Henry's death, is the latest text in the volume and its most compact summary: it presupposes *Incarnation* (2000) and *C'est moi la vérité* (1996). The double claim of filiation and departure governs everything that follows: Henry presents himself not as a critic of phenomenology from outside but as its radicalizer.

Phenomenology's originality lies in its object. Other sciences study specific phenomena — physical, chemical, biological, historical, legal, social, economic; phenomenology asks what makes a phenomenon a phenomenon: pure phenomenality, variously named manifestation, showing, unveiling, uncovering, appearing, revelation, or, traditionally, truth. Once its object is so distinguished, a further distinction seems required: between the phenomenon in its particular content and its phenomenality — between what appears and appearing as such. Heidegger formulates it in § 44 of *Sein und Zeit*, separating truth in the secondary sense (what is true, what is unveiled) from unveiling itself, "das ursprünglichste Phänomen der Wahrheit" (p. 60). The list of synonyms is not idle: by placing "revelation" and "truth" alongside "appearing," Henry prepares the identification of phenomenality with the Johannine truth that ends the lecture. The distinction between what appears and appearing is the one Henry will show to collapse in life.

A second founding intuition of phenomenology is that appearing is more essential than being: a thing can be only because it appears. Henry gives Husserl's formula, borrowed from the Marburg School and slightly modified, as "Autant d'apparaître, autant d'être" ("so much appearing, so much being"; the original maxim reads "so much seeming, so much being"). Henry radicalizes this priority of phenomenology over ontology: something can appear only if appearing appears in itself, as such. Yet the principles remain undetermined — "so much appearing, so much being," "to the things themselves" — as long as it is not made clear in what appearing consists, what concrete phenomenological mode appearing takes, the pure phenomenological "matter" in which phenomenality becomes phenomenal. Behind this indeterminacy, and thanks to it, historical phenomenology slips in a particular conception of phenomenality, the one ordinary thought first offers and the oldest, least critical prejudice of traditional philosophy: phenomenality borrowed from the perception of worldly objects, ultimately the appearing of the world itself. "Matter" (*matière*) is the key term of *Phénoménologie matérielle* (1990): the question is not what appears, nor even that appearing is, but what appearing is made of — its phenomenological substance. The "radicalization" — that appearing must itself appear — is the step classical phenomenology never took; it took for granted that appearing is a seeing, a light, without asking how the light shows itself. Henry's later formulation of this as the "reversal of phenomenology" (*Incarnation*, Part I) is in nuce here.

It may be hard to accept that for Husserl, too, the answer to the explicitly posed question of the "how" of givenness (objects "in their how") is the appearing of the world. Does Husserl not refer phenomenality, traditionally, to consciousness and so to a kind of "interiority"? But consciousness is defined as intentionality, and intentional consciousness is nothing but the movement by which it throws itself outside; its "substance" is exhausted in this coming-outside that produces phenomenality. To reveal by coming outside, by setting at a distance, is to make visible (*faire-voir*), and the object is phenomenologically what is set before and so made visible. Appearing here is the object's appearing in a double sense: what appears is the object, and the mode of appearing is the object's — setting at a distance. The unavoidable question follows: how does intentionality, which makes everything visible, reveal itself to itself? By another intentionality aimed at it? Then phenomenology suffers the bitter fate of classical philosophy of consciousness, an infinite regress of consciousnesses behind consciousness. Or is there another mode of revelation, whose phenomenality is no longer that of the outside? Phenomenology has no answer, and a grave crisis arises, leading to aporia: phenomenality itself becomes problematic if its principle escapes it. As is known, Husserl had in the end to consign the constituting ego to "anonymity." Henry's argument is the regress objection to higher-order theories of self-awareness, which he takes to be fatal to intentional phenomenology as such. The final allusion is to the late Husserl's "anonymous" functioning ego, the ego that functions without being given to itself, a theme developed by Fink. For Henry the anonymity is not a profound discovery but a confession of failure: the principle of phenomenality has slipped out of phenomenality.

Heidegger brings the appearing of the world to its highest elaboration. From § 7 of *Sein und Zeit*, the phenomenon is understood from the Greek *phainomenon*, from the root *pha-*, *phōs*, light, so that to appear means to come into the light, "ce à l'intérieur de quoi quelque chose peut devenir visible, manifeste en lui-même" (p. 62). The world is the ek-static horizon of visibilization within which everything can become visible, and the second division of *Sein und Zeit* explicitly identifies that horizon with exteriority: the world is temporality, and temporality "das ursprüngliche 'Außer-sich' an und für sich selbst" (p. 329). The same quotation anchored essay II (p. 42). Henry's reading of § 7 stresses the etymology of light because "light" is the whole problem: if appearing means coming into the light, what is invisible can only be what is not yet or no longer shown.

The appearing of the world has three decisive traits. Listing them introduces the phenomenology of life, whose first thesis will be that no life can appear in the appearing of the world. Henry's procedure is a contrastive phenomenology: each trait of world-appearing will be matched, and reversed, by a trait of life's revelation.

First, since the world's appearing consists in being-outside-itself, everything that shows itself in it shows itself outside: as exterior, as other, as different. Exterior, because the structure is exteriority. Other, because ek-stasis is a primordial otherness: whatever is outside me is other than me, whatever is outside itself is other than itself. Different, because this ek-stasis is identically a Difference, the operation that, opening a distance, makes different whatever appears thanks to it. Such appearing turns away from itself with such violence, expels with such force, being nothing but the original expulsion of an Outside, that whatever it lets appear can only be exterior "au sens terrible" — thrown out, driven from its true dwelling, its homeland, deprived of what is most its own, abandoned, without support, lost, "la proie de ce délaissement auquel Heidegger devait livrer l'homme pour en avoir fait, en tant qu'« être-au-monde », un être de ce monde, rien de plus" (pp. 62–63). The capital "Difference" glances at Heidegger's ontological difference and Derrida's *différance*: both are, for Henry, names for the work of ek-stasis. Thrownness and "dereliction" are read not as existential truths but as the effects of a mode of appearing that throws whatever it shows. The vocabulary of exile ("dwelling," "homeland") prepares the Johannine theme of the Son's home in the Father.

Second, the appearing that unveils in the world's Difference does not only make different what it unveils; it is, in principle, totally indifferent to it — neither loving nor desiring nor protecting it, having no affinity with it. Clouding sky or the equality of a circle's radii, a goat or a seaplane, an image or a real thing, the formula of the universe's secret — it does not matter. Like the Light of Scripture that shines on the just and unjust alike, the world's appearing lights everything "sans faire acception des choses ou des personnes, dans une neutralité terrifiante" (p. 63). Victims and executioners, charity and genocide, rules and exceptions, wind, water, earth: all stand before us in the same way, in the ultimate mode we express as "it is," "there is." The scriptural echo (Matthew 5:45) is turned around: the evenhandedness that in the Gospel signifies the Father's love signifies here the world's indifference. The list juxtaposing victims and executioners turns the *es gibt* into an ethical charge: an appearing that shows everything alike cannot ground any difference of value.

Third, this indifference — which makes the world's appearing anything but a Father to his Sons, a brother to his brothers, a friend to his friends (one who knows all that his friend knows, a brother who knows all that his brothers know, beginning with the first of them, the Firstborn Son) — conceals a more radical poverty: the world's appearing cannot confer existence on what it unveils. This incapacity explains its indifference; indifference and neutrality here mean, and derive from, impotence. Heidegger, who first thought the world phenomenologically as pure appearing, recognized both the indifference (anguish, in which everything becomes indifferent) and the impotence: unveiling unveils, discovers, "opens," but does not create (*macht nicht*). Thus appears the ontological poverty of the world's appearing, unable to posit reality by itself. The parentheses about the friend and the brother allude to John 15:15 ("all that I have heard from my Father I have made known to you") and to Romans 8:29 (the firstborn among many brothers), cited explicitly on p. 68. The third trait is the decisive one: the world can show but not give being. "Ontological poverty" repeats the Kantian "ontological indigence" of essay I (p. 13), now said of appearing itself.

This poverty is not peculiar to Heidegger; it is already in Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason*, which understood the question of the world as phenomenological and gives a rigorous description of the world's phenomenological structure. That structure is co-constituted by the a priori forms of the pure intuitions of space and time and by the categories of the understanding. "Forms of pure intuition" means pure ways of making-visible, considered independently of the contingent "empirical" content they make visible; "a priori" means they precede all actual experience. Beyond their specificity (substance, causality, reciprocal action), the categories share the same basic phenomenological meaning: they belong to making-visible and make it possible by securing its unity. The structure of that unifying power is the same as that of the pure intuitions: making-visible by placing outside. In Kant's decisive claim, forms of intuition and categories are both representations — in German *Vor-stellen*, "placing before." What matters is the *Critique*'s repeated thesis that the phenomenological formation of the world by these joint acts of making-visible can never posit by itself the reality that is the world's concrete content — a reality Kant had to ask of sensation. The paragraph reprises essay I's reading of Kant (pp. 11–13), now as a phenomenology of the world rather than a critique of the soul.

But the appeal to sensation as sole source of reality conceals an appeal to life, to a radically different mode of appearing. Life is phenomenological through and through: neither a being nor a mode of being of beings. It is not the life of biology — which, since the Galilean revolution, no longer speaks of life, its object being reduced to material processes homogeneous with physics. The only life that exists is "la vie phénoménologique transcendantale," the original mode of pure phenomenality, which Henry will henceforth call revelation (pp. 64–65). Life's revelation is opposed trait for trait to the world's appearing. Whereas the latter unveils in being-outside-itself, so that everything it unveils is exterior, other, different, life's revelation — bearing no gap and never differing from itself — reveals only itself. "La vie est une autorévélation" (p. 65). Self-revelation means two things: life itself accomplishes the work of revelation, being anything but a thing; and what it reveals is itself. The opposition between what appears and pure appearing, present in classical thought and foregrounded by phenomenology, disappears in the case of life: its revelation and what is revealed in it are one. The answer to Kant's sensation is given in one step: what makes sensation the bearer of reality is that it is felt, that is, lived — life's self-revelation, not the forms that receive it. The verdict on biology summarizes the argument of *C'est moi la vérité*: science, having set aside sensible qualities since Galileo, has no access to the living as living. The collapse of the appearing/what-appears distinction in life is the central thesis of the late Henry: in life, the "how" and the "what" coincide.

Wherever there is Life one meets this extraordinary situation, even in the humblest impression. Take a pain. Ordinary apprehension first takes it as "physical pain," referred to a part of the objective body. Perform on it the reduction that retains only its painful character, the "painful as such," the purely affective element of suffering. This "pure" suffering reveals itself: only suffering lets us know what suffering is, and what is revealed in that revelation is suffering. That the world's being-outside-itself is absent from this mode of life shows in that no gap separates suffering from itself: driven back against itself, crushed under its own weight, it cannot establish any retreat, any line of escape from itself and what is oppressive in its being. With no inner gap, the very possibility of directing a gaze at it is excluded. "Personne n'a jamais vu sa souffrance, son angoisse ou sa joie" (p. 65). Suffering, like every modality of life, is invisible. The reduction applied here is not Husserl's (suspending the world's existence to keep its sense) but its inverse: Henry suspends the objective reference of pain to keep its reality — the affective self-givenness that no noematic sense captures.

"Invisible" thus does not designate a dimension of unreality or illusion, some fantasized back-world, but its opposite. It is the world's appearing that, throwing everything outside itself, strips it of reality, reducing it to a series of exterior appearances with no "inside," each referring to another, as empty as itself, in the indefinite play of references that is the world. By Heidegger's own admission, the world's appearing cannot create what it unveils. In life, by contrast, each modality is a reality — "abrupte, immédiate, incontestable, invincible" (p. 66). But as soon as I try to see it, it vanishes. I can form an image of my suffering and represent it to myself; yet its reality is never outside it. In the representation I am before a noematic unreality, the meaning "suffering." Only when all distance is abolished, when suffering undergoes itself in pure suffering and joy in pure enjoying, is there actual suffering, revelation and reality one. Henry anticipates the Nietzschean charge of a "back-world" (*Hinterwelt*): the invisible is not behind the world but more real than it. The distinction between suffering and the noematic sense "suffering" is the decisive one for the critique of intentional analysis of affects: Husserl and Scheler describe the sense of feelings, never their reality.

The third trait opposing life's revelation to the world's appearing: whereas the latter differs from whatever it lets show itself and is indifferent to it, life keeps within itself what it reveals; it remains in it, in each living being, as what makes it live, and never leaves it as long as it lives. A new relation, foreign to the world, proper and internal to life, must now be considered: the hitherto unthought relation of life to the living, without which nothing can be understood of the living being we are. Foreign to the world, acosmic, invisible, it is a relation of absolute immanence — for how could there be a living being that did not carry life within it? But there is also the question why there is a living one in life, why no life is possible that would be anonymous, impersonal, foreign to all individuality. This question, like that of immanence, is not metaphysical, a matter for speculation or endless debate; it belongs to phenomenology, to a phenomenology of life, of which it becomes the central question. It is also an original question, which compels us to go back to an absolute life, "la Vie dont parle Jean" (p. 66). The relation of life to the living is the late Henry's reformulation of the ipseity problem. Essay I asked how a self belongs to its powers; essay II answered with the identity of affecting and affected. The question now becomes why there is any self at all in life — and the answer can no longer be found in finite life, which does not give itself to itself.

Absolute Life is the life that has the power to bring itself into life. Life is not; it comes, and never ceases to come. This coming is its eternal arriving in itself, the process in which it gives itself to itself, "s'écrase contre soi," experiences and enjoys itself, constantly producing its essence, which consists in this self-experience and self-enjoyment (p. 67). No experience occurs as self-experience unless it generates, in its very accomplishment, the Ipseity in which it experiences and enjoys itself. Since we speak not of the concept of life but of a real, phenomenologically actual life, the Ipseity in which it comes into itself is also phenomenologically actual: a real Self, "le Premier Soi Vivant," in which Life, experiencing itself, reveals itself to itself — its Word. Life's self-generation is thus accomplished as its self-revelation, which does not come at the end of the process but belongs to it, consubstantial with it, as an immanent condition of its effectuation. "Au commencement était le Verbe." No life without a living one, the Self that every life carries as self-experience; but no Self without the Life in which it is given to itself, so that outside life no Self is possible. Does this analysis of absolute Life not take us away from phenomenology, which keeps to concrete phenomena we live, into speculation, dogmatics, belief? Have we not yielded to "the theological turn of French phenomenology" denounced by Dominique Janicaud? Life "is not" but "comes": Henry replaces being with advent, against Heidegger's ontology but also against any substance metaphysics of God. The Self is not added to life but generated in its self-experience as its condition; this is the phenomenological rendering of the Trinitarian generation of the Son ("consubstantial"). Henry names Janicaud's 1991 polemic himself; his answer, in the next units, is that the structure of absolute Life is read off our own living, not posited by faith.

But are we not living beings too — living in the sense of a life that experiences itself, not a complex of material processes that know nothing of themselves, and living Selves? The strange analogy between the inner process of absolute Life experiencing itself in the Self of the First Living and our own life revealing itself in the singular Self each of us is becomes less extraordinary once their difference is established. The appeal to our own experience is Henry's methodological reply to Janicaud: absolute Life is not inferred but is implied in what each of us undergoes in living, as the next unit shows.

Our life is finite, unable to bring itself into itself, and its Self is finite too. Husserl, in a manuscript of the 1930s: "Ich bin nicht nur für mich, aber Ich bin Ich" — "I am not only for myself, but I am I" (p. 68). I am not only for myself, this individual appearing in the world, a thing among things and a man among men, constantly representing himself to himself, always concerned with himself, caring for things and others only for his own sake. To refer everything to oneself one must first be the Self to which everything is referred, able to say "Ich bin Ich." Yet this "I am I" is nothing original. I am myself, but I did not bring myself into the self I am. I am given to myself, but I did not give myself to myself. I am given to myself, and my life to itself in the Self I am, only in the self-givenness of absolute Life in its Word. A living transcendental Self like man's exists only in the "Word of life" of the First Letter of John, whom Paul calls "the firstborn among many brothers" (Romans 8:29). We too are born of absolute Life. To be born does not mean to come into the world: things appear an instant in the world's light and vanish, but they are not "born." Birth concerns only the living, and for them means coming to be as one of the living transcendental Selves each of us is. Only because we have come into life can we come into the world. The distinction between "being for oneself" (reflexive self-concern, Heidegger's *Sorge* "for the sake of itself") and "being I" (the self-identity that self-concern presupposes) is the hinge. Henry's key formula of the 1990s follows: "I am given to myself, but I did not give myself to myself." Its decisive feature is that the passivity of the self toward itself, established in essays I and II, is now traced to its source. "Transcendental birth," not worldly birth, becomes the theme of essay VI.

Our transcendental birth is thus clarified. How do we come into life? Insofar as life comes into itself, and in the way it does. Because absolute Life comes into itself by experiencing itself in the ipseity of the First Living Self, its Word, every man given to himself in the ipseity of that life comes into himself as a living transcendental Self. That is why all transcendental phenomenological life is marked at its heart by a radical, insurmountable individuality. Individuality, which essay II grounded in the self-sufficiency of auto-affection (p. 51), is now grounded in the generation of the Self within absolute Life: finite selves are individuals because Life's self-revelation always takes the form of a Self. The footnote's indication that all biblical citations come from the *Bible de Jérusalem* is a reminder that Henry quotes Scripture as a phenomenological text, by its standard French version.

A historical remark with grave consequences: life was the great absentee in Western philosophy, which, inheriting from Greece, defined man by thought. When life returned to the European stage with Schopenhauer in the early nineteenth century, it was a life without individuality, anonymous, impersonal, savage, which established its reign over philosophy and the whole of culture, giving it a tragic and absurd character and opening the way to brute force, violence, nihilism. The one-paragraph genealogy targets Schopenhauer's will, Nietzsche's will to power, and their twentieth-century political consequences. Henry's point is that the return of life could be catastrophic precisely because it was thought without ipseity — the error the river image of essay II (p. 51) already rejected.

The phenomenology of life faces a last question. In every living being life comes as a Self belonging to every determination of life, so that "il n'y a aucune souffrance qui puisse n'être la souffrance de personne" (p. 69). Because God is Life, one must say with Meister Eckhart, "Dieu s'engendre comme moi-même" — an abyssal statement sufficient to dismiss all the "crises of the subject" of contemporary nihilism. If nihilism regards life not only as anonymous but also as unconscious, it is because modernity, again duped by the Greek *phainomenon*, which reserves manifestation for the light of exteriority, cannot grasp the invisible in its own phenomenological positivity. In what does that positivity consist? The question of the unit sets up the next stage: the "unconscious" life of Schopenhauer and Freud is life misread as non-appearing, because only light counts as appearing. Eckhart's sermon (Henry cites Sermon 6 in the Aubier translation) supplies the formula that unites the Son's generation and the self's: God begets himself as me.

Consider suffering once more. "Suffering reveals suffering" needs correcting. The self-revelation accomplished in suffering cannot be the work of suffering in its particular content, since it occurs equally in joy, boredom, anguish or effort. Anguish is revealed to itself in its affectivity, in the "auto-impressionnalité pathétique" that is the flesh of this suffering and of every modality of life (p. 69). That is why all of them are affective modalities. There is, in Maine de Biran's brilliant intuition, a "feeling of effort," so that every form of action is possible only in the pain or satisfaction of that effort — not as an objective, itself unconscious displacement, but as an "I can" experiencing itself in and through its affectivity. Affectivity is therefore no particular sphere of our life; it penetrates and ultimately grounds the whole domain of action, of "work," and so of economic phenomena, which cannot be separated from human existence as people today think they can. The correction is important: what reveals is not the particular tonality but the affectivity common to all, the "pathos" in which each is given. Henry thereby distinguishes affectivity (the mode of revelation) from affects (its modalities), the confusion of which underlies the reproach that he reduces everything to feelings. Biran's feeling of effort — the theme of essay I — and Marx's living labour are both placed under affectivity.

Likewise there is a pathos of thought, which explains classical philosophy's privilege of evidence. Behind it one easily recognizes the reign of the visible that dominates our culture, still captive to Greek *theoria*. But thought, rational thought included, is given to itself only in life's pathic self-revelation, and Husserl himself, despite founding phenomenological method on the seeing of evidence, had to write: "La conscience qui juge un « état de chose » mathématique est une impression" (p. 70, from the time-consciousness lectures). The quotation turns Husserl against himself: if even a mathematical judgement is, as lived, an impression, then the self-givenness of thought is not evidence but impressional, that is, affective. *Phénoménologie matérielle* builds its reading of the lectures on such passages.

All modalities of life, theoretical and cognitive thought included, are affective at bottom, because the phenomenological matter in which pure phenomenality originally becomes phenomenal is an "Archi-passibilité" in which alone every self-experience becomes possible (p. 70). In John's words, God is not only Life but Love; thus an essential connection is established between the pure fact of living and affectivity. "Arch-passibility" is the late Henry's term for the original capacity to undergo oneself; "passibility" (*passibilité*, the capacity to be affected) replaces the earlier "passivity," whose negative connotations are avoided. The Johannine "God is love" (1 John 4:8) is read phenomenologically: life's self-revelation is affective, and love names its affective essence.

If our tonalities have their ultimate possibility in life's essence, they are never explained solely by worldly events taken as their "motives" or "causes." "A misfortune has happened," we say, meaning that an objective event — accident, illness, bereavement — has produced suffering, to the point of being identified with it. But however dramatic, such an event can produce suffering only in a being capable of suffering, a living one given to itself in a life whose essence is arch-passibility. Why then does the feeling take this tonality rather than another? All modalities of our life are distributed by a decisive dichotomy between positive ones (pleasure, happiness) and negative ones (pain, sadness), so that our whole existence seems caught in an affective becoming that is far from indeterminate, oscillating between malaise and satisfaction, suffering and joy — neutral tones like boredom or indifference being a kind of neutralization of this primitive oscillation. How can the dichotomy be explained if it does not result from worldly events alone? The argument repeats essay II (p. 52) in a later idiom; the new element is the treatment of neutral tones as neutralizations, so that the bipolar structure is exhaustive.

The answer has been given. Since the essence of living is self-experience in the immanence of a pathic auto-affection without gap or distance, life is marked by radical passivity toward itself: a "se souffrir soi-même," a "se subir soi-même," "plus fort que toute liberté" (p. 71), present in the most modest suffering, unable to escape itself, driven back against itself in a primordial passion proper to all life and every living being. Only because of this primitive suffering, which belongs to every self-experience as the concrete phenomenological mode of its accomplishment, is something like "a suffering" possible. "Stronger than any freedom" opposes Henry to Sartre once more: freedom presupposes the self it cannot choose.

Yet in accomplishing this self-suffering, life experiences itself, reaches itself, grows with its own content, enjoys itself — it is enjoyment, joy. Two original, fundamental phenomenological tonalities, a pure suffering and a pure enjoying, are rooted a priori in the self-experience that is the essence of any conceivable life. On this division rests the dichotomy visible across all our affective tonalities. What we discover, along with life's deepest essence, is the a priori, transcendental possibility of the passage of all our tonalities into one another — a continuous sliding, whether gradual or a sudden "leap," legible in the concrete becoming of everyday existence. That becoming can seem absurd and incomprehensible, subject to a contingent history or the play of unconscious drives, as it did to Verlaine, looking back over his life: "Vieux bonheurs, vieux malheurs, comme une file d'oies…" (p. 72, *Sagesse* I, 6). Verlaine's line images the disillusioned view: affects follow one another like a file of geese, without inner connection. "Unconscious drives" names the Freudian alternative. Both locate the order of affects outside the affects themselves.

This impression is superficial once we understand that the potential modification of our many modalities belongs to an original possibility of passage between the fundamental tonalities of life's essence. Pure suffering is the concrete phenomenological mode in which life comes into itself, its embrace with itself in pure enjoying — and so the possibility of every conceivable happiness and joy, which in the end is always "la joie de vivre, le bonheur sans limite d'exister" (p. 72). The thesis is stronger than in essay II: suffering is not merely convertible into joy; it is the very path of life's self-embrace, so that joy is the truth of suffering.

Considered in their specific content, suffering and joy differ, as do malaise and satisfaction, desire and fulfilment; this difference — the will to replace negative modalities with positive ones — usually determines action, from the most elementary impulse of need to satisfy itself. Yet despite their difference and sometimes violent opposition, suffering and joy and their modalizations are united in a more original identity: that of suffering and enjoying as co-constitutive of life's essence and ipseity. To grasp it we must not lose sight of the finitude of our life, but perceive it in its Ground — not where it seems to experience itself in a psychological facticity unable to account for itself, but where it is given to itself in the self-givenness of absolute Life, at the place of our transcendental birth. That was Kierkegaard's insight when he understood that at the height of suffering, at the limit of despair, despair turns into beatitude, when "le moi plonge, à travers sa propre transparence, dans la puissance qui l'a posé" (p. 73). The identity of suffering and joy is now grounded not in finite life's self-relation but in its being given by absolute Life. The Kierkegaard formula, already paraphrased at the end of essay II (p. 56), is now quoted and placed at the site of transcendental birth. "Psychological facticity" names the misreading Henry guards against: affects are not brute psychological facts but modes of a givenness whose ground is absolute.

Arch-passibility also accounts for the most singular feature of the human condition: incarnate existence. Interpreted at once as existence in a body, it refers to the question of the body, and that question, like any fundamental one, to a phenomenological substratum, a mode of appearing. The mode that seems obviously the body's is the world's appearing, in a double sense. Every body, ours or any other, shows itself in the world, holding its phenomenological properties from the world's, first of all its exteriority. But this worldly body is not only exterior; it has sensible qualities, and so presupposes a second body, a transcendental body that senses, sees, touches and hears it by its various senses. In twentieth-century phenomenology these powers are understood as intentionalities, so the transcendental body constituting the universe is an intentional body. In this second sense our body is a body of the world, because it opens us to it. The appearing on which this opening rests is the same as that in which the object-body of tradition shows itself: being-outside-itself. But intentionality, which makes everything visible, cannot bring itself into phenomenality. The aporia on which Husserl foundered recurs for the body reduced to the intentional body: each performance of this transcendental body can give us what it gives — to see, touch, hear — only if it gives itself to itself originally in the giving it performs. Such immanent self-givenness occurs only in life, in its pathic self-revelation (pp. 73–74). The paragraph compresses essay I's argument against Merleau-Ponty into the late vocabulary and the structure of *Incarnation*: the object-body, the intentional body, and the self-given flesh that the intentional body presupposes. The regress argument against intentionality (p. 61) is applied to the body.

Only then is our conception of the body overturned: when the appearing to which it is entrusted is no longer the world's but life's. Our body then differs totally from all other bodies in the universe: it is no longer a visible body but a flesh, "une chair invisible" (p. 74). Since flesh has its phenomenological foundation in life, it holds from life all its phenomenological properties: not only acosmism and invisibility, which suffice to distinguish it radically from the "body" of tradition, but also "ce petit fait, que toute chair est la chair de quelqu'un" — not by contingent association but because a Self is implied in every self-revelation of life and is built up in every flesh together with it, in the very event that gives it to itself in its transcendental birth. Flesh also holds from life its very reality, the pure phenomenological matter of "auto-impressionnalité," which is identical with pathic auto-affection. Precisely: the pure phenomenological matter of every true, radically immanent auto-affection, in which life pathically undergoes itself, is flesh. Only because flesh is the matter of auto-impressionality, deriving its possibility from life's auto-affection, does it constitute the reality of all our impressions. The French *chair* translates Husserl's *Leib* and Merleau-Ponty's *chair*, but Henry's flesh is the opposite of Merleau-Ponty's: not the "element" common to sensing and sensed, visible in principle, but the invisible self-impression of life. The equation "flesh = matter of auto-affection" closes the circle opened in essay I: the soul, the original body, and flesh are one structure.

Our life is finite and comprehensible only from the infinite life in which it is given to itself. Just as our Self, unable to bring itself into itself, refers to the First Living Self, the Word in which absolute Life reveals itself to itself, so the auto-impressionality that makes possible every impression and every flesh presupposes the arch-passibility of absolute Life — its original capacity to bring itself into itself as pathic phenomenological effectuation. Only in this arch-passibility is any flesh "passible," that is, in turn possible — flesh being nothing but this passibility of a finite life drawing its possibility from the arch-passibility of infinite Life (pp. 74–75). The wordplay *passible / possible* carries a thesis: the possibility of flesh is its capacity to undergo itself, and this capacity is received. The parallel between Self and flesh (both generated in absolute Life) prepares the Johannine conclusion.

Here the phenomenology of life can claim not to remain prisoner of traditional philosophy. Can it not illuminate decisive elements of our culture from its non-Greek source, Judaeo-Christian spirituality? Since every flesh is given to itself only in life's arch-passibility, the phenomenology of life discloses the singular link between the two initiatory sayings of John's Prologue: "In the beginning was the Word" and "And the Word became flesh" (John 1:1, 14). The first has been accounted for, if no life is possible that does not imply the Self in which it experiences itself. If every flesh is passible only in the arch-passibility of life in its Word, then the Incarnation ceases to be absurd, as it was for the Greeks. Between Word and flesh there is more than affinity: an identity of essence, which is that of absolute Life. Once flesh is returned to life, it is no longer the objective body with its strange forms, its incomprehensible sexual determination that can arouse our anguish, delivered to the world and endlessly subjected to the question "why?" But, as Eckhart saw, life is "sans pourquoi" (p. 75). Flesh, carrying within it the principle of its own revelation, asks no other instance to enlighten it about itself. When, in its innocence, each modality of our flesh experiences itself, when suffering says suffering and joy joy, it is Life that speaks in it, "et rien n'a pouvoir contre sa parole." The "absurdity" of the Incarnation for the Greeks is the Pauline "foolishness" (1 Corinthians 1:23) understood philosophically: if flesh is a worldly body, a God made flesh is a contradiction; if flesh is life's self-impression, the Word's incarnation is life taking on a finite mode of its own essence. The allusion to sexual difference as a source of anguish prefigures *Incarnation*'s analysis of Kierkegaard's *Concept of Anxiety*. The final image — suffering saying itself — prepares essay X, where the "word of life" is set against the language of the world.

How else to name this arch-passibility, beyond all passibility yet present in it, immanent to every flesh as what gives it to itself, beyond all sensible or intelligible evidence, but an "Archi-intelligibilité," an "Archi-gnose," whose essence John described as the coming of absolute Life in its Word before it made possible the coming of the Word in a flesh like ours? Johannine arch-intelligibility is implied wherever there is life; it extends to the beings of flesh that we are, taking up into its "Parousie incandescente" our derisory sufferings and hidden wounds, as it did the wounds of Christ on the cross (pp. 75–76). The purer each suffering, the more reduced to itself and to its phenomenological body of flesh, the more strongly the limitless power that gives it to itself is felt in us. And when that suffering reaches its limit in despair, then, in Kierkegaard's words, in relating itself to itself and willing to be itself, the self plunges through its own transparency into the power that posited it, "et l'ivresse de la vie nous submerge." "Heureux ceux qui souffrent. Au Fond de sa Nuit, notre chair est Dieu. L'Archi-gnose est la gnose des simples" (p. 76). "Gnosis" is chosen deliberately and defused at once: not an esoteric knowledge reserved for initiates but the knowledge every flesh has of itself in undergoing itself — the "gnosis of the simple." The beatitude ("Blessed are those who mourn," Matthew 5:4) is read as a phenomenological statement: suffering reduced to itself is the place where the generating Life is most felt. "The Night" and "the Ground" (*Grund*) are Eckhartian and recall John of the Cross; the formula "our flesh is God" is not pantheism but the claim that in the Ground of its self-givenness finite flesh is one with the Life that gives it. The essay thus closes the arc from the Greek *phainomenon* (light, world) to the Johannine Word (Life, flesh) that frames the whole volume.

## IV. Four Principles of Phenomenology

Phenomenology rests on four principles it explicitly claims as foundations. The essay first appeared in the *Revue de métaphysique et de morale* in 1991 as a discussion of Jean-Luc Marion's *Réduction et donation*, and Henry lightly revised it for this volume. Its structure follows from that occasion: a general critique of phenomenology's principles, culminating in a confrontation with Marion's "fourth principle" and his theory of the call.

The first principle, "so much seeming, so much being," comes from the Marburg School. Because "seeming" (*apparence*) is equivocal — it can mean appearing or mere semblance — Henry prefers the rigorous formulation "autant d'apparaître, autant d'être" (p. 77). The correction removes from the principle any suggestion that appearing might be illusory: appearing is not opposed to being as appearance to reality; it is what being consists in.

The second is the "principle of all principles," formulated by Husserl in § 24 of *Ideas I*: every originary giving intuition is a legitimate source of knowledge, and so of any assertion, rational ones included. Henry gives it neutrally here; it will turn out to be the one that does the damage.

The third is so vehement that it sounds like a slogan, even a cry: "Zu den Sachen selbst!" — "to the things themselves!" The tone matters for what follows: the exclamation already presupposes that the things are somewhere else, to be reached.

The fourth was defined much later by Marion in *Réduction et donation*, but it bears on the whole of phenomenology as a hidden presupposition always at work: "d'autant plus de réduction, d'autant plus de donation" — "so much reduction, so much givenness" (pp. 77–78; *Réduction et donation*, p. 302). Henry's decision to place Marion's formula among phenomenology's founding principles is itself a strong claim: the link between reduction and givenness was operative from Husserl on, but only Marion named it.

These four principles share two traits. First, despite their claim to radicality, they are fundamentally undetermined, and the indeterminacy is the more dangerous because these are founding principles: their purely formal character drains them of much of their rigour and fruitfulness. The charge of formalism will be turned, at the end, against Marion himself.

Second, despite their formality, they contain latent tensions incompatible with the coherence expected of a system of presuppositions meant to unify an inquiry — tensions that amount to genuine contradictions. The essay's plan is thus a double one: indeterminacy, then contradiction.

The first principle correlates appearing and being, and the correlation is overwhelming because immediate: if something appears, it thereby is. So strong is it that it seems to reduce to identity — to appear is, identically, to be. The principle aims neither at the extension nor at the intensity of the phenomenological and ontological determinations it relates but at the identity of their essence: being "is" because and insofar as appearing appears; their reign and their essence are one. Henry reads the proportion "so much… so much" not as a measure but as an identity claim.

To think this identity further is to question it, because appearing and being do not stand on the same plane; their ontological dignity differs: "l'apparaître est tout, l'être n'est rien" (p. 79). Being is only because and insofar as appearing appears; identity of essence means a single power at work, and that power is appearing's. Apart from appearing, being is nothing at all; it finds its essence only in appearing, whose own essence is actually to appear. The identity thus resolves into a foundation of being by appearing. The radical formulation is Henry's constant thesis since *L'essence de la manifestation*: ontology is grounded in phenomenology, not the reverse.

To ask about the meaning of being as such, about what would let being be by its own force or will, is nonsense. Being has no force or will; it is a *flatus vocis*, unless we recognize in it the sui generis power that makes it and everything be, that of appearing as appearing. The polemical target is Heidegger's *Seinsfrage*: a question about being that does not first ask how being appears is empty.

Phenomenology therefore stands higher than ontology. But "higher" may mislead: there are not two domains, one prior to and the other produced by it. Phenomenology and ontology — the text's ellipsis evidently means this — are not two but the same, one "thing" whose essence is to appear and is constituted exclusively by appearing. Henry guards against reading the priority as a hierarchy of regions: being is not a lesser region beneath appearing, but appearing itself, named from what it gives.

One must cite the thinker who, long before modern phenomenology, grasped the founding intuition of any pure phenomenology capable of rising to its concept — which neither Husserl's nor Heidegger's was. At least twice Descartes reduced being to appearing so radically that nothing of being remains that is not, in substance, appearing, and pure appearing: an appearing in which what appears is the pure fact of appearing. In the *Regulae* Descartes still understood it traditionally, under improper titles such as *lumen naturale*; in the lightning intuition of the *Meditations* he named it in its true essence: *cogitatio*. The relation appearing/being is then laid bare: "Nous sommes par cela seul que nous pensons" — the being we are, to which all being surviving doubt is reduced, holds its power to be solely from appearing's power to appear. In the *Meditations*' language: "Je suis une chose qui pense c'est-à-dire dont tout l'être est de penser" (p. 80, AT IX-2, 28; IX-1, 22). Being lies in appearing and is exhausted by it. Henry's Descartes is the one of *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*: *cogitatio* is not representation but appearing appearing to itself, the self-givenness that precedes all seeing. The contrast between *lumen naturale* (light, hence world) and *cogitatio* (self-appearing) maps exactly onto the duality of appearing the essay will establish.

The third principle shows a duality of terms not due to language but to what language speaks of: *zu* on one side, *die Sachen* on the other — access to something, and the something reached, the content to which access gives access. The grammar of the slogan already separates appearing (the "to") from what appears (the "things").

As soon as we give the slogan its simplest explication we fall back into the most traditional problematic and abandon what the first principle had given: the identity of appearing and being on the basis of the reduction of the latter to the former. The explication turns the principle of phenomenology into the oldest schema of epistemology.

Hence the contradiction. Being is no longer reducible to appearing and no longer draws its power to be from appearing if there are *Sachen*, things in themselves to be reached along a path, by a means of access that is only a means toward things that are the end — a being that already is by itself before it is met. The third principle reintroduces the thing in itself.

The problem of appearing is not set aside: in phenomenology's imperative, the "zu" that leads directly to things is appearing itself. But appearing, thought as a pure possibility of access, is then subordinated in principle to what it gives access to. The subordination goes so far that access is deployed only for the sake of the thing, its true goal, and is itself determined by that goal: the nature of the object decides the appropriate means of knowing it, what procedures and methods must be set up to reach it as it is. The relation of phenomenology and ontology is reversed, since the thing is the sole aim and the means depend on its nature. Henry describes the methodological realism of the Husserlian regional ontologies, where each region of being prescribes its mode of givenness, and Heidegger's maxim that method follows the matter.

This reversal ends in aporia. What could we know of the thing's nature and of how it determines the means of access, unless the thing and its nature had already been discovered to us in their appearing and through it? To the claim that the thing's nature decides the means, one must reply that the means of access make, and are, its nature: seeing determines the visual character of what is seen, hearing its sonority, space its spatiality, time its temporality. Once the naive determination of phenomenology by ontology is set aside, there remains the problem of their duality — presupposed by classical thought and by contemporary phenomenology, but abolished in a radical reduction of being to appearing. The counter-thesis — access makes the nature — is a transcendental idealism of modes of appearing: the "how" of givenness is constitutive of the "what."

The connection of being and appearing is made in the phenomenon, whose concept denotes something that shows itself and so unites the thing (being) and its showing (appearing). That something "is" only insofar as it shows itself does not settle whether the "something" that becomes phenomenon is not in itself different from appearing, even heterogeneous to it. What justifies the question is that what is given in the phenomenon — the worldly phenomenon at least — is given precisely as already there before the "discovery" the phenomenon offers, before appearing installs it as something that appears for us. Henry locates the realist moment in the phenomenology of perception itself: the perceived thing is given as preceding its being perceived. The doubled "at least, the worldly phenomenon" anticipates that this structure belongs to one mode of appearing only.

A new contradiction: the radical reduction of being to appearing is replaced by their unavoidable dissociation. For being is independent of appearing if, however obscurely, it "is" before it shows itself to us in the phenomenon. The first and third principles are now openly in conflict.

The concept of phenomenon itself becomes contradictory, or at least ambiguous. Positively, it expresses the primitive embrace of being and appearing, the unshakeable ground affirmed by the first principle on which phenomenology wished to rest. As long as something appears, no critique has any purchase: one may say "this phenomenon is an illusion," but nothing in the phenomenon is touched, as long as it goes on appearing and we keep to its appearing. The thesis echoes Descartes's *videre videor* in the second *Meditation*: even if what I see is illusory, it is certain that I seem to see.

Removed from critique, the phenomenon is not removed from analysis. What analysis first questions is not the connection appearing/being but the reduction of being to appearing — the idea that there is no being other than that of appearing as such. On inspection, the phenomenon does not imply this radical reduction; it seems rather to presuppose, along with their connection, their distinction, at least as an ideal possibility. Is it not this distinction, inscribed in the phenomenon's phenomenological structure, that makes analysis necessary? The distinction will be the lever: analysis presupposes that the phenomenon can be divided, and the division reveals a difference of nature.

Analysis separates in the phenomenon what appears and the fact of appearing. The concept of phenomenon is double, ontic and phenomenological. Ontically, it designates what appears — this table, this proposition, this memory. But that ontic content, what common sense means by "phenomenon," does not exhaust the concept, which includes phenomenality, the fact that it shows itself and is a phenomenon only thereby. The distinction is the one Heidegger draws in § 7 between the "ordinary" and the "phenomenological" concept of phenomenon, and the one Henry cited from § 44 in essay III (p. 60).

How far does this splitting go, and what becomes, in its devastating rupture, of the primitive unity of appearing and being? This must be thought through. It is no longer a merely notional distinction between content and pure appearing if appearing is unchanged while what appears in it constantly changes. This permanence implies a difference of nature. In traditional thought, which understands phenomenality as light — natural light, the light of reason, of the world, or the world itself — the difference between appearing and what appears takes the form of absolute indifference: the indifference of light to whatever it lights, as Descartes says in the first of the *Regulae* (AT X, 360). The *Regulae* passage (wisdom remains one however diverse its objects, as sunlight is one whatever it illuminates) supplies the image of the neutrality of light that essay III (p. 63) turned into an ethical charge.

But if content and appearing differ so far as to be absolutely indifferent to each other, each falling back on its own side with nothing in common, how can their unity still be preserved in the phenomenon as its ground? The aporia of the phenomenon brings to light that of the appearing/being relation on which phenomenology meant to build. How could the content, the thing, the being, be brought into being by appearing if it is in principle indifferent to it? How could being hold its being from appearing if, in itself and as the phenomenon's content, it is irreducible to the phenomenon's phenomenality? The principle of phenomenology, the internal bond of appearing and being, is struck; all of phenomenology loses its bearings and drifts (pp. 83–84). This is the "ontological poverty" of world-appearing (essay III, p. 63) derived from the concept of phenomenon itself: a light indifferent to what it lights cannot give it being.

And how could it be otherwise, as long as appearing — and being reduced to it — remain purely formal concepts, and the founding principles remain phenomenologically undetermined? The diagnosis returns from contradiction to its cause: the contradictions arise because no one has said what appearing is made of.

What can the first principle mean if one does not know what appearing means, and so what being means, a being whose being is appearing? What sense has the proportion uniting them, "une proportion à jamais énigmatique entre deux termes également inconnus" (p. 84)? The formula is exact: a ratio between two unknowns determines nothing.

The third principle is no clearer if the access expressed by "zu," which is appearing itself, remains unelucidated, if we do not know how things appear and so how to go to them. The question whether things differ from the pure possibility of access — whether access must be thought apart, or things and access share one essence — remains unanswered, though phenomenology's ultimate claim to circumscribe being by phenomenality, even to reduce it to phenomenality, depends on it. The undecided question is exactly the one Henry answers: in life, access and what is accessed coincide; in the world, they differ.

The fourth principle suffers the same uncertainty. Its proportion between reduction and givenness leads back to the sources of phenomenology and to the first principle as reformulated: the reduction, phenomenologically understood, is a reduction to appearing. That it can be carried more or less far, with greater or lesser radicality, as "so much reduction…" implies, does not mean only that appearing must be rigorously distinguished from what appears in it, with which thought has always confused it. If the radicality of the reduction must concern appearing itself, must we not conceive that appearing is not as simple as all these statements assume — not a single, monotonous way of showing — but involves fundamental differences and, beyond them, "une différence absolue entre deux modes hétérogènes de manifestation" (pp. 84–85; the printed "mondes" is evidently "modes")? Irreducible to a univocal formal meaning, appearing differs as such, in the pure phenomenological matter of which it is made. Only attention to this matter, to the how of appearing, can give sense to the principles; only a view directed at the phenomenological substantiality of pure phenomenality can say whether it is homogeneous with being and able to determine it. Henry reads Marion's principle more radically than Marion: if reduction can be intensified, it must eventually bear on appearing itself and divide it. This is the "radical reduction" introduced below. "Matter" and "substantiality" announce the programme of *Phénoménologie matérielle*, published the year before the article.

From the standpoint of a material phenomenology, the principles look worse than undetermined. The suspicion arises that, thanks to this very indeterminacy — the failure to fix appearing's actual, concrete modalities — a particular conception of phenomenality, the one that first and mostly presents itself to ordinary thought and constitutes traditional philosophy's oldest and least critical presupposition, borrowed from everyday perception of worldly objects, imposed itself not as what it is, a particular form of experience, but as the structure of appearing in general and of every phenomenon. "Cette confusion catastrophique de l'apparaître du monde avec l'essence universelle de l'apparaître" corrupts almost all Western philosophies, so that critical understanding of them mostly consists in detecting it at the root of their postulates (p. 85). In Husserl, it is the second principle that displays the confusion most fully. The paragraph is the general formula of Henry's history of philosophy: every system is to be read for the point where world-appearing is silently universalized. The same passage appears almost verbatim in essay III (p. 60), written nine years later.

This famous principle, presented as the most fundamental, hides under its simplicity a double thesis that makes it contradictory. On the one hand, it aims explicitly at universality: under the title "intuition" it speaks of what makes possible every phenomenon and every experience in any domain whatsoever. Appearing in general — the universal condition of phenomena, phenomenality in its first and omnipresent structure — is designated as "originary giving intuition." Henry restates the principle's universal scope precisely in order to measure it against its actual content.

On the other hand, "intuition" designates a particular mode of appearing, not appearing as an undetermined concept. In Husserl, intuition names the structure of consciousness as "consciousness of something," as intentional. Strictly, it qualifies fulfilled intentionality, but intuition owes its power to bring into phenomenality to intentionality as such, which does so by surpassing itself toward what is thrown before it as its correlate, a transcendent object. The transcendence of the object, its being set at a distance, constitutes phenomenality as such. The contradiction is a category mistake: a species of appearing (ek-static, intentional) is made the genus.

From this one-sided definition, given as universal, follows a limitation whose meaning is epistemological but above all vital. Not only knowledge is struck with insurmountable finitude — the West's most constant thesis — instead of being grounded; our very life is set aside, forgotten, lost, if the essence of life is that of original phenomenality and if phenomenality drawing its substance from the ek-static light of a world is excluded in principle from this "Archi-Révélation." But intuition is only a name for that transcendence; it implies an unconscious but radical elimination of life (p. 86). "Arch-revelation" is introduced here as the name for life's self-revelation insofar as it is the origin of all appearing.

Phenomenology's ultimate claim, to reach being itself in phenomena, here meets not only contradictions but absolute failure. By placing intuition at the source of all experience and knowledge, phenomenology deploys a kind of appearing in which being never "is" in its most original essence — as it wells up in us as the infinite Life that constantly gives us to ourselves and engenders us as it engenders itself in its eternal auto-affection. Far from being tied in intuition, the bond of appearing and being comes wholly undone there: in the phenomenon issuing from intuition, especially in evidence, being is not presented but set aside, abolished. To require that being offer itself in intuition, ideally in its perfected form, evidence, is to deny that any being other than intuited or intuitable is possible; with respect to the original essence from which appearing and being draw, it is to deny its very "existence." "Le second principe, en vérité le premier, est un meurtre" (p. 87). The violence of the final formula is calculated: the principle of principles kills life in the sense that it makes life's mode of appearing inadmissible as evidence. "In truth the first" means that it is the principle that actually governs Husserlian practice.

The principle of principles' elimination of what, as life's auto-affection, constitutes the arch-revelation of appearing explains the fate of historical phenomenology, insofar as it never broke with Husserl's founding presupposition — which makes Heidegger's critiques of Husserl ineffective. Insofar as what makes being is precisely not the appearing grasped under the concept of intuition but escapes it irreducibly, the task of never finding what is at issue — not only the "meaning of being" but the force that makes it be — is entrusted to an endless quest, to what "they" call a hermeneutic. But what is opposed to intuition or intentionality under various titles — "Dasein," "transcendence," "ek-static truth" — only names the condition of intuition or intentionality: no phenomenality other, in its pure phenomenological materiality, than that of the ek-stasis in which all intuition unfolds is conceived or envisaged. The very question of the possibility of an impressional phenomenological matter, of a pathic auto-affection, never comes to mind (pp. 87–88). Henry's verdict on the Heidegger–Husserl debate: Heidegger's critique of intuition in the name of understanding and disclosedness changes nothing, because disclosedness is the transcendental condition of intuition, not its alternative. The sneer at "hermeneutics" — the endless quest for a meaning never given — targets Gadamer and Ricœur as well.

In Husserl, phenomenology's central gap — that it lacks in principle, and in the principle of principles, the transcendental life that was its main concern — is masked by the systematic character of the inquiry. Once intuition is the foundation of every possible form of being, a universal phenomenological ontology can be achieved only by an exhaustive elucidation of the possible forms of intuition and their corresponding types of evidence, evidence being in each case intuition's most perfect mode and so the norm for each region of being. The patient pursuit of this grand project uncovered new forms of intuition and unknown regions of being. Yet this extraordinary enlargement of human experience goes together with a tragic limitation: all these carefully described modes of access are forms of intuition, from which life withdraws by nature. Phenomenology thus produced a "reduction" in a purely negative sense of what it wanted to enlarge and free — our relation to being as our own life. The pun on "reduction" sets up the turn to Marion: the reduction as practised narrowed, where it should have opened.

The fourth principle has the merit of restoring a positive meaning to reduction by naming phenomenology's two key concepts and relating them proportionally. Far from limiting or omitting — from "reducing" — reduction opens and gives. What does it give? Givenness. The first leads to the second; widening and deepening one widens and deepens the other: "so much reduction, so much givenness." This must be shown. Henry's agreement with Marion is real and precedes his critique: the reduction is not subtraction but access to givenness.

The first appearance of the reduction, in the lectures of 1905 and 1907, remains burdened with the ontico-phenomenological equivocation denounced above. Seeking the "pure phenomenon," Husserl does not clearly distinguish what appears from appearing itself. The equivocation corrupts the concept of "absolute givenness," "self-givenness" (*absolute Gegebenheit, Selbstgegebenheit*), which means both the datum and the way it is given. Obtaining such data becomes the aim. In 1905 the sensation data are the absolute data reached by this first reduction, while whatever exceeds these indubitable subjective appearances — the "transcendent" object constituted from them — is affected with uncertainty and put out of play. Sound and visual impressions are certain; the object aimed at through them but not given in them may not exist. What is directly reached, the pure phenomena, are the phenomena of consciousness that aim at the object, not what they aim at. The reference is to *The Idea of Phenomenology* (1907) and the contemporaneous time lectures. Henry's complaint is that the first reduction divides contents (certain vs. doubtful) rather than modes of givenness — an "ontic" division.

Yet the same 1905 lectures explicitly designate the objects of phenomenology as "objects in their how," introducing the decisive distinction between what is given and the mode of its givenness, the "object" and its "how," which is the how of givenness, givenness itself. The division between certain and suspended contents is only a means to go back to how they are given, which alone makes them certain or not. From reduction to givenness, even within the ontic equivocation, the path is travelled from 1905, from the reduction's first appearance. The phrase "objects in their how" (*Gegenstände im Wie*) was the anchor of essay III's reading of Husserl (p. 61). Henry finds Marion's principle already at work at the birth of the reduction.

The true sense of reduction is therefore never ontic: not a division between secure contents on which knowledge could be founded and doubtful ones. In principle the reduction is phenomenological, because it concerns phenomenology's own object, the how of givenness. How are the contents that can claim certainty given, and how those that cannot? In and through evidence for the former, outside and independently of it for the latter. Never is the certain content, what is evident, a foundation for knowledge, but only what makes it evident: evidence itself. That is what it means to say that evidence alone is a principle; that is the meaning of the principle of principles (pp. 89–90). The reading is charitable: Husserl's principle of principles is restated as a principle about the mode of givenness, not about privileged contents.

But analysis has already objected that, under this principle, what alone matters — phenomenology's object, and every ontological determination that receives its consistency from original appearing — is lost. Life's arch-revelation never gives itself under the gaze of evidence nor coincides with it, and the being we are, as living beings generated in that arch-revelation, escapes it too. It is no accident that Husserlian phenomenology, subordinating all ontology to the transcendental Ego, proved helpless when it had to determine the "being" of this "Ego" or what might take its place. The "anonymity" of the ego (essay III, p. 61) is the symptom: a principle of evidence cannot give the being of what is not evident.

The disappearance of original appearing — phenomenology's proper object — under the principle of principles compels us to return to the reduction and give it a truly radical and new meaning. The principles presuppose a pure reduction, which, opposing appearing to its ontic content, finally thematizes appearing as it is. But this pure reduction to pure phenomenality, instead of freeing it in its fullness, hides its most primitive power. Extending and completing the pure reduction, "la réduction radicale réduit l'apparaître lui-même": it sets aside in appearing the expanse of light we call the world to discover what this horizon of visibilization could never become visible without: the auto-affection of its transcendental exteriority in the outsideless pathos of Life. Only a reduction that goes to the end of its power to reduce, suspending the ek-static "Dimensional" of visibility into which every conceivable giving intuition, evidence itself, all possible making-visible, throws itself, discovers original givenness — that which, giving life to itself, gives it to be life, and gives it at the same time every thing of the world and every possible world, since the world's givenness occurs nowhere but in life's self-givenness. The reduction must therefore be pushed to its end, by the "more" that radicalizes it, so that givenness gives itself according to its own excess — an excess belonging to it as its very possibility, without which nothing, not even the most trivial being, would ever be given. The trait uniting the method and object of phenomenology "selon une proportionnalité encore inouïe" is then disclosed in the fourth principle (pp. 90–91). Three reductions are now in play: the ontic division of contents (1905), the pure reduction (appearing vs. what appears), and Henry's radical reduction (world-appearing vs. life's self-appearing). "Dimensional" is Heidegger's term (*das Dimensionale*) for the opening of the clearing. Henry's thesis that the world's horizon must itself be self-affected to appear makes life not an alternative to world-appearing but its condition. "Excess" deliberately borrows Marion's vocabulary of the saturated phenomenon, assigning its true site to life.

Three remarks follow. First, the "more" of original givenness does not merely mean the other appearing classical phenomenology always missed — the other face, which is no face the world turns toward us, no conceivable face, but "le Non-visage de l'essence invisible de la vie." It is not a matter of adding, to the ek-static or intentional how exploited by evidence, the unthought how in which life embraces itself in its own pathos. The "more" means that, in this wholly other way of giving, the finitude striking every ek-static horizon loses its power. In the horizon, everything seen refers to the unseen, everything real to the unreal, everything given to the non-given; every givenness is punctual, provisional, surrounded by unfulfilled horizons, by potentialities inevitably unaccomplished, subject to flowing away, disappearance and the vanity of hope. In Life, by contrast, which touches every point of its being and is lacking nowhere, "tout est là tout entier à chaque instant." This is possible only because in life's ultimate givenness there are no adumbrations or aspects, no horizons of fulfilment or contents to fulfil them, nothing beyond or short of an irremissible experience whose phenomenological matter is pathos and whose modalities belong to it in their unsurpassable fullness each time (p. 91). "Non-face" glances at Levinas's face, which Henry places among the modes of the world's appearing. The decisive point is qualitative: the "more" is not more of the same givenness but the absence of the horizonal structure that makes worldly givenness always partial. Husserl's adumbrations (*Abschattungen*) are the paradigm of finite givenness; life has none.

Husserl wanted nothing to be lost in the reduction and everything regained at a higher level of consistency and legitimacy, as with the cogitatum in what he took to be the reduction of the world itself. Can the radical reduction, working within pure appearing and recognizing its duplicity in order to preserve life's most original givenness, claim a comparable result? Leaving the reign of the visible, every conceivable world, is it not affected with a negative index? At the end of this unheard-of reduction that removes all transcendence and all it gives, can one still say "so much reduction, so much givenness"? The objection is the obvious one: a reduction to immanence seems to lose the world.

Yet no affection by the world, and so by any being, would ever occur if this ek-static affection were not self-affected in Life, which is nothing but this primitive auto-affection. Because the world has being only in life, because it "is" only as a living cosmos, only the radical reduction that draws in appearing the final line between immanence and transcendence preserves what escapes transcendence in principle while acting in it as its condition. The "living cosmos" — the world as it is lived, not as represented — anticipates essay X's "life-world" read through life rather than perception.

Every seeing carries within it a non-seeing without which it would see nothing: the immanent experience it constantly has of itself. That is why it is a pathic seeing, a seeing in desire, boredom, passion, and subject to them. Of the radical reduction to pure immanence one must say not only that it forgets and removes nothing, but that it alone gives what it brackets its particular properties, which seeing, intuition and evidence left to themselves do not explain at all. The "non-seeing" in seeing is the self-givenness of vision itself, which essay I (p. 34) argued could not be another vision. The affective coloration of the seen (desirable, horrible) is traced to the pathos of the seeing.

Not only the desirable, horrible or odious character of what is seen, but seeing as such holds its power from life. That the radical reduction to pure immanence, unnoticed by Husserl and contemporary phenomenology, alone keeps the promise of losing nothing, whereas the reduction to evidence simply misses life and so givenness in its original content: such is the second remark. Henry claims for his reduction exactly what Husserl claimed for his: it loses nothing, because the world itself is given only in life.

The third remark concerns the relation of the radical reduction to the pure reduction. The pure reduction comes first on the path that can lead to the radical one: one must put beings out of play and think appearing in itself in order to recognize its duplicity, the division of the two realms, and their relation — the grounding of finite givenness in a givenness that excludes all horizon and limitation. The sequence matters: Henry does not bypass Husserl's or Heidegger's reductions but passes through them.

But — and this is the important point — though first, the pure reduction cannot truly be accomplished if it stops at itself, because the appearing it isolates, however pure, does not subsist by itself. Here lies contemporary phenomenology's chief and most powerful illusion: to believe that Being, defined phenomenologically as an ek-static Dimensional, finds in it a sufficient phenomenological condition — ultimately, that transcendence is phenomenologically and ontologically an autonomous essence. Because the reduction was not carried to its end as a reduction to pure immanence, the givenness it reaches is not only essentially finite; in fact it gives nothing, and itself least of all. Precisely because it did not accomplish toward itself the original work of self-givenness, because appearing did not occur as self-appearing and so does not appear, nothing else can appear in it. For the other of appearing, the being, can appear only insofar as appearing appears in itself and as such (p. 93). This is the essay's central argument in its sharpest form: the "autonomy of transcendence" is the thesis of *L'essence de la manifestation*'s critique of ontological monism. An appearing that does not appear to itself cannot make anything else appear. The argument is a transcendental condition, not an inference: the self-appearing of appearing is required for there to be any appearing of something.

That self-appearing is what the abstract appearing of historical phenomenology cannot produce. Because appearing remains unthought in its ownmost, inner possibility, it has always been understood as the appearing of something other than itself, foreign to its phenomenality — the appearing of what appears, of beings. That what appears in appearing is first and necessarily appearing itself, classical phenomenology never clearly saw. Its theme, the phenomenon as showing itself, involves only an abstract appearing that cannot subsist by itself and constantly refers to its contrary, the opaque and dead element of ontic determination. Why did classical phenomenology confine itself to the appearing of beings, ignoring any appearing of another order, if not because it took beings as its guide? The "ontic presupposition" of Heidegger's phenomenology, which Henry criticized in *L'essence de la manifestation* (§§ 11–13, as he notes below), is generalized to the whole of phenomenology.

In the end the phenomenon of phenomenology, despite the illusory significance given to a supposed subjectivity exhausted in relation to things, was nothing but the Greek appearing. All the critiques of subjectivity that proliferate today are false critiques: they can at most reproach the concept of subjectivity for thinking badly — for instance, ontically — the appearing they keep to, the worldly thing's; they never think another appearing. So they remain, unknowingly, enclosed in what they criticize (Henry refers to his article "La critique du sujet," in *Cahiers Confrontation* 20, 1989). By demanding the radicality of a reduction that suspends the phenomenality proper to beings, the fourth principle forces the way to a more original givenness. The *Confrontation* issue was the well-known "Après le sujet qui vient" (1989), to which Derrida, Nancy, Marion and Henry contributed; Henry's charge is that the anti-subjectivist consensus shares the Greek concept of phenomenon with the subjectivism it attacks.

The fourth principle emerges at the end of Marion's inquiry but illuminates it retrospectively and gives it its novelty. The subordination of ontology to phenomenology that has guided Henry's analysis is explicit in Marion's brief but decisive confrontation with Husserl in the fifth study. Heidegger's critique of his master, too often accepted, is questionable. Fascinated by the new fields of objects intentional analysis opened up, Husserl did reserve his attention to these objectivities rather than to being in the universality of its meaning. But all these fields — notably the field of objects-in-general of formal ontology — are subordinated in principle to a higher authority of another order, and that authority is phenomenological: though improperly called the transcendental Ego, it signifies pure phenomenality as such. This shows in the 1912 text of *Ideas* cited by Marion, where, freed from the ontic reference that constantly weighs on it in Heidegger (his 1943 postscript to *What Is Metaphysics?* speaks of the wonder "that beings are"), "the wonder of wonders" is "pure consciousness," "the phenomenon par excellence" (pp. 94–95). It is no longer enough to object that the "being" of this subject remains undetermined — as were the difference between "thing" and "thing-for-consciousness" in the reduction, or the objectivity of formal ontology's objects. With Marion one must ask whether this I does not "except itself" from being, whether it stands "outside being" — a hypothesis that would suffice to disqualify Heidegger's problematic of being, at least in its claim to ultimate radicality. Henry sides with Marion's defence of Husserl against Heidegger's charge (in GA 20) that Husserl neglected the question of the being of consciousness. The juxtaposition of the two "wonders" is pointed: for Husserl the wonder is the phenomenon of consciousness, for Heidegger the ontic fact that beings are. The thesis that the ego is "outside being" is where Henry's and Marion's projects converge.

The direct confrontation with Heidegger, which runs through the whole book and culminates in the sixth study, completes the disqualification of ontology in favour of a new mode of phenomenological thinking, perhaps a new phenomenology. The main reason for going beyond the existential analytic of *Sein und Zeit* is the ontic precondition in which its thought of being remains trapped. Dasein may be the unique being in whose being its being, and ultimately being itself, is at stake; it remains a being — hence Marion's critique that the meaning of being cannot be read directly off any being; a being, even Dasein, only ever lets one read the being of beings (p. 95, Marion p. 208). Henry adds a note recalling his own sharp critique, in 1963, of the ontic precondition of Heidegger's phenomenology (*L'essence de la manifestation*, §§ 11–13). The note establishes priority discreetly: Marion's objection to Heidegger repeats Henry's of a quarter-century earlier.

By deliberately putting beings out of play, insofar as they are foreign in themselves to pure phenomenality, the phenomenological question of being passes through phases carefully analysed and all surmounted (the analytic of anguish, the hermeneutic of the phenomenon of the Nothing) and ends in "the irruption of Being itself, whose 'voice' directly summons man," the only thing able to encircle the final enigma of the "phenomenon of being" (Marion p. 278). Here, Henry thinks, the subordination of ontology to phenomenology itself is accomplished, with the disclosure, at the height of the reduction, of a givenness that exceeds all conceivable being. Henry endorses the trajectory of *Réduction et donation*: Heidegger's own path (anguish, nothing, the call of being) is a series of reductions that leads beyond being.

Two decisive traits mark Marion's problematic insofar as it now obeys the fourth principle. The first is the regression from Being to Being's claim or call, demanded and accomplished in the closing analyses: the *Anspruch* precedes and alone makes possible the *Sein*; "the claim more than being"; Dasein exposes itself to being only by yielding to the call; Dasein must be thought wholly from the instance that claims it; the "there" is determined through and through by the call, since it serves only to answer it (Marion pp. 297–299). The reduction, which the book follows from objects to the consciousness of an I, from "beings" to Dasein, from all beings to being, leads to being only to subordinate it to something more essential and older: the claim and the call. Henry's catalogue of Marion's formulas establishes the first trait accurately; the second trait, the call's revocation by boredom, follows.

What does the claim add to Being that is more fundamental than Being? What is its interpellation but the fact that Being comes, appears to us without mask or detour, without intermediary or delay, so that we cannot escape its grip or its immediacy? The call of being is simply its upsurge in us, the embrace in which it gives itself to us while giving us to be. There would be nothing without this triumphant irruption of a revelation, the revelation of the Absolute. Henry translates Marion's "call" into his own terms: the call is a mode of appearing — immediate, gripping, undergone. This translation is the hinge of his critique: if the call is appearing, one must ask in what phenomenality it appears.

Here the problematic of the call takes a singular, surprising turn. After patiently establishing that in being, finally thematized in its own sense, what matters is the call, the way it grips us, the *Ereignis*, the problematic abruptly reverses itself. What comes into its essence last and first is neither being nor its call, but another call having nothing to do with being's call, revoking it to reign in its place. Before grasping the meaning of this rejection of Being's call, one must see how it works — what authority higher than being and its call can intervene and dismiss them. The "other call" of *Réduction et donation*'s final pages is the hinge of Marion's later doctrine of givenness; Henry treats it as the point where Marion's phenomenology risks becoming formal.

That authority is boredom, which Marion, in an original move, treats as a "counter-existential": instead of giving us access to being as a structure of Dasein, boredom turns us away from everything that is, because it first turns us away from what lets every being be, being itself. More radically still, the silent call being addresses to the bored I no longer interests it, "no longer says anything to it" (Marion p. 284). Hence the extraordinary situation of the greatest peril and disgust, where the "I" is, as it were, there for nothing and no one. Marion's boredom is constructed against Heidegger's "profound boredom" (GA 29/30), which for Heidegger reveals beings as a whole; for Marion it suspends the call of being altogether.

From a Heideggerian standpoint one might contest this possibility of Dasein's detaching itself, through boredom, from being and its call. If Dasein is essentially constituted by its relation to being, the being in whose being being in general is at issue, how could the relation be abolished without abolishing Dasein? By its ownmost essence Dasein "stands ek-statically in the truth of being" — a truth that is its flashing, the lighting by which Dasein is lit from its depths. That lighting flash is the call; how could Dasein, wholly lit by it and placed in the light of being, in the *Da* of *Sein*, suddenly and miraculously no longer be? Would it not have to cease being what it is, *Da-sein*? Does this possibility not "simply contradict the very determination of Dasein as being-there, which it cannot not be"? Henry presents the Heideggerian objection at full strength before crediting Marion with answering it.

Marion's great merit is to have sought, within the analytic of Dasein itself, the possibility of revoking the call that supposedly constitutes it. On one side, the wonder that being arouses — which, like the call, "attunes Dasein to what is destined to it and which, without wonder, could not be manifest" — implies at least the attention Dasein lends it, which it may also withhold. That the call is rooted in the eidetic necessity of the ek-static flash, in the truth of being, does not prevent but presupposes the contingency of a response that may fail to occur despite the call, through a boundless boredom of which only the radical refusal of every call can give us an idea. On the other side, if Dasein in its inmost being is openness to the truth and so to Being's call, it retains at least — another limit-formulation of boredom — "the possibility of not being itself," which amounts to "revoking the call of being itself" (pp. 97–98). Marion's argument turns on the contingency of response: a call can be unanswered. Henry will turn precisely this freedom of response against him.

This reading has undeniable critical power: it turns Heidegger's major themes against him, or re-evaluates them. So with its sudden, singular apology for inauthenticity. The possibility of inauthenticity is indeed that of revoking the call of being, denying "that the referral of being constitutes the ultimate possibility of what I am" (Marion pp. 290–293). The famous analysis of everydayness is reversed: if in everyday existence Dasein evades its destiny as Dasein, the Being destined to it in its "there," is it not because this destiny is not necessarily its own, because another destiny is offered it? Everydayness, for Heidegger a fall away from one's own being, becomes in Marion's reading evidence that being is not Dasein's only destiny.

The mere possibility of a destiny other than hearing and answering the call of being fundamentally shakes Dasein's ontological definition in *Sein und Zeit*. It forbids understanding the ek-static relation to Being as the essence of every conceivable *Da*, a possibility that is an essence, and reduces it to one possibility among others. The analytic of Dasein ceases to construct the philosophy of what makes man's *humanitas*; it merely indicates one of the virtualities of his nature. The allusion is to the *Letter on Humanism*: the *humanitas* of man was, for Heidegger, his ek-sistence in the truth of being. Marion's argument regionalizes it — a result Henry welcomes.

However paradoxical this ultimate reduction — putting being out of play in its claim to define our being by its call — its meaning is explicit, and the closing analyses admit it: disqualifying being's call through boredom acting as a counter-existential aims only to open us to another call, "to any other possible call," or better not to another call but to "the call as such." A "model of the call," a "pure form of the call," replaces the call of being. The reason given: every concrete call, being's included, presupposes as its own possibility a pure structure of the call. "Before being has claimed, the call as pure call claims." "The claim of being itself can call only by assuming this pure form" (Marion pp. 294–297). Henry cites the formulas that will be his target: "model," "pure form," "call as such."

The subsumption of being's call under the pure form of the call gives this problematic of limits a double advantage. It does not contradict the immense thought that nourished it but integrates it — the existential analytic included — into a wider view open to modes of experience other than those inscribed in the "phenomenon of being." And, by reference to any other possible call, it avoids making the refusal of being's call, interpreted as the condition of openness to the call as such, look formal, undetermined and empty. Henry presents fairly the gains Marion's strategy offers before charging that the second gain is not secured.

And so it contradicts the fourth principle. The fourth principle pushes reduction to the limit — reducing beings to being, and finally being's call — only to lead us not to some undetermined X, a model, a pure form, an Absolute or Transcendent defined formally, but to givenness and its height, to the most original appearing. In the fourth principle everything is phenomenological. Just as reduction is phenomenological insofar as, first as pure and then as radical, it takes phenomenality itself as its theme and in it the most primitive mode of its phenomenalization, so the givenness it leads to cannot be thought as form or model but only from the How of that first phenomenalization (pp. 99–100). Henry turns Marion's own principle against Marion's conclusion: a reduction to a "pure form" of the call is a reduction to a concept, not to a givenness.

Marion's thinking does first set out on this path. Why replace being with its call or claim, if not to designate in it what comes first, the flash of an appearing that overwhelms us and, in flashing, makes us be along with it? But if this call of being must be dismissed to open another, more essential call, what does the final substitution mean? Henry's question isolates the ambiguity of Marion's book: is the "other call" a different appearing or a formal generalization?

There is only one answer. If being's call is only its appearing, and the call must give way to another, the opposition can only mean that of two phenomenalities, and so of two phenomenologies. It implies the duplicity of appearing and indicates a hierarchy. Henry reads Marion as unknowingly confirming the duality of appearing — the thesis of *L'essence de la manifestation*.

Of what phenomenology is the call of being the name? Of its ek-static truth. Precisely because Heidegger's being finds its phenomenological substratum there can and must it be criticized; that is why it makes sense to "except oneself" from being. Levinas's "à-Dieu" and "otherwise than being," Marion's "outside being," can dismiss being only because being has usurped even its name — because, in Heidegger at least, it represents only a regional authority. Thought that subordinates ontology to phenomenology sees this unthought but decisive limitation as soon as it performs the subordination. "L'essence de l'Être dont l'appel peut être suspendu, c'est la phénoménalité du monde, la finitude de cet Être, c'est la finitude de tout horizon extatique" (p. 100). The bringing together of Levinas and Marion is pointed: both escape "being" only because Heidegger's being was the world's appearing, a region; what they reach beyond it must therefore be another appearing, not an "otherwise" without phenomenality.

To dismiss being so understood — which "is" only in Dasein's understanding of it as a "meaning" — is possible on one condition: that the ek-static unfolding of appearing in the world's horizon be met by its non-ek-static self-appearing in Life's pathic essence. Dismissing being is possible only phenomenologically: only if, in the absence of any ek-static phenomenality, something is still possible rather than nothing — namely Life's arch-revelation. "« Autrement qu'être » veut dire « apparaître autrement »" (p. 101). Only Life in its arch-revelation still "calls" and can "call" when being has fallen silent. The formula "otherwise than being means appearing otherwise" is the essay's thesis in five words, and Henry's whole response to the French "theological turn": the beyond of being is not a transcendence beyond phenomenality but another phenomenality.

Only because Life's "call" is phenomenologically determined — because in its affective flesh and in the brutality of emotion or love it resembles nothing else, certainly not the unveiling of beings in the world or the world's ek-stasis — can one speak of a call other than being's. There is not, ever, beyond life's pathic irruption in us, beyond its word whose words are our desire, passion or love, a pure form of the call, a call structure higher than or different from that pathos. Henry's rejection of the "pure form" rests on a principle: a call is real only in its phenomenological matter; abstracted from that matter, it is a concept.

The call is always determined. Its determination is phenomenological and is exhausted each time in its actual phenomenality — which is never exhausted: appearing always arises anew, but each time in its own way. To recognize this "way" in the how of its concrete phenomenological materiality is not to stand at a lower level of generality than a pure form or structure. On the contrary, the call structure, usually described by the bipolarity of call and response, borrows that arrangement from a determinate mode of appearing — one in which opposition constitutes phenomenality: the world's. Never mind that the Call/Response pair, replacing the classical Subject/Object dichotomy, claims to renew our relation to being: it merely inverts a relation thought in both cases as constitutive of personality, and so preserves it. Far from escaping Being's call and its implicit phenomenology, the call structure refers back to it and receives from it its "structure": the opposition of ek-stasis. The argument is decisive against Marion: the supposedly formal "call as such" is in fact a call-and-response structure, i.e. two poles across a distance — ek-stasis again. Marion's "interloqué" (the self constituted by the call, at the close of *Réduction et donation*) is criticized here as the subject-object relation reversed.

This opposition is conceived as founding a freedom: the call awaits and elicits a response that may or may not be granted. Marion rests on this freedom of response, of acceptance or rejection, the ultimate possibility for the I-Dasein "no longer to assume the destiny of being as that of its being" (Marion p. 293). The freedom of response is what Henry's life excludes.

What characterizes life's touch is that it precedes any response and awaits none. In life's irruption, in its flood that crosses us and makes us drunk with it and ourselves, there is no Gap, not the slightest retreat in which the possibility of a response, a yes or a no, could form. This impossibility of withdrawal and rejoinder, this being driven back against oneself, adjusted point for point to all one is, is the eternal, irremissible, tireless and serene experience Life has of itself at every instant — "la blessure qu'elle creuse en nous, qui est notre subjectivité même et ce qui fait de nous des vivants" (p. 102). The passivity of essays I–III is turned against the call structure: a call that could be refused presupposes a distance, and life knows none. The "wound" names the same structure as the "self-suffering" of essay II (pp. 52–53).

If there is no room here for a response that would let us assume or refuse the destiny of being, it is because there is, strictly, no call either. Life's call stands beyond every call; "il ne nous fait pas la proposition de vivre" — it has already thrown us into life, crushed us against it and ourselves, in the suffering and enjoying of an invincible pathos. The call has already made us live when we hear it; our hearing is nothing but the sound of life, its murmur in us, the embrace in which it gives itself to itself and us to ourselves in one and the same givenness. "Does not make us the proposal of living" quotes Merleau-Ponty's formula cited in essay I (p. 26: bodily existence "continually makes me the proposal of living"). Henry rejects both: Merleau-Ponty's world and Marion's call alike offer something that could be declined; life does not.

Hence there is nothing in any living being but Life — a life not its own in that it did not create, posit or will it, yet its own, irreducibly and forever, for the same reason: there is nothing in it, not the least experience of the slightest impression, that is not the experience life has of itself, no particle of its Self that is not the Self of Life. That is why we say we are born of Life and that this birth does not cease: in the auto-affection by which we feel ourselves at each instant there is nothing but Life's own auto-affection, its arch-revelation (pp. 102–103). The double sense of "not its own / its own" formulates the late doctrine precisely: received, yet irreducibly mine. "Unceasing birth" is the theme of essay VI.

That is also why the self generated in life — the "me" (*me*) in Marion's terms — is also an "I," an "ego": because life, in the mystics' admirable phrase, "cannot give little"; it can give nothing but itself and so the Ipseity it eternally generates in itself and in all that lives. Henry accepts Marion's accusative "me" (the self as recipient of the call) but restores its nominative force: the receiver of life is a full ego because life gives itself wholly. The phrase "cannot give little" recalls Eckhart and the Rhineland mystics.

Despite its remarkable character, *Réduction et donation* poses formidable problems. Insofar as the question of givenness refers to that of appearing, it repeats its difficulties. Either one gives appearing a particular phenomenological content by slipping behind it the conceptions of phenomenality of twentieth-century phenomenologies; or one wants it "pure" — hence Marion's effort to expel beings from the existential analytic, to wash it of every ontic trace before freeing it of its Heideggerian ek-static structure. The dilemma is exact: givenness either has a phenomenological matter (and then which?) or is pure (and then formal).

But can appearing be stripped of every actual phenomenological meaning and content? What makes it what it is is neither a word nor a concept, nor the undetermined idea of an all-purpose appearing like that of the principles. Appearing is the eternal, tireless coming into itself, the appearing of appearing that appears in a perfectly defined mode of phenomenalization, being in its original self-appearing nothing but life's self-revelation, the auto-impressionality of its pathos, its primitive upsurge in itself. Henry's positive thesis: the only "pure" appearing is life's, and it is perfectly determinate.

How then superimpose on this living process of phenomenalization a "model," a "structure" able to subsume any type of givenness or call? The rhetorical question names the error: subsumption under a form is the operation of concepts, not of givenness.

Pushing to the limit the reduction meant to open it to "pure givenness," to this higher form of "call" dominating every particular call while giving each a pure possibility of calling, phenomenology advances toward a dangerous frontier beyond which it takes leave of itself without suspecting it. Or one might say, paradoxically, that it returns to its starting point, when appearing was a wholly undetermined concept — as if that concept had only been the precursor of the "pure givenness" leading to the call as such, opening us not to another call but to a "model," "a pure form of the call." This pure form resembles the unelaborated, univocal concept of phenomenology's beginnings. At least early phenomenology, giving itself the great structures of intentionality and ek-static phenomenality, went on to do its immense work (pp. 103–104). The essay's arc closes: it began with the indeterminacy of the principles and ends by finding the same indeterminacy at the summit of Marion's reduction. Henry's barbed concession — the founders at least had a determinate (if one-sided) phenomenality to work with — implies that Marion has less.

When the phenomenological element supporting a system of thought falls into total indeterminacy, even empty formalism, remote from any rigorous analysis of the mode of manifestation proper to appearing's coming into itself in its own phenomenological substantiality, pure givenness and the pure forms of call it opens onto would have only a verbal relation to phenomenology. "Verbal relation" is the harshest charge Henry can make: a phenomenology without phenomenological matter is phenomenology in name only.

Pure concepts of givenness, of "model," of "pure forms of call," are in themselves concepts. Like objects of thought, obeying laws of implication and connection, classical discourse is made of such forms, on which attention can focus. If every human community rests on an omnipresent phenomenological life from which it draws its force and certainty, setting phenomenology aside would mean setting aside this hidden, all-powerful life. Its ebbing from culture would leave room for traditional speculation, for a philosophy of language cut off from this substratum of Truth, free to unroll endlessly its verbal inventions and wordplay. The closing jab links Marion's formalism to the culture-wide "philosophy of language" Henry deplores (essay II's swipe at linguistic analysis, p. 40; essay X on language). The stakes are cultural, as in *La barbarie*: a phenomenology that loses its matter abandons life to speculation.

## V. Non-Intentional Phenomenology: A Task for the Phenomenology of the Future

Henry first clarifies the sense of a non-intentional phenomenology. Its project is critical of intentional — that is, classical — phenomenology, and its critical reach extends beyond it to a large part of philosophy in general; it assumes the risk of a claim that may seem excessive. The claim is to circumscribe and denounce a reductive conception of phenomenality that misses the original, fundamental modes in which phenomenality becomes phenomenal. If every philosophy develops, poses and tries to solve its questions on a ground of phenomenological presuppositions, conscious or not, then a critique of phenomenality concerns philosophy as such. To conceive phenomenality otherwise is to impose a new procedure of thought and new fields of inquiry (pp. 105–106). The text was written for a 1992 colloquium in Nice on intentionality between cognitive science and phenomenology and published in Janicaud's volume *L'intentionnalité en question* (1995). The occasion explains the essay's sobriety and its final turn to the social world: Henry is addressing the claim, common to phenomenology and cognitive science, that mind is essentially intentional.

Non-intentional phenomenology also aims to found intentionality. It shows, first, that intentional phenomenology developed while leaving in total — and phenomenological — indeterminacy what ultimately makes intentionality possible. Second, by restoring this foundation phenomenologically, rescuing intentional life from the "anonymity" in which it is lost in Husserl, it reinscribes intentionality in a ground older than itself and recognizes in intentionality the non-intentional that lets it accomplish itself. So reinscribed, intentionality is freed from the uncertainty that alone permits arbitrary or aberrant uses of its concept. The constructive side of the programme should be stressed against the usual reading of Henry as an enemy of intentionality: intentionality is not denied but grounded. The "aberrant uses" glance at the cognitivist reconstruction of intentionality as representation in a physical system.

The debate over whether phenomenology is originally intentional can proceed only from a clear grasp of its object: phenomenality as such, appearing. The definition of phenomenology as method must be set aside as non-original. As method, phenomenology is a process of elucidation, a making-visible, an evidencing — for instance of the sedimented meanings implied in and covered by actual or apparent ones. But this process is possible only on the ground of appearing: only because something appears can its elucidation be pursued. Method refers to phenomenology's object and depends on it. The target is Heidegger's § 7, where phenomenology is "primarily a methodological concept," and the genetic phenomenology of sedimented meaning (*Crisis*, "Origin of Geometry").

Even when method is thought in its reference to appearing, the understanding of appearing is already falsified. The appearing at issue is the one that makes method possible: the appearing whose unfolding opens the field of a seeing and a making-visible, into which intentionality throws itself and in which it relates to whatever it relates to. Phenomenological method — the systematic implementation of an intentional process seeking to make thematically present the meanings intentionality itself constituted or preconstituted in its original syntheses — moves in a domain suited to it by a sort of pre-established harmony, since it is the domain of intentionality in general and of the elucidating intentionality that method is. But the appearing into which intentionality throws itself is the ek-static horizon of visibility. Hence phenomenological method proceeds like the experience of the world, by intentional references seeking intuitive fulfilment, each actual intuition raising around it the infinite chain of new horizons. And hence any critique of intentionality lacks phenomenological meaning as long as it merely refers intentionality back to the ek-static appearing in which it unfolds (pp. 106–107). The "pre-established harmony" between method and object is a vicious circle: a method of seeing can find only what is seen. The final sentence again disqualifies Heidegger's critique of Husserl (as in essay IV, p. 87): referring intentionality to transcendence explains nothing.

Start, then, from phenomenology's object rather than its method. Husserl and his commentators have repeatedly designated it as not things but the way they are given, not objects but "objects in their How" (*Gegenstände im Wie*, Hua X, 117). The phenomenon is considered not in its particular content but in the How of its givenness — its pure phenomenality, not what appears but the mode of its appearing, and finally appearing as such. The same Husserlian phrase anchored essays III (p. 61) and IV (p. 89).

But appearing can give what appears in it only if it appears itself as such. For if appearing did not appear as appearing — given that the thing cannot bring itself into appearing or promote itself to the condition of phenomenon — nothing would appear and there would be nothing at all. "La question de l'apparaître de l'apparaître lui-même, de la donation de la donation elle-même [...] est la question de la phénoménologie, sa « chose même »" (p. 108). The *Sache selbst* of phenomenology's slogan is reclaimed: the "thing itself" is not things but appearing's self-appearing.

Returning to Husserl's definition of phenomenology by its object, the How of givenness, one meets the same equivocation as in its definition as method. The context of "objects in their How" is the analysis of the flow of lived experiences of inner time-consciousness, a flow at once of experiences and of the intentional unities in them. The How of their givenness is clearly indicated: intentionality, in the triple mode of the original syntheses that constitute immanent time — consciousness of the present, retention, protention. "Die Form des Wie ist die Orientierung: das Jetzige, das soeben Vergangene, das Kommende" (p. 108). The time-forming intentionalities thus give immanent experiences; the unities aimed at in these are themselves given by intentionality; so the totality of what is given, transcendent things a fortiori, falls under intentionality, which is the How of givenness as such. Henry shows that Husserl's "How" was never left formal: it was intentionality from the start. That is the second face of the equivocation diagnosed in essay IV.

When givenness is entrusted to intentionality, a very particular phenomenological situation arises, taken as self-evident and universal and no longer reflected on: the power that gives is different from what it gives — appearing from what appears, phenomenality from the phenomenon, intentionality from its noematic correlate, the "objectivity" it makes visible. In a language that shows this historical phenomenology's affinity with classical philosophy, "all consciousness is consciousness of something." On one side appearing (consciousness), on the other something, a being. The being is in itself foreign to appearing and cannot become phenomenal by itself; appearing is necessarily the appearing of something else. Appearing turns away from itself so radically and violently that it is wholly turned toward the other than itself, toward the outside: it is intentionality. Because, as intentionality, appearing is so essentially carried off toward what it makes appear, it is no longer appearing that appears but what it makes appear in it: the being. Phenomenology's object is so denatured that it is no longer appearing but the appearing of beings, and finally beings as they appear (pp. 108–109). Henry inverts the received view that "consciousness of" marked phenomenology's break with classical philosophy: it is rather its continuation — the same structure of a consciousness defined by its object.

The hidden but decisive meaning of the thesis that appearing is the appearing of beings now shows itself. Such appearing is not only very particular, not the original appearing that is phenomenology's "thing itself"; it is not even considered in and for itself. Precisely because it turns away from itself and throws us toward what differs from it — the being, which yet appears only in appearing and insofar as appearing appears as appearing — the fundamental determination, the self-appearing of appearing, is lost, replaced by the relation of appearing to beings: intentionality. In an important 1935 article Fink, restating Husserl's own claims, declares: "Il faut voir, seulement voir." That seeing is the principle and ultimate criterion of knowledge, not to be analysed but only deployed, is said explicitly: one must put vision to work, establish originary evidence as the ultimate criterion; vision legitimates itself only in operation; one cannot go behind vision; vision may be imprecise or incomplete, but only a better vision can correct it; it can "deceive," mis-see, but the possibility of deception contradicts vision so little that only a better vision can rectify it (pp. 109–110, citing "Le problème de la phénoménologie d'Edmund Husserl" in the French collection *De la phénoménologie*). Henry's choice of Fink is strategic: Fink, Husserl's assistant and authorized spokesman, states the method's presupposition in its most uncompromising form. "One cannot go behind vision" is exactly the thesis Henry denies: behind vision there is vision's self-givenness, which is not a vision.

Become the principle and sole criterion of phenomenality, monopolizing appearing and reducing it to its seeing, intentionality does not thereby hold itself within itself: as making-visible, it throws itself toward what is seen, so immediately that its seeing is nothing but the being-seen of the seen — the noematic object whose phenomenological status is defined and exhausted by its condition as object, by being placed there before the gaze. There is no other appearing than that whose phenomenality is this Before as such, pure exteriority, the appearing of beings. The "Before" (*Devant*) translates the *vor* of *Vorstellung*, as in essay III (p. 64).

In intentionality's takeover of appearing, two phenomenological determinations have occurred. First, appearing is nothing but being-seen as such, being-there-before. One speaks of vision, making-visible and seeing, but in this seeing seeing itself is eclipsed, leaving before it only the seen and its being-seen, the "before" as such. Second, this being-seen is invincibly the being-seen of what is seen, the appearing of beings — the appearing that suits beings and no other. The fit is so tight that a reciprocity is established: the relation of appearing to beings is a correlation. Though this is phenomenology, what finally matters is no longer appearing or givenness but the being that gives itself in it. Authentic givenness makes sense only as givenness of the authentic being. If this phenomenology attends not to objects but to how they are given, it is only to reach their true being beyond appearance or inauthenticity: the being must give itself as it is so that we can know it truly. If the givenness at issue in Husserl and Fink implies this essential relation to beings, it is because, as givenness, it is the very view in which beings are originally disclosed in their true being. Fink: "Si un authentique savoir de l'étant doit être possible, il ne peut se fonder que sur un se-manifester, un se-montrer originaire, un apparaître de l'étant authentique." Knowledge — finally appearing — cannot be determined autonomously, independently of beings; it is what it is only as the appearing of beings: "ne pas préconnaître le savoir, mais le déterminer dans son être auprès de l'étant se montrant soi-même, cela signifie faire de l'étant en tant que phénomène la dimension d'origine de la réflexion philosophique" (pp. 110–111). "Correlation" is the key term: the noetic-noematic correlation, Husserl's great discovery, is read as the subordination of appearing to beings. Fink's refusal to "pre-know knowledge" — that is, to ask how knowing is given to itself before being directed at beings — is precisely what Henry demands.

Hence Fink's double conclusion: phenomenality is "the true being of beings"; and phenomenology is "the attempt at a self-grounding of human knowledge by return to the self-givenness of beings" (p. 111). The formulas are cited as the most explicit confession of the position Henry attacks: self-givenness attributed to beings.

The concept of self-givenness Henry introduced at the outset — showing that givenness must itself be given and that only givenness can do this, that appearing could make nothing appear unless it first appeared as such (classically: nothing would be conscious unless consciousness were conscious as pure consciousness, which classical thought never established) — is here applied to beings, as paradoxically as absurdly. Paradoxically, because self-givenness can refer only to givenness, as its first question. Absurdly, because beings, foreign in themselves to givenness, are a priori unable to promote any givenness, still less to give givenness to itself. In fact self-givenness — *Selbstgegebenheit* in Husserl, *Selbstgebung* in Fink — designates phenomenality, but phenomenality thought exclusively as the manifestness of beings. Tied to beings rather than to givenness, the concept is wholly subverted, emptied of possible meaning: it no longer aims at the ultimate inner possibility of givenness but at the mere fact that a being appears (pp. 111–112). This is the essay's pivotal terminological move. Husserl's *Selbstgegebenheit* means "the thing itself is given, in person"; Henry reads *Selbst* as referring to givenness itself. The two meanings mark the difference between phenomenology of the world and phenomenology of life.

How do beings appear, how are they given? "L'hypothèse de la phénoménologie husserlienne, écrit Fink, repose dans la supposition que la conscience originaire entendue de manière intentionnelle est le véritable accès à l'être" — access to being, that is, to beings. Once entrusted to intentionality, appearing is turned away from itself and becomes that of beings, beings as they show themselves. "What is sought," Fink says, "is the self-givenness of beings," and the search unfolds as "the analytico-intentional theory of the self-givenness of beings," Husserl's systematic elucidation of the noetic-noematic correlations of intentional consciousness. Henry does not minimize this immense work and its decisive, often revolutionary results. But the question remains whether, despite them, the systematic elucidation of noetic-noematic correlations truly answers phenomenology's programme of accounting exhaustively for all that is and can be for us. It would only if the Husserlian hypothesis held: that intentionality is the true — and, Henry adds, the only possible — access to being. The concession is real; the objection is that the noetic-noematic programme presupposes what it never examines, intentionality's own givenness.

Here the question of self-givenness, once freed from its ontic bog and no longer improperly referred to beings, shakes classical phenomenology's achievements and claims at their base. If self-givenness is returned to its proper place, givenness itself, and if classical phenomenology's givenness is intentionality, the question becomes: how do we have access to intentionality itself, which gives beings? By another intentionality? It is the very question of intentionality that deposes it from its claim to be "the true access to being." When intentional phenomenology asks itself phenomenology's question — that of givenness, of phenomenality — and so asks it of intentionality, it destroys itself as intentional phenomenology, as phenomenology placing intentionality at the principle of phenomenality (pp. 112–113). The regress argument of essay III (p. 61) is restated as self-refutation: intentional phenomenology cannot answer its own founding question without abandoning its founding thesis.

To avoid misunderstanding: this critique has nothing to do with Heidegger's, that Husserl left the being of intentionality in the dark. Phenomenology is not concerned with being, still less with the meaning of being; being is not its proper theme. There is phenomenology as soon as ontology is regarded as a secondary discipline and put in that place, because phenomenology's theoretical presupposition, first seen and formulated by Descartes, is to subordinate being to appearing. The question of intentionality is therefore not that of its being but of its appearing; and since intentionality is the appearing of beings in its various modes, the question is whether the appearing of intentionality is homogeneous with or identical to that of beings — whether intentionality's appearing derives from intentionality itself. Henry separates his critique sharply from Heidegger's (GA 20, §§ 10–13), which asks about the being of consciousness. His question is phenomenological, not ontological: how does intentionality appear? The priority of appearing over being is again credited to Descartes, as in essay IV (p. 79).

From the start a series of difficulties, even aporias, suggest that intentionality's appearing — the appearing that reveals it as operative intentionality — is not the one it produces, the making-visible that discloses beings. The phenomenality of that making-visible is the being-seen of beings, not seeing itself, which has vanished into that being-seen. "Le voir n'est jamais vu, l'intentionnalité jamais son propre objet" (p. 113). It is not and cannot be. In Husserlian phenomenology as taken up by Fink, the difficulty is that the categories concerning the how of givenness cannot be borrowed from what is given in it. The formula "seeing is never seen" is the volume's most concise statement of the argument: if intentionality were given to itself intentionally, it would be its own object, which it cannot be without ceasing to be the seeing.

One of the last propositions of Fink's article asks: "Can we determine conceptually that through which (*Wodurch*) we have things and objects with the concepts valid for things and objects?" But the fundamental phenomenological concept "through which" we have things and objects is intentionality, and the question implies that intentionality is not an adequate concept for thinking intentionality. That is: intentionality does not accomplish its own revelation; the self-appearing of appearing is not the appearing of beings. Fink's "meontic" problem — the constituting cannot be described in categories drawn from the constituted — is read as evidence for Henry's thesis.

In Husserl the aporia appears in blinding light when the decisive question of the self-revelation of the flow of absolute consciousness — consciousness as the givenness of givenness's self-givenness — is posed explicitly in § 39 of the 1905 time lectures. Husserl appeals to intentionality: since consciousness, and every form of appearing, is intentionality, intentionality must reveal intentionality to itself, and this intentionality constantly relating itself to itself so as to self-reveal is the flow's self-constitution, conceived as the flow's very structure. Thus, by a hidden but extraordinarily violent philosophical decision, the structure of conscious life is bent to the conditions of its self-revelation insofar as that revelation is entrusted to intentionality as the only known appearing. But in this self-constitution one must distinguish, though they are said to coincide, the constituting and constituted phases of the flow, and recognize, with Fink, that the categories suited to constituted phases do not apply to constituting ones, and that we know nothing of the latter. Husserl himself: the phases of the flow in which phases of the same flow are phenomenally constituted cannot be, and are not, identical with the constituted phases (French translation, p. 109). "C'est le principe même de la phénoménalité qui s'abîme dans la nuit" (p. 114). There would be no end to listing the ways in which, in classical phenomenology, appearing manages not to appear; let classical phenomenology unroll the formulations of its major failure, the last of which is no doubt Heidegger's withdrawal of being (pp. 114–115). The "extraordinarily violent decision" is that the structure of consciousness (retention, protention, longitudinal intentionality) is dictated by the only available model of manifestation, not by the phenomena. Heidegger's "withdrawal of being" (*Entzug*) is listed as one more form of the same failure: appearing that does not appear, now raised into a doctrine.

To answer rigorously whether the original appearing that ensures its own appearing is homogeneous with intentionality's making-visible, one must perform the most radical reduction, putting intentionality itself and its making-visible out of play. Only such a reduction can set before us the problem of original appearing as self-appearing — an appearing in which appearing itself appears, and in which it is appearing as such that produces its self-appearing. The radical reduction of essay IV (pp. 90–91) is stated in its simplest form: bracket intentionality and see what remains.

One may ask whether such a self-appearing "exists," or is a speculative and problematic concept. In phenomenology speculative concepts have no place; "exist" can mean only appear. The self-appearing in question is one only in and through its actual appearing, in its phenomenological actuality, as such incontestable. What must be clearly seen is that the very nature of this appearing must make it possible as self-appearing. Were it intentionality's making-visible, which constantly turns from itself and brings into appearing only what differs from it, it would not occur as self-appearing — which is intentionality's case, always carried off toward things and objects. The essence that makes appearing possible as self-appearing is a phenomenological essence: the pure phenomenality of this appearing, its phenomenological matter, the pure substance of which it is made. The methodological point of essay II (p. 50) recurs: auto-affection is not a postulate inferred as a condition but a phenomenon, and its possibility depends on its phenomenological matter.

Where do we see such a self-appearing? Nowhere. This means either that it does not exist, or that, heterogeneous to intentionality's seeing and in principle withdrawn from it, it cannot be seen by it. What forbids dismissing the second possibility is that it is the case of intentionality itself — and so of modern consciousness as "consciousness of," of knowledge, and of science as a form of knowledge. The argument is a tu quoque: intentionality cannot be seen either, yet no one denies that it exists. Whatever gives intentionality to itself must therefore be something other than seeing.

That original self-appearing escapes intentionality's seeing — and first of all phenomenological method as an intentional method — leaves only one possibility: that it appears by itself, through and in its own phenomenality, asking nothing of intentionality's seeing or a world's visibility. Only outside intentionality, independently of every ek-static horizon of visibility, is the arch-revelation constituting appearing's self-appearing accomplished — "arch-revelation" because, occurring outside and independently of ek-stasis, it occurs "before" it. This arch-revelation as self-appearing is the most mysterious but also the simplest and most common thing, what everyone knows: life (p. 116). The paradox of the most mysterious and the most common is a constant in Henry: life is invisible to thought yet known by every living being in living.

Life is phenomenological in an original, founding sense: not as one phenomenon showing itself among others, but as creative of phenomenality. Phenomenality arises originally with life, in the form of life, and in no other way. It finds its original essence in life because life experiences itself, and this self-experience is the self-appearing of appearing. "Creative of phenomenality" is the strongest statement of the thesis that life is not something that appears but the original appearing itself.

The proposition must be understood with absolute rigour. Life's appearing makes nothing else appear, does not turn away from itself, institutes no difference; only on this condition is it not the appearing of something else, a being, but a self-appearing — only on this condition is life life. The phenomenality of this self-appearing, the phenomenological substance of this pure self-experiencing, is "une affectivité transcendantale" that alone makes possible something like fear, suffering or joy — something that experiences itself and relates only to itself, whose self-relation is its affectivity. So with all modalities of life, revealed to themselves in and through the immanence of this pure affectivity. Affectivity is life's phenomenological essence, "cette chair impressionnelle où le voir de l'intentionnalité n'a aucune part — en ce sens le non-intentionnel pur" (pp. 116–117). "Transcendental affectivity" distinguishes the condition from its empirical modalities (fear, joy): the guard against reading Henry as a psychologist of emotion. The equation affectivity = flesh = the non-intentional gives the essay's title its positive content.

Husserl's phenomenology did not ignore the non-intentional; it designated it, as *hylē*, a fundamental layer of consciousness. In a few very rare texts of the time lectures, *hylē* appears not as a mere component of consciousness but as what lets consciousness be consciousness, giving it to itself before it gives anything. But this decisive function of the *Urimpression*, which would make hyletic phenomenology the fundamental discipline of phenomenology, is immediately reversed: *hylē* becomes a matter for a form that alone can illuminate it and make it a phenomenon — intentionality (Henry refers to "Phénoménologie hylétique et phénoménologie matérielle," the first chapter of *Phénoménologie matérielle*). The original essence of phenomenality as the revelation of the impression as impression — its impressionality, its affectivity — is concealed, and promotion to phenomenon is again entrusted, according to a tradition that runs through and corrupts all Western thought, to the light of an Outside. With this concealment of phenomenality's secret source by ek-static phenomenality, nothing less than the concrete life of human beings is removed from philosophical thought, which is thereby handed over — by phenomenology itself — to exteriority and finally to a ruinous objectivism coinciding with that of Galilean science, which defined itself by excluding subjectivity, that is, life, from the rational knowledge of the material universe (p. 117). The *Ideas I* schema (*hylē* animated by *morphē*, § 85) is the target: Husserl turned the impression into stuff awaiting form. Henry's indictment links phenomenology's intentionalism to Galilean objectivism, the theme of *La barbarie* and essay X: the *Crisis*'s critique of Galileo turns against Husserl.

The phenomenology of life also proceeds by a reduction, a radical phenomenological reduction that has nothing to do with the Galilean reduction or with Husserl's; it acts toward both as a "contre-réduction." It is radical because it concerns no being but phenomenality itself. In an unheard-of act it puts out of play the phenomenality of the world, the ek-static horizon in which, since Greece, everything that can show itself and become the object of knowledge — scientific knowledge especially — is believed to show itself. When the world's horizon is suspended, there remains only an arch-revelation bringing itself into itself in and through its own phenomenality: life's non-ek-static pathos. Descartes performed this radical reduction in article 26 of *The Passions of the Soul*: holding doubtful everything that shows itself in a world, and so the world's phenomenality, likened to that of a dream, he retains as valid within this universal dream only what owes its phenomenalization not to the world but to its affectivity — fear, for instance, and all the soul's immanent modalities. If I dream, everything I see is false; but if in the dream I feel fear, that fear exists absolutely as I feel it (p. 118). *Passions* § 26 is Henry's favourite Cartesian text: the passions are certain even in dreams, because their being is their being felt. "Counter-reduction" means that, where Galileo bracketed sensible qualities to keep the measurable, and Husserl bracketed existence to keep the intentional correlate, Henry brackets the world's appearing to keep what both had excluded.

Does the radical reduction to invisible, acosmic pathic life not turn us from the world and throw us into a self-enclosed mysticism given over to its night? In conclusion Henry wishes to show that only a non-intentional phenomenology can found, first, our opening to the world as such and, second, the world's content. On the second point: the unveiling in which the world's appearing consists does not account for what it unveils, the being. Being unveiled in the world's ek-stasis does give a being certain irreducible phenomenological features, notably being unveiled as other in the otherness of a horizon. But that is a general trait that says nothing of each being's specificity; throwing the being outside itself and emptying it of its reality, it installs it in an unreality of principle. In the world no real content, no real "being," is possible. The world's appearing cannot distinguish the things a poet speaks of from those really around me in the room where I read; nor can Husserl's separation of intentionalities. For one perceives, in the sense of perception, only on condition of a sensation — that is, of life (pp. 118–119). The poet's things and the room's things are given in the same ek-static mode; what distinguishes perception from imagination is the presence of sensation, which is life. This is the volume's sharpest rejoinder to the charge of acosmism: the reality of the world itself comes from life.

Henry's thesis on the world's "content" in brief: it is only the objectification of life, so that the "reality" of this content is life itself, while its worldly presentation is only a re-presentation, the "phenomenon" of this life in the sense of what does not contain its reality, which lies nowhere but in its own pathos. The world's content — leaving aside, as Marx said ironically, a few Pacific coral reefs — is social praxis, so that the laws governing this social substance are not those of the world's phenomenality, of its putting-into-image (of which Kant gave an elaborate structure), but invisible, pathic laws, the laws of life. It is the rooting of force in affectivity, the irruption of drive under the weight of affect, the indefinite iteration in life of need and action, that determines every society, and so the world's content, as a society of production and consumption. The quip about coral reefs is from *The German Ideology*: there is almost no "nature" left untouched by human activity. Henry's social phenomenology is Marx read as a phenomenologist of living labour, with Freud's drive added: force rooted in affect.

A non-intentional phenomenology not only shows that the objectivity of the world's content is an appearance, not its original and real phenomenological status; it also explains how and why this apparent objectivity of social reality is legitimated and theoretically grounded. Life's phenomenological status implies and requires theoretical objectification. Life produces nature as the totality of use-values it needs; their exchange presupposes measuring the living labour that produced them; but that labour, as a modality of subjective life, is neither measurable nor qualitatively assessable; so the economy substitutes for these pathic activities a set of ideal objective equivalents, which constitute the economy. The economy is only life's ideal double, intelligible only from life. Today the economy is destroyed from within by the invasion of Galilean technology, which, eliminating living labour from the production process, eliminates its ideal equivalent — money — and makes exchange, and the economy itself, progressively impossible. Since everything happening before our eyes, the immense threat spreading over the Earth, is ultimately explained by the laws of life, which result from its own phenomenality, only a non-intentional phenomenology can understand our world by producing the transcendental genesis of the many substitutes for life that make up its "rational," calculable structure — substitutes invented by life itself in order to continue and increase itself (pp. 119–120). The argument compresses *Marx* and *Du communisme au capitalisme* (1990): value is the ideal equivalent of unmeasurable living labour, and automation, by expelling living labour, destroys the source of value. The "transcendental genesis" of economic categories from life is the counterpart, in social theory, of Husserl's genesis of logical categories from perception.

And our access to the world? In Husserl it is entrusted to intentionality, which does not bring itself into appearing, so that, left to itself, an unconscious vision, it would see nothing and open us to nothing. That seeing does not see itself leaves it able to see anything only on condition of its self-givenness in an arch-revelation foreign to all seeing, life's. "Il n'y a pas d'intentionnalité mais seulement une vie intentionnelle" (p. 120). To recognize the phenomenality proper to this life — the pathic auto-affection that makes it possible as life, radically heterogeneous to intentionality's seeing — is the task of non-intentional phenomenology. The formula "there is no intentionality, only intentional life" sums up the founding project announced at the outset (p. 106). Intentionality is an achievement of life, possible only because it is first given to itself.

Because intentionality is possible only as intentional life, it is not an indeterminate or indifferent gaze; the meanings it constitutes draw their deep motivations from life and ultimately derive from it. The difference among intentionalities, Husserl's brilliant discovery, must refer to differences in life. As the content of what is seen refers to life, of which it is mostly only a substitute, so does the way we see it. The universe of representation is never explained by representation, as modern thought from Schopenhauer to Freud undertook to say. But the ultimate principle of its explanation cannot be relegated to a back-world, left to speculation or fables. The more the principle of what we see escapes seeing, the more strongly the need for a non-intentional phenomenology is felt, and the more its power will impose itself (pp. 120–121). Schopenhauer's will and Freud's drives rightly explain representation by what is not representation, but they make the principle a speculative unconscious; Henry's alternative is a phenomenology of that principle. The theme is worked out in *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*.

Non-intentional phenomenology does not only make the world intelligible; it has its own domain, the immense domain of life, for whose exploration we have so far had only fragmentary indications or abrupt intuitions, which the arts and other forms of spirituality have offered more generously than philosophy. To recognize this domain in its specificity, trace continuous paths in it, and develop adequate methodologies is the task of non-intentional phenomenology — "une des tâches sans doute de la phénoménologie de demain" (p. 121). The reference to the arts points to *Voir l'invisible* (1988), Henry's book on Kandinsky, where abstract painting is read as the expression of life's invisible pathos. The modest ending — "one of the tasks" — answers the colloquium's question: a phenomenology of the future must take life as its own field, not as the ground it presupposes.

## VI. Phenomenology of Birth

What does it mean to be born? Is it not to come into being, to enter existence, outside, before and after which there is nothing? The question of birth thus seems to refer at once to the question of being that dominates our tradition. That in this tradition being is not truly thematized for itself but confused with beings and understood from them does not contradict the primacy of the question of being; rather, it demands that this primacy be fully established and philosophy reformulated accordingly. Henry opens with the Heideggerian position at its strongest, as the natural frame for the question of birth. The essay appeared in *Alter* 2 (1994), an issue on "temporality and affection," and belongs to the years of *C'est moi la vérité*.

But thematizing being for itself neither brings philosophy before its proper task nor truly corrects the tradition, because being is never first, holding its being only from appearing as such. "So much appearing, so much being" is flouted when ontology's traditional theme again becomes philosophy's, and still more when that theme is assigned to phenomenology itself, as in the "retrograde" declaration that, "taken at the level of its subject matter (*sachhaltig*), phenomenology is the science of the being of beings" (pp. 123–124, citing Heidegger, GA 20, p. 223). The quotation from the 1925 *Prolegomena* is chosen because there Heidegger explicitly subordinates phenomenology to ontology. "Retrograde" turns Heidegger's charge against tradition back on him: ontology is the old prejudice.

The claim to bring phenomenology back to ontology, making it the instrument of a thought of being, is "une illusion langagière" that leaves untouched the grounding of ontology in phenomenology, since by "beings" there is only what appears and by "being" only appearing itself. The linguistic universality of the question of being has only formal precedence, if what being amounts to is decided each time in the concrete modes of phenomenality's actual phenomenalization, not in its empty concept or pre-concept. "Pre-concept" targets the "vague, average understanding of being" with which *Sein und Zeit* begins (§ 2). Henry's objection: a pre-understanding of a word is no access to a phenomenon.

The question of birth shatters this precedence of ontology, the presumed universality of the sense of being. Birth cannot mean coming into being so long as being claims the status of a universal principle. Coming into being is the lot of every being — stone, air, water — yet none of these is born or dies except metaphorically. Being born belongs to the living, and only to them; it cannot fall under being as universal, under a "sense of being in general" fitting stone and living being alike. This sudden unsuitability of the sense of being demands a re-evaluation of ontology's theme amounting to its dismantling — a dismantling ontology cannot perform itself. Only a radical and pure phenomenology, setting aside every foreign presupposition and thematizing appearing in its own phenomenality each time, can recognize and so dissociate the appearing in which the living being's coming-to-be consists from that to which all worldly beings and the world itself owe their being — so that the "being" of birth's coming-to-be and the world's have nothing in common except the secret phenomenological interweaving of the modes of appearing to which being refers in each case (p. 124). The argument from birth is elegantly simple: a universal concept of being cannot distinguish being born from merely beginning to exist. The "secret interweaving" of the two modes of appearing announces the essay's later thesis that world-appearing is itself self-affected in life.

The phenomenological transcription of ontological themes is possible because, just as phenomenology never appeared in pure form in past philosophy, traditional ontology never did without a phenomenological foundation that determined it unawares. The appearing governing traditional ontology is the world's, whose phenomenality consists in the "outside." Transcribed, coming into being means coming into the world. And is that not what everyone, philosopher or not, takes birth to mean? The everyday equation of birth with "coming into the world" is shown to rest on the ontology of exteriority.

The difficulty of attributing being in the same sense to a being like a stone, shown in the world, and to the living one that comes into the world at birth and to which the world shows itself, though real, seems surmountable — for a phenomenological reason: the appearing in which the stone shows itself is the same as the one to which the living opens, the world's appearing. One appearing gives being to stone and living being, so a single being rules over both, being universal, its universality resting on that of the appearing it refers to. Henry restates the univocity of being as a consequence of the univocity of appearing.

Ask fundamental phenomenological questions. How does appearing give being to the stone? By making it appear. How? By making it seen. How? The stone is seen as placed outside itself; being-outside-itself defines its appearing and so its being, being as such. If being can be further thought as temporality, it is because "temporality is the original 'outside itself' in and for itself" (p. 125, *Sein und Zeit*, p. 329). The chain of "how?" questions is Henry's method in miniature: each step descends from what appears to the mode of appearing.

How does appearing give being to the living one? Again by making it seen, placing it outside itself, so that it is seen like the stone? Then the living one's phenomenological situation would equal the stone's, and their difference would not be an essential phenomenological difference generating an ontological one, but an ontic difference. Yet the living one sees the stone, while the stone sees nothing. The paradox is complete beneath the obviousness: how could two beings — provisionally still calling the living one a being — with the same phenomenological status differ phenomenologically in the most essential sense, one having a relation to phenomenality, the other lacking it? How could two things coming into being in the same appearing, lit by the same light, differ so that the living one is transformed inwardly into this light, becoming nothing but it, while the stone, lit by the same light, stays foreign to it, blind and opaque forever, in a night so deep we can conceive it only by abstract negation of the world's clarity (pp. 125–126)? The asymmetry "the living sees, the stone does not" cannot be accounted for by a light that falls equally on both. Heidegger's own triad (the stone is worldless, the animal poor in world, man world-forming, GA 29/30) is implicitly in view: worldhood presupposes an access to appearing that the world cannot supply.

A crucial difficulty arises. That one and the same appearing sometimes lets an x appear outside itself, x being deprived in itself of this appearing's phenomenality, and sometimes grants x, now y, the unheard-of privilege of receiving it inwardly, identifying with it and becoming a pure phenomenological matter — this double condition, definable only phenomenologically, is nevertheless independent of the nature of this appearing, since appearing is supposed to be the same in both cases. Being inside or outside appearing owes nothing to appearing's essence and everything to what is originally and in itself heterogeneous to appearing — to beings! The exclamation mark registers the absurdity: a phenomenological difference would be decided by something non-phenomenological.

In classical thought the absurdity is that it cannot in the least legitimate the distinction on which it rests and which it holds essential: subject and object. The object is the being seen in its appearing, which is being-outside-itself, so that the ob-ject is the being outside itself, seen as such. How can the subject differ from the ob-ject, from the outside-itself in which beings appear, if there is no appearing but this outside-itself, if its "consciousness" is reduced to it — a condition recognized by the "progress" of the philosophy of consciousness, by phenomenology above all, for which consciousness is consciousness of something, intentionality, or, in its final definition, *In-der-Welt-sein* (pp. 126–127)? The hyphenation *ob-jet* (thrown-before) shows that subject and object share one phenomenality; nothing then distinguishes them. The scare quotes on "progress" mark Henry's view that the development from consciousness to being-in-the-world only consummates the reduction of subjectivity to exteriority.

Birth interpreted as coming into being can mean only coming into appearing, interpreted as coming into the world — which designates two entirely different conditions, of which only one bears on birth and can define it, while the intraworldly being, merely entering and leaving the finite place of light that is the world, is in itself foreign to birth and to death. The phenomenality by which "coming into the world" is defined thus allows no distinction between what can be born and what cannot. The image of things entering and leaving "a finite place of light" is the Heideggerian clearing (*Lichtung*) as a stage on which beings appear and disappear — a model of manifestation, not of birth.

The difficulty cannot be removed as long as being born is defined as coming into the world. Related to *In-der-Welt-sein*, the phenomenon of birth also loses its apparently essential link to life. To clarify this, Henry examines the relation of being-in-the-world to life, treated briefly in § 10 of *Sein und Zeit*. There, the great contemporary philosophies of life — to be set aside, their theses reduced to a few random citations — replace any consideration of life itself, whose analysis is reduced to the statement that life is "a particular kind of being" (*eine eigene Seinsart*). Poor as it is, the statement is still erroneous, since life is no kind of being nor any being whose phenomenological presupposition is the "outside itself." Yet Heidegger's explicit thesis is: "life is a particular kind of being, but essentially it is accessible only in Dasein" (p. 127, *Sein und Zeit*, p. 50). Henry's critique of § 10 is a constant (cf. *C'est moi la vérité*, *Incarnation*): Heidegger dismisses Dilthey, Bergson and Scheler in a few lines and then makes life accessible only through Dasein, i.e. through the world.

What does being born mean when access to life belongs to Dasein alone? This access is at first disconcerting, being not specifically access to life but to any intraworldly being. To reach the living being insofar as access consists in its self-exteriority — as with the stone or Fichte's wall — is at most to see it as one sees stone or wall (without knowing why one attributes life to it); it is in no way to become alive oneself. Yet that is what being born means: to reach life so as to become alive oneself, sharing henceforth in the unbreakable experience of living, its suffering and enjoying. Birth is not a cognitive access to life but entry into it. That distinction suffices to rule out Heidegger's formula.

Can Dasein found access to non-living beings, to living beings, and to life in the sense of becoming alive itself? For non-living beings, Dasein is nothing but the "outside itself" in which every being shows itself as "other," "facing." For living beings, Dasein as outside-itself certainly makes them visible like any other; but that this being is discovered as "living" no longer derives from Dasein at all. An essential law of appearing whose phenomenality is the outside-itself shows here: the disclosure it performs in no way explains what it discloses — in this case, the being's character as living. But if being alive, or the mere meaning of being so, does not come from Dasein's appearing, where is its phenomenological origin? The "law" is the one stated in essay III (p. 63): world-appearing shows but does not create. Applied to the living, it means the world cannot even supply the meaning "alive."

Why Dasein's appearing, the outside-itself, cannot give access to life is perfectly comprehensible. In the outside-itself each "point" of the "medium" is not only external to every other, as in space; it is also outside itself, so that this "outside itself" is its appearing — an appearing that admits only the exterior, the other. In this medium of radical exteriorization no living is possible, if living is experiencing oneself and not something else — experiencing oneself so immediately that nothing can break the pathic flesh of this experience, and any setting-outside of this essentially non-ek-static pathos could only destroy it. "L'hétérogénéité phénoménologique matérielle de l'apparaître pathétique en lequel la vie s'accomplit et de l'apparaître extatique qui se déploie dans la différence du monde et des choses, est radicale, irréductible, insurmontable" (pp. 128–129). The distinction between spatial exteriority (points external to one another) and ek-static exteriority (each point external to itself) corresponds to that between *partes extra partes* and Heidegger's temporal ek-stasis; the second is more radical because it splits each moment from itself.

What does "life accomplishes itself" mean? (Heidegger asked the same ironically about Scheler's "accomplishment" of acts: "what is the meaning of 'accomplishing'?" — *Sein und Zeit*, p. 48.) It means that life auto-affects itself, that the phenomenological matter of this original affection is a pathos, that this pathic auto-affection is a process — the eternal process in which life comes and keeps coming into itself, so that there is no other way to reach life and its living than within this process and through it. Hence no access to life's living is possible in a world's appearing, where life can be represented only as an unreal noematic meaning, of which the objective character "being alive" is only the statement. Hence also the illusion that access to life occurs in Dasein's being-in-the-world implies a ruinous confusion between life's living and the external appearance of living beings, whose property of being such refers to and presupposes this living rather than founding or exhibiting it. Henry answers Heidegger's ironic question about Scheler with the essay's thesis: "accomplishing" is life's self-affecting process, not an act.

Dasein's inability to give access to life's living concerns Dasein first: it is the inability to open itself to life by becoming alive itself. As the outside-itself, in which no living is possible, Dasein is in principle unable to come into life. Heidegger's philosophy confesses this unconsciously, in a problematic meant to establish against all scepticism the original character of truth — which holds because, with the disclosedness of being-in-the-world as Dasein's disclosedness, truth is always presupposed, and so is Dasein as mine. From the presupposition of Dasein's truth as what always precedes and need not be questioned arises the abrupt question: "Has Dasein, as itself free, ever decided, and will it ever be able to decide, whether it wants to come into 'Dasein' or not?" (pp. 129–130, *Sein und Zeit*, p. 228). The passage is § 44c on the presupposition of truth: the sceptic cannot be refuted, because Dasein never chose to exist. Henry reads Heidegger's aside as an involuntary admission that coming into Dasein is not Dasein's doing.

For Dasein, coming into Dasein is being born. Whether Dasein freely decided to be born calls for an immediate no. That is indeed a feature of birth and its mystery: birth is a kind of absolute beginning, and yet this absolute beginning presupposes a "before it." It is this antecedence to the absolute beginning of birth, a "before it" more absolute than it, that makes birth a limit-experience, referring in its very self-experience to what seems impossible to experience. That there is no free decision about Dasein's coming into itself — which would presuppose being placed before birth so as to decide about it — goes without saying. What must be understood is precisely this impossibility of taking any stance toward it. Two misreadings must be avoided: the existentialist one — Sartre's man "condemned" to be free — which treats the impossibility as merely factual; and Heidegger's, which, reducing the "not" of "cannot" to the *Nichts* of Being and so to ek-static phenomenality, denatures still more surely the decisive phenomenon of Dasein's inability to decide whether to come into itself (Henry refers to his critique of Heideggerian *Nichtigkeit* in *L'essence de la manifestation*, §§ 42–43). The "before more absolute than the absolute beginning" is the essay's central figure. Sartre makes the impossibility a brute fact; Heidegger makes it a structure of thrownness, i.e. of the nothingness in the ek-static horizon. Both miss its positive content, which is life's prior givenness.

These two misreadings can be avoided only if the question receives a radically phenomenological meaning, since decision, freedom and willing are not at issue. Transcribed phenomenologically, it asks: how can one come into Dasein, how does the mode of revelation that gives access to Dasein reveal? That Dasein does not decide to come into Dasein — that, as another proposition says, it is "not brought into its 'there' by itself" (*nicht von ihm selbst in sein Da gebracht*, *Sein und Zeit*, p. 284) — means phenomenologically: it is not in Dasein, in the outside-itself of being-in-the-world, that one comes into Dasein so as to take place in it, identify with it, and, once there, relate with it to the world, "have" a world, be in the world. Our coming into the world presupposes our coming into Dasein, which is not Dasein's doing. How do we come into Dasein such that this coming owes nothing to it, nothing to the outside-itself of being-in-the-world? In and through life. Far from our having access to life only in Dasein, it is only in life and through it that we have access to Dasein (pp. 130–131). The reversal of Heidegger's thesis of p. 127 is exact: not life accessible only in Dasein, but Dasein accessible only in life. Heidegger's thrownness (§ 58, "not brought into its there by itself") becomes evidence for a prior givenness.

If Dasein is itself possible only as living Dasein, the question of birth unfolds thus: coming into the world presupposes coming into being-in-the-world, into Dasein; but coming into Dasein, which does not bring itself into itself, owes it nothing. Far from solving the question of coming into the world, the question of coming into Dasein is an entirely new phenomenological question, referring to a mode of revelation structurally different from Dasein's. If we come into Dasein only in life, the question of birth is displaced entirely: it leaves the world and being-in-the-world, and nothing pertaining to them — existential or categorial structures alike — concerns it. The question becomes how one comes into life. Understood originally, it refers to an original birth, an "Ur-naissance" having nothing to do with what we first and naively call birth, and escaping both the sciences of the world and the traditional philosophy that only thematized our relation to it. "Ur-birth" (*Ur-naissance*) is formed on Husserl's *Ur-impression*: it is the primal givenness of the living self, not a biological event.

To be born is therefore to come into life. The proposition has at least two senses, the most immediately graspable not the most essential. Coming into life means coming to life, entering it, acceding to the extraordinary and mysterious condition of being a living being — mysterious because of life's phenomenological status, whose elucidation is a main task of a phenomenology of birth, possible only within a phenomenology of life as one of its branches. But it is the second sense that matters now: coming into life means that this coming can occur only in life and from it. Coming into life means coming from life: life is not the point of arrival of birth but its point of departure. This rooting of every birth as Ur-birth in the prior essence of life explains why man as living — not intraworldly man, the man-entity endowed with the property of being alive, but true man, whose whole essence is living — why this original man, provisionally called the living ego, is nothing originary, nothing intelligible from itself, but only from the essence of life that eternally precedes him in the very process by which it constantly engenders him as its necessary result, constantly gives birth to him (pp. 131–132). The double sense of "into" (*dans*: entering, and within) turns birth from an arrival into a provenance. The phrase "true man" (*l'homme véritable*) anticipates *C'est moi la vérité*'s contrast between "man" as worldly entity and the "Son."

How does life engender the ego in itself, making it a living ego? Insofar as and in the way that it engenders itself. Life engenders itself in the process of its eternal auto-affection, in which it comes into itself, "s'écrase contre soi," experiences itself, enjoys itself, being nothing but "l'éternelle félicité de cette pure jouissance de soi" (p. 132). Living consists in this pure self-experience as pure self-enjoyment, possible only thus and existing nowhere else. That this auto-affection is a process means it occurs as a movement, the self-movement in which life constantly comes into itself and experiences itself. This self-movement is a specific temporalization: radically immanent, non-ek-static, pathic self-temporalization, which is the very temporalization of birth (to be returned to). The principle "as life engenders itself, so it engenders the ego" is the Johannine-Eckhartian structure (the Father begets the Son as he begets himself). "Self-temporalization" will be developed at the end of the essay as the answer to how birth can be continuous.

In this self-temporalization of life's self-experiencing auto-affection an Ipseity is built, since living's experience is possible only as the experience it constantly has of itself — so that its structure is identically that of a Self. Life engenders itself as a Self. But no Self is possible except as a singular Self, since the self-experience accomplished in life's pathic auto-affection is an actual one, necessarily this experience with its own singular content, so that the Self implied in it is actual and singular too. Strictly, it is not the particular content that particularizes the Self, but the Self's singularity, engendered with it and in the same way and belonging to it in principle, that singularizes any "content" it can experience. "Moi-même je suis ce Soi singulier engendré dans l'auto-engendrement de la vie absolue et ne suis que cela. La vie s'auto-engendre comme moi-même" (p. 133). The generation of the singular Self that I am in absolute life's self-engendering is my transcendental birth. The reversal of the order of individuation — singularity is not derived from the content but singularizes the content — is decisive against any empiricist account of the self as a bundle. "Life engenders itself as myself" transposes Eckhart's "God engenders himself as me" (essay III, p. 69).

Since this transcendental birth occurs from life — in it — so that my generation as singular Self is possible only in absolute life's self-generation and as its effectuation, the two cannot be separated: the singular Self comes to itself only in absolute life's coming into itself and bears it within as its never-abolished presupposition, its condition. Life thus traverses each one it engenders, so that nothing in it is not alive, nothing does not contain life's eternal essence. "La vie m'engendre comme elle-même." Generated as singular Self in my transcendental Ur-birth, I am born; since this Ur-generation is only a mode of the self-generation of absolute life, which is not born, I am not-born (p. 133). The paradox "born and not-born" (*né / non-né*) echoes Eckhart's sermon on poverty ("in my unborn mode I can never die") and announces the essay's last movement: how the born self relates to the unborn life.

Henry deepens the two points in turn. First: why does the singular Self generated in absolute life present itself as a transcendental "me" (*moi*) or "ego"? "Transcendental" because at issue is its innermost possibility, its ipseity. "Me" and "ego" are not the same, though classical thought slides between them in extreme confusion without seeing a problem in the double designation. That the singular Self is and must first be said in the accusative, as a "me," translates the fact that it is engendered: it did not bring itself into its condition of experiencing itself as a Self but holds this condition only from life's eternal auto-affection. Because this engendering is phenomenological in a radical sense — life's original phenomenalization as its immanent pathic temporalization — what motivates putting the self in the accusative is legible in the self: its feeling of being fundamentally passive not only toward its own "states," even the most active, but first toward itself and its own "condition." I am myself without having had any part in this "being myself"; I experience myself without being the source of this experience; I am given to myself without this givenness depending on me in any way. I affect myself — I am affected, and by myself, the content affecting me being still myself and nothing else (the felt, touched, willed, desired, thought) — but this auto-affection defining my essence is not my doing. So I do not affect myself absolutely; I am auto-affected, and thus engendered as a Self in life's auto-affection. "Moi désigne en fin de compte ce caractère d'être auto-affecté du Soi singulier que je suis" (p. 134). The grammatical analysis is the essay's sharpest conceptual contribution: the accusative "me" (French *moi* as object case, as in "il me donne") names the self as recipient. Henry distinguishes "I auto-affect myself" (which would make me my own origin) from "I am auto-affected" (the content is myself, but the affecting is not my doing). This corrects any reading of auto-affection as self-production.

Since, engendered in absolute life, the Self experiences itself passively and so as a "me," it is not only a "me": experiencing itself in the immediacy of the suffering and enjoying that constitute this experience, it comes into possession of itself and of each of the powers traversing it, and is able to exercise them. A new capacity is conferred, as extraordinary as being a "me," though it is its simple consequence: the capacity to be in possession of oneself, at one with oneself and all that one bears and owns as the real components of one's being — among them the body's powers, to grasp, move, touch. Because the self can use each power only in its pathic experience of it, this experience is the ultimate power gathering all these powers, the ultimate capacity by which it disposes of all capacities. Advancing armed with all these powers and holding them at its disposal, this "me" that has taken hold of itself and of all that lives in it is an "I" (*je*) (pp. 134–135). The passage from accusative "me" to nominative "I" is the passage from passivity to power. Biran's "I can" (essay I) is thus grounded in the passivity of the self toward itself: I can only because I am first given to myself with my powers.

"Je s'écrit : je Peux" (p. 135). "I can" is not a synthetic proposition; no power is added to the essence of the I, which is the Power as such — the ultimate Power of being in possession of all its powers, on the ground of the "me" within it. Husserl's "Ich, ego…" (*Cartesian Meditations*, § 44) should be taken neither as an accident of writing nor as a tautology: the ego is an ego by being a "me." "Me, ego…" marks the completion of my transcendental birth. Henry reads Husserl's apposition (in the reduction to the sphere of ownness) as a compressed genealogy: the *moi* (given to itself) grounds the *ego* (the power that acts).

The ego that, by being a "me," has taken hold of its powers to exercise them henceforth freely and indefinitely lives itself as the "hyperpower" inhabiting all its native capacities and functions, by which it puts them into play at will. Possessing them makes it free to will their exercise. This free will is nothing but the being-in-possession of these powers in the hyperpower of their pathic experience, and is founded on it. We are never free toward anything external, but only within this hyperpower that has placed us inside each of our powers to exercise them. The ego is free only on the ground of a "me" that necessarily precedes it, ultimately of the Self engendered in absolute life (pp. 135–136). Freedom is grounded in passivity, not opposed to it — against Sartre's freedom without ground and Kant's spontaneity.

But the ego that bears all this and results from it lives itself as the fundamental "I can" described brilliantly by Biran and, on the ground of this egoity, as a free will. The metaphors in which the ego spontaneously expresses its condition, or what it naively takes it to be, are telling. Interior to the power, being only that power's interiority to itself, it takes itself for the power's source, origin, the place where power takes shape, gathers itself and, gathered, springs forth — the starting point, the centre from which it acts and radiates. In § 25 of *Ideas II* there is a constellation of such metaphors, all designating the ego as starting point, source-point, centre of radiation, *terminus a quo* from which everything begins and is possible. The ego's self-image as source is called "naive": it mistakes being at the centre of its powers for being their origin. This is the "transcendental illusion of the ego," a central theme of *C'est moi la vérité*.

In these texts, as in all those concerning the ego, Husserl's interpretation is overdetermined by an unquestioned and reductive conception of phenomenality that also governs European thought. The ego is linked to consciousness (in extreme confusion), understood as intentionality, so that the starting point, source-point, centre of radiation and of function is that of intentionality. This superimposition shows everywhere — e.g.: "All the multiform particularizations of the relation to objects, here called acts, have their necessary *terminus a quo*, the ego-punctum, from which they radiate" (*Ideas II*, French trans. pp. 157–158). From this overdetermination of the ego-punctum by the presupposition of intentional and ek-static phenomenality — the presupposition also obeyed by a metaphysics of representation — comes the concept of "subject" that dominates modern philosophy, so that even the many critiques of it in this half-century share its illusory presuppositions unawares (Henry again cites "La critique du sujet"). The ego has indeed become the subject, the subject of representation, the "I represent to myself" before which, to which and for which everything is dis-posed. Yet the ego is not explained by the structure of representation, even if it makes it possible. Proof: the phenomenological essence of representation can be deployed without any ego, understood as pure "outside," as ek-stasis, without subject or ego. Only when the ego is placed as subject at the principle of the outside does its interpretation fall into a metaphysics of representation that refers everything appearing in this "outside," now a mere condition of objects, to the ego/subject (pp. 136–137). The proof is ingenious: Heidegger's subjectless ek-stasis shows that representation does not need an ego; therefore the ego's origin must lie elsewhere. The critics of the subject and the metaphysicians of the subject agree that the ego is a pole of representation, and that is their shared error.

Where then does the ego come from, if not from the "outside"? Whence the concept of subject, if not from a metaphysics of representation, which presupposes it as what its own phenomenality does not contain? It is a radical phenomenological reduction — not Husserl's, but the one that brackets intentionality and, more radically, the ek-static phenomenality in which it unfolds, the outside as such — the reduction that keeps, outside the world, to the sphere of life, that delivers the ego. Not by making it visible, for the living ego escapes all seeing and is never visible in a world — whereas Husserl's reduction grasps it only in its unreal parts, as idealities and potentialities, while its concrete living core dissolves, reduced to the ideal limit of a purely conceptual "present." In truth no phenomenological reduction, as a process of thought, can deliver the ego. The radical reduction is possible only because, as a movement of thought, it points to a place other than its own, where the ego comes to itself in its own mode of provenance. Where does the ego come from, if not from a phenomenological reduction? (pp. 137–138). Henry limits his own method: the reduction does not produce or show the ego; it points to where the ego is already given, in life. The jab at the "purely conceptual present" targets Husserl's living present as the ideal limit-point of retention and protention (essay II, pp. 53–54).

The ego comes from its transcendental birth in life: there is no reaching the ego except that by which it reaches itself, and this is the reaching by which absolute life originally reaches itself. In the self-temporalization of its pathic auto-affection, life experiences itself in its essential Ipseity as the singular Self that is the "me" to which the ego owes being an ego. Thus the transcendental ego is born, in an absolute immanence — life's — so that in such Ur-birth there can be no question of world or being-in-the-world. One should not only say that purely spiritual beings, angels or archangels, foreign to the world, are conceivable; one must say "que tel est notre cas" (p. 138). The provocation about angels is deliberate: the transcendental ego is, in its birth, as acosmic as an angel. Henry does not deny embodiment — flesh is itself acosmic (essay III, p. 74) — but denies that the world enters into the ego's constitution.

Since the ego is born in life so that life remains in it as its condition, a phenomenology of birth faces a second task: not to grasp the ego's generation in absolute life's pathic auto-affection, but to travel the inverse path. We living egos are in the life of the ego that is each time ours. How is what gives it to itself, and was before it in order to do so, revealed in it? How does what is born relate to what is not born? How does the living one, in its transcendental Ur-birth, refer to the non-birth that is absolute life's self-engendering? The second task is the one that will bring in temporality: how can a "before" be present?

A paradox appears. On one side, absolute life's self-engendering, in which the singular Self is engendered, precedes it as its presupposition: life engenders itself before it, as an absolute before without which no Self would come to life. On the other, this presupposition of every conceivable transcendental ego is also its condition: the auto-affection of absolute life, constantly accomplished, in whose actual auto-affection alone the ego is constantly engendered. Were this self-accomplishment interrupted for an instant, the ego would be destroyed. The ego's coming in absolute life is thus no event like what we usually call birth. "Nous ne sommes pas nés une fois pour avoir ensuite à mener notre vie propre" (p. 139). We believe so, as living egos, insofar as each ego, put in possession of its powers and exercising them freely, lives itself as a centre of initiative. But it is in possession of its powers and of itself only insofar as absolute life constantly auto-affects itself in it — because there is only one life, one auto-affection, in which the ego is auto-affected as the singular Self it is. "We are not born one day but constantly engendered" in life's absolute self-engendering. That is why birth is not an event but a condition. This condition makes us "les Fils transcendantaux de la Vie absolue," not the sons of men, of the empirical selves that are only objectifications of transcendental selves, all of which presuppose absolute life's self-engendering (pp. 138–139). The doctrine of continuous birth parallels Descartes's continuous creation (*Meditation III*), transposed from being to life. "Sons of Life, not sons of men" draws on John 1:13 ("born not of blood… but of God"), developed at length in *C'est moi la vérité*.

The question then: how to understand, in each ego grasped in its transcendental birth as a Son, the relation of its presupposition to its condition? Its presupposition is absolute life's self-engendering, an absolute before; its condition — as Son — requires this self-engendering to be in it as what engenders it at each instant. How does what is before, in a scarcely thinkable antecedence, remain within the ego as a condition it can neither shed nor cut itself from? Are we not separated from what is before us? Is not every before, for one who comes after, necessarily past, lost? And if this before is life, has not one who comes after it already lost life — is he not already dead (pp. 139–140)? The objection is formulated at its sharpest: a before that is past would leave the born one cut off from life.

Here the brief indications on life's temporality become decisive. Any misinterpretation of the before that precedes our birth — of our birth's relation to what is not born — is ruled out by recalling that absolute life's self-temporalization is essentially non-ek-static. Contemporary phenomenology understands the before precisely as ek-stasis: for something like a "before" to appear, the one to whom it appears must relate retrospectively to it as before him, so that his relation takes the form of a specific ek-stasis, in whose "outside" appear the horizon of the before and, within it, what was "before." By its ek-static nature, this horizon is one of unreality, as is everything appearing in it, which, appearing so, no longer is. The "before" of retention and of Heidegger's "having-been" is an horizon of unreality (essay II, p. 53).

The before of birth escapes this double condition, since life's self-temporalization is radically immanent, deploying no horizon. What is engendered never relates to what engenders it as to an ek-static before. How then does it relate to it so that what engenders it is a before and never ceases to be? Every form of relation (*Logos*) that does not derive from ek-stasis derives from pathos. A feeling relates to itself not ek-statically but in and through its transcendental affectivity. The original temporality here is that of its affectivity and self-experience, which, though constantly changing, does not cease. As the experience unfolds and what is experienced arrives ever anew, the experienced, through its constant change, is constantly the Self itself, as is what experiences it. "Le Soi est un automouvement s'autoéprouvant," constantly self-experiencing in its very movement, so that from this self-experiencing movement nothing ever detaches or slips away. This pathic remaining-in-itself of the Self is its living, stronger than any change and making it possible, its present consubstantial with this living — its "Présent vivant" (pp. 140–141). "Every relation that does not derive from ek-stasis derives from pathos" is a law of Henry's phenomenology: there are two forms of *logos*, the world's and life's. The "living present" is given its non-Husserlian sense, as in essay II.

The living ego holds its living only from absolute life's living, and its self-temporalization only from that life's eternal self-engendering. Because in this self-engendering life, constantly growing from itself in its moving self-experience, never leaves itself, the ego borne by it does not leave itself either, and is possible both as ego and as living. Absolute life's self-temporalization is a "loi phénoménologique matérielle" whose analysis would reveal astonishing properties, two above all. First, unlike the constantly affirmed irreversibility of objective time and, more essentially, of the ek-static temporality whose temporalization is the world, absolute life's temporality is reversible, since it temporalizes itself as suffering and enjoying, and the passage between these fundamental tonalities goes both ways, each remaining in the other as its phenomenological condition and substance. Second, in this pathic self-temporalization nothing unreal ever occurs, only the self-movement of an endless self-experience in the reversibility of suffering and enjoying. Hence the second material law: "dans la vie tout est réel, de même que toute réalité se tient dans la vie" (p. 141). Reversibility is the most surprising claim: the arrow of time is a feature of ek-static time only. In life, joy can return to suffering and suffering to joy without anything passing away. The second law answers the objection of p. 140: nothing in life is past, because nothing in life is unreal.

These two laws let us specify the ego's relation to its absolute before, the life that engenders it at each instant. The ego relates to itself only in absolute life's self-relation, and that is how life engenders it; the ego unfolds the constant self-experience of its living only in life's pathic self-temporalization. Two consequences follow.

First, as to the ego's relation to itself: it is non-ek-static and pathic, so that in it living is "omni-real," intact and whole, and only on that condition possible as living. Non-ek-static means in particular that the living ego never relates to itself in memory or forgetting, but only in the absolute Forgetting that means the principled impossibility of any memory: "l'Immémorial." "La mémoire n'est pas une catégorie de l'ipséité. L'ego vivant n'a ni passé ni avenir ni présent" (p. 142). The "Immemorial" responds to Levinas's "immemorial past" — but for Henry it is not a past that never was present, but a presence that was never past. Memory belongs to representation; the self, in its self-givenness, has nothing to remember about itself. *Généalogie de la psychanalyse* reads the Freudian unconscious as this "Immemorial."

Second, as to the ego's relation to life, the final question: it is inscribed in the ego itself, which relates to itself as this singular Self only in absolute life's self-relation. This "in" is the problem; it is birth. The "in" is supplied by absolute life's self-relation, since life, joining itself, joins the ego to itself and so joins it to life, the ego joining itself only as life joins itself. This self-relation, as relation to life, as life's relation to itself, is both non-ek-static and pathic. "In" life the ego relates to no ek-static before; only pathically does it relate to life's Before. This pathos is thus determined: it is the pathos of what, experiencing itself, experiences absolute life's self-experience, so that its radical passivity toward itself is its radical passivity toward life — life's radical passivity toward itself become, in its ipseization and singularity, the ego's passivity toward itself. This mode of passivity is the most constant feature of every life issuing from a birth, a life like ours. "La condition de l'ego est le pathos de sa présupposition. La vie de l'ego transcendantal est la phénoménologie de sa naissance" (p. 142). The final formula answers the paradox of p. 139: the "before" of life is present in the ego not as memory but as the felt passivity of being given to oneself. The essay closes the line begun in essay I (pp. 36–37), where passivity toward oneself was the essence of ipseity: that passivity is now read as the felt trace of birth. To live is to undergo one's birth, continuously.

## VII. Suffering and Life

Henry will speak of life and the suffering proper to it, but first fixes the historical moment from which the questioning arises. It will no longer be Greek, a bio-logy. A Greek *logos* about *bios* considered animals showing themselves in the world, with sensible properties, because our knowledge of them in the world is sensible knowledge. Heidegger still holds this view when he writes that life "is a particular kind of being, but in principle accessible only in Dasein" (*Sein und Zeit*, p. 50) — in being-in-the-world, in the world. The lecture was written for an international conference on philosophy and psychiatry at the Paris Faculty of Medicine in June 2001, a year before Henry's death. Its audience explains its double structure: a compressed phenomenology of life, then an application to depression and despair. Heidegger's § 10 formula, already dissected in essay VI (p. 127), is now placed in the Greek lineage: life seen from outside.

After Galileo explained that sensible knowledge is a lure — that the things of the universe have no colours, odours, tastes or sounds, but are extended material bodies with shapes and figures, rationally graspable by geometry — the Greek meaning of "biology" disappeared, though the title survives. François Jacob: "On n'interroge plus la vie aujourd'hui dans les laboratoires" (*La logique du vivant*, 1970, p. 320). Laboratories study blind material processes homogeneous with those of physics. Jacob's sentence, a molecular biologist's own, is Henry's favourite witness (*C'est moi la vérité*, *Incarnation*): biology has no object called "life."

Two remarks. First, Galileo's ideas spread at once through learned Europe and determined a new science that placed geometrico-mathematical knowledge of the material universe at the principle of modern societies' organization, giving them a face wholly different from traditional societies. The social consequence is the theme of *La barbarie*.

Second, at the very moment he adheres to Galileo's decision to reduce the human world to the world of science, Descartes performs what must be called a "contre-réduction." Instead of simply excluding sensible qualities — sensibility, passions, emotions, will, subjectivity in all its modes — from knowledge, he gathers them up: they have no place in things, but they constitute the substance of our being. Things feel nothing and do not feel themselves; what is proper to our sensations, affections, passions and desires is that they experience themselves. Descartes named them *cogitationes* — a strange, ill-chosen name that would cause total misunderstanding of the cogito, among the great Cartesians and down to today, where it has produced the many critiques and "crises of the subject" that still lead contemporary culture astray (pp. 143–144). The term "counter-reduction," used in essay V (p. 118) for Henry's own method, is here attributed to Descartes: what Galileo removed from the world, Descartes reassigns to the soul. "Ill-chosen" because *cogitatio* suggests thinking-as-representing, which led to reading the cogito as a self-representation.

What experiences itself in the manner of an impression is life, transcendental phenomenological life, the only life that exists, which inhabits each modality of our existence, from the humblest pain. The sentence fixes the essay's object: suffering as a mode of life's self-impression.

To clarify radically the question of pain or suffering in its relation to life, Henry poses it phenomenologically. Phenomenology asks not about things but about how they show themselves, the "How" of their manifestation and revelation. There are two fundamental modes in which everything given to us is manifested: the appearing of the world and that of life. The duality, argued at length in essays III–V, is here simply stated for a non-specialist audience.

The world's appearing is a medium of pure exteriority, the "outside itself" (*das Außersich*), which the second division of *Sein und Zeit* shows to be hollowed out in the joint opening of the temporal ek-stases of future, present and past that together shape the horizon of visibilization. Since the world's appearing is the outside-itself in which everything we see and can see shows itself, a Difference is instituted in principle between what appears in the world and the world's appearing itself — an appearing arising from and lighting up in this Difference. The condensation repeats essay III (pp. 62–63).

Life's revelation is opposed trait for trait. The world's appearing unveils in the outside-itself, so that everything it unveils is exterior, other, different; life's revelation, bearing no gap and never differing from itself, reveals only itself. Life reveals itself; it is self-revelation. This means, first, that life is not a blind or unconscious process — it performs the original work of revelation; second, that what is revealed in this work is life itself. The opposition between what appears and pure appearing disappears in the case of life. One never comes to life from a power of revelation other than it: "On n'accède à la vie qu'en elle, à partir d'elle. C'est la vie qui vient en soi" (p. 145). "Not a blind or unconscious process" is addressed to psychiatrists: the vital is not the biological or the Freudian unconscious drive but self-revealing.

That life never needs a power of knowledge other than itself to know itself shows in every modality of life, in the most primitive impression. Take a pain. Ordinarily it is referred to a part of the objective body, as if it had its seat there. To set aside this "false judgement," as Descartes calls it, perform the phenomenological reduction that retains only its painful character, the "painful as such," the purely affective element of suffering. This "pure suffering" reveals itself: only suffering lets us know what suffering is, and what is revealed is suffering. The world's outside-itself is absent from this mode of life, since no gap separates suffering from itself; driven back against itself, crushed under its own weight, it cannot establish any retreat, any line of escape from itself and its oppressiveness. With no inner gap, directing a gaze at it is excluded. "Personne n'a jamais vu sa douleur ou sa souffrance. La souffrance est invisible, comme la vie" (p. 146). The paragraph repeats essay III (p. 65) almost verbatim, with one addition: the localization of pain is Descartes's "false judgement" (*Principles* I, § 67; the amputee's pain in *Meditation VI*), which Henry reads as a confusion of pain with its representation in the body.

Two difficulties arise. First: suffering reveals suffering, and that is why we who suffer know what it is. But our concrete existence consists also of pleasures, desires, great happiness, and these too instruct us about themselves — not words or concepts, which, however inadequate, presuppose this original phenomenological givenness of feeling, without which no representation, theorization or therapy of affective existence would be conceivable. So the self-revelation of suffering cannot be the work of suffering in its particular content, if the same original self-givenness is at work in joy, anguish or effort. But if self-givenness is found in every feeling independently of its particularity, does it not stand outside it again, foreign to its specific impressional content — the painfulness of pain, for instance? The first difficulty is sharp: if what reveals is common to all affects, it seems to become a neutral form indifferent to their content — exactly the structure of world-appearing. The answer will be that the common element is not neutral but is itself affective (suffering and enjoying). The aside on therapy prepares the conclusion: every therapy presupposes the affect's self-givenness.

The second difficulty comes from the plurality of tonalities that make up our existence. Since they are diverse, often opposed or contradictory, must their succession, which composes our history, not seem incoherent, even absurd? And if their principle does not lie in a common essence, in self-revelation, must we not seek their "cause" or "motive," as common sense does, in worldly phenomena? "A misfortune has happened": an objective event — accident, professional or emotional failure, illness, bereavement — has produced suffering, to the point of being identified with it. But such an event, however dramatic, can produce suffering only in a being transcendentally constituted so as to be capable of feelings, capable of suffering — and the question remains why the feeling takes this tonality rather than another. Essay III's argument (pp. 70–71) is restated with a list ("professional or emotional failure") pitched to clinicians.

All modalities of our life are distributed by a decisive dichotomy between positive modalities (pleasure, happiness) and negative ones (pain, sadness), so that our whole existence seems caught in an affective becoming that is anything but undetermined, oscillating between malaise and satisfaction, suffering and joy — tonalities like envy or indifference being a kind of neutralization of this primitive oscillation. (The printed "envie" is likely a slip for "ennui," as in essay III, p. 71.) How to account for this dichotomy if, obeying a hidden rule and not resulting from the world's chances alone, it must find its ultimate condition in ourselves, in our life? The "hidden rule" is what the essay will seek in the structure of self-experience.

The phenomenology of life must therefore be questioned further. Life comes into itself; only in life can one come into it, in the original and absolute process by which it comes into itself, "s'écrase contre soi," and experiences itself in the self-experience that constitutes its essence. No experience occurs as self-experience unless life generates in itself the Ipseity in which it experiences and enjoys itself. Since we speak not of the concept of Life but of a real, phenomenologically actual life, the Ipseity is not a concept either but a real Self, phenomenologically actual, in which life, actually experiencing itself, reveals itself to itself, makes itself life. No Life without a living one, without the living Self every life carries insofar as it experiences itself; but no Self without the Life in which every Self is given to itself and outside of which none is possible (pp. 147–148). The text repeats essay III (p. 67) without the Johannine vocabulary — the Word is not named before a medical audience, though the structure is the same.

That Life's coming into itself generates the Ipseity of an actual Self as the immanent condition of its accomplishment is an original fact whose importance will appear. But phenomenology demands more: that the phenomenality of this experience be recognized in itself, if it differs fundamentally from the world's exteriority, where everything is given as "object" or "facing." The pure phenomenological materiality of the self-experience proper to all life and every living Self is an original Affectivity — henceforth a "pathos." As foreign to the world and its outside-itself, acosmic and non-ek-static, Life has been called invisible. The invisible is not the antithetical concept of phenomenality, opening onto some back-world peopled with dreams and ramblings, as our "pauvre monde livré au règne sans partage de l'objectivisme et du positivisme" may think. Leading back to the original essence of Revelation, the invisible points to the weightiest, most incontestable reality: the pathos of our life that makes it and us what we are (p. 148). The guard against misreading the invisible as fantasy (essay III, p. 65) is repeated for psychiatrists, for whom "invisible" might suggest delusion.

As the phenomenological reality of life's self-experience, Affectivity is not undetermined. First, every self-experience necessarily carries the Self in which it experiences itself, so that every conceivable tonality is irreducibly singular, this one or that, belonging to this Self or that. But further: in its particular affective content, this originally ipseized tonality receives a properly affective determination from Affectivity as the phenomenological reality of life's self-experience. The answer to the first difficulty (p. 146) begins: the common element is not a neutral form but determines each tonality affectively.

In its pathic immediacy, self-experience is a "se subir soi-même," a "se supporter soi-même," a "se souffrir soi-même" — a pure "suffering." This suffering marks with insurmountable passivity everything that holds its revelation from pathos: every feeling, every form of life. "Toute vie est une passion" (p. 149). Every tonality experiences itself in this radical passivity toward itself, irremediably delivered over to itself, burdened forever with its own weight — an experience of fundamental impotence, the impossibility of changing oneself or acting on the feeling, since in the very way it is given to itself reigns a radical passivity "plus forte que toute liberté." Thus Affectivity leaves the indeterminacy that would make it a mere concept: in the primitive suffering in which life's self-experience is accomplished, affectivity receives the feature of an original suffering. This suffering does not come from an external event striking us like an aggression from outside; it is no hetero-affection by an intraworldly being or the world. It arises from life's innermost possibility, belonging to the process in which life comes into itself, since every self-experience is phenomenologically accomplished in this primitive suffering as "self-suffering." Suffering is an original phenomenological tonality of life, from which alone every particular suffering is possible. All the sufferings that arise in our existence, after identifiable events or unpredictably, even incomprehensibly — the "experiences of suffering" psychology treats as contingent facts — are, in their hidden origin, nothing of the kind: they are the many declensions of this primitive suffering, referring to it as an a priori buried in life's invisible. That they are possible only from it means they occur only in it, are given to themselves only in it. That is why, in every pain, the pure element of suffering showed the radical passivity of pathos, the impossibility of breaking the unbreakable bond that ties every tonality to itself, a bond constitutive of both its affectivity and its Ipseity (pp. 149–150). The thesis that psychological suffering is a "declension" of transcendental suffering has a direct clinical implication: suffering is not an anomaly to be explained by external causes but a mode of a structure belonging to every life. "Every life is a passion" plays on the double sense of *passion* (affect and undergoing, *pati*).

What is realized in the radical passivity of suffering — in the impossibility of suffering escaping itself — is its very coming into itself, its being-given-to-itself, being-seized-by-itself, being riveted to itself in perfect adherence, in a self-experience that is self-attainment, the upsurge of feeling in itself in self-enjoyment and joy. Here the second fundamental phenomenological tonality of life appears. If life comes into itself in suffering, in suffering too it enjoys itself, experiencing itself. The feeling's impotence to rid itself of itself is identically its power, the building gathering in which life is united to itself and embraces itself, its irruption into itself, "la fulguration de la présence originelle dans la Parousie pathétique de la vie" (p. 150). Joy is not a consequence of the Parousia, coming after the coming of what comes, like a wonder before being, the Platonic *thaumazein*. So the enjoying at issue, of which every "positive" tonality is a modalization, has nothing to do with a stance toward what would already be there, an attitude toward something, a thinker's or philosopher's enthusiasm. Enjoying co-belongs to the transcendental possibility of the self-experience constitutive of living; it is a founding phenomenological tonality, like suffering, from which it results and with which it is identical. Suffering and enjoying, suffering and joy in their original possibility, together and indistinctly, compose and designate the mode of phenomenalization of life in its own phenomenality, the actuality of its Parousia. The contrast with Platonic wonder (*Theaetetus* 155d) and with Heidegger's *Erstaunen* (discussed in essay IV, p. 98) is pointed: philosophical joy is a response to being; life's joy is not a response but the self-givenness itself.

The unity of suffering and enjoying in life's self-coming is intelligible. Suffering is enjoying because in and through suffering life's being-given-to-itself, its self-enjoyment, is accomplished; the phenomenological effectuation of this suffering is this enjoyment. Likewise enjoying implies suffering, since the enjoyment of enjoying is realized only in the self-experience of suffering. Thus in the phenomenological affectivity of every conceivable living one recognizes this affinity, or rather "contemporanéité originaire," of suffering and enjoying (pp. 150–151). "Contemporaneity" is a deliberate choice: the two are not successive phases but the same event under two tonalities.

This contemporaneity explains the dichotomy of affectivity, its enigmatic division between negative and positive tonalities. The negative ones, with their many nuances — displeasure, unpleasantness, malaise, painfulness, up to acute pain, unbearable suffering and finally despair — find their phenomenological possibility in the primitive suffering that co-belongs to life's experience; the positive ones — agreeableness, satisfaction, fulfilment, pleasure, joy, happiness, finally beatitude — are rooted in the primitive enjoying in which life experiences itself in the fullness of its presence, and are the various ways this self-coming is accomplished. The two scales end in despair and beatitude, the poles of the Kierkegaardian dialectic that concludes the essay.

Yet the diversity of tonalities raises a phenomenological difficulty. Take a pair, pain and pleasure, as they are experienced: do they not appear not only different but opposed? How can two contrary tonalities like suffering and joy come from the same origin, life's single phenomenological essence? The objection is given its phenomenological form: lived opposition seems to refute common origin.

The opposition, one will add, is so decisive that it motivates human action, which consists in fleeing pain and seeking pleasure. Such effort works spontaneously at the immediate level of desire, whose tension aims at transforming the malaise of need into satisfaction, replacing a negative tonality with a positive one. This is the classic hedonist schema, from Epicurus to Freud's pleasure principle.

But here the objection refutes itself. As long as each tonality is regarded as a separate, autonomous entity, it is very hard to understand how it can change from and by itself into its opposite, how suffering can lead to happiness. Yet what defines an existence like ours — as the humblest desire and all human action show — is not an addition of discrete tonalities each locked in itself, but their incessant passage into one another, and it is this passage that must be explained. If we say happiness is fragile, it is because it bears within it the a priori, transcendental possibility of turning, from and in a sense by itself, into the contrary tonality of unhappiness. When such a transformation — a depression, for instance — occurs in someone's existence, it is normal to seek its "causes" or "motivations" in certain traumas and to try to act on them. Yet the radical modification that has occurred was inscribed in that existence as a possibility in principle. In what does it consist? How can suffering change into joy, or happiness into an indelible sadness (pp. 151–152)? The hedonist objection presupposes what it cannot explain: the passage from need to satisfaction. Henry then introduces depression: it is not only caused by trauma; it actualizes a possibility inherent in every life. This is not a denial of aetiology but a claim about its conditions.

Suffering can turn into joy because that from which every form of suffering is possible — the primitive suffering in which life undergoes and bears itself in radical passivity — is the very thing in which it takes hold of itself, experiences and enjoys itself. Likewise every form of happiness can turn into suffering because life's self-enjoyment is never separate from the primitive suffering in which it comes into itself, which potentially opens onto all forms of suffering. "C'est dans ce jeu primitif du souffrir et du jouir que prend naissance ce qu'on appelle une dépression, c'est là aussi qu'elle pourra être surmontée" (p. 152). This original connection must be deepened to grasp the enigmatic laws of phenomenological life that hold in their hands the coming of happiness and unhappiness. To do so, Henry will consider depression in its extreme form, despair, presupposing that "au fond d'elle-même, toute dépression est désespoir," and will legitimate this by Kierkegaard's analysis in *The Sickness unto Death*. The structural point — that the site where depression arises is also the site of its overcoming — is the essay's clinical thesis. The identification of depression with despair is a phenomenological claim (depression is at bottom a relation of the self to itself), not a diagnostic one.

Two results of the inquiry, one explicit, one implicit, are recalled. The first, the theory of the generation of the Ipseity of a real Self as the immanent condition of every life's self-experience, grounds not only the triviality that every depression is someone's — no anonymous, impersonal, material, blind, hence unconscious process — a triviality relative, besides, to an age of materialist objectivism and of the "crises of the subject" or of "identity" celebrated by philosophy, media ideology, films and even women's weeklies. Despair is indeed a crisis of identity, but not in the sense of a disappearance or absence of identity: in despair, in depression, "le moi est là plus que jamais" (p. 153). Because despair has its site in life, it is always despair of the self, and despair of the self about itself. Kierkegaard: one does not despair of not having become Caesar but of the self that has not become Caesar. Analysis is thus referred from the world, whence everything seems to come, to the Self, where everything happens. It is always the Self one is that one despairs of — which plunges despair, and so every depression, into its torment. The satire of the "identity crisis" is pointed: the depressed person suffers not from lacking a self but from having one he cannot be rid of. Kierkegaard's Caesar example (*The Sickness unto Death*, Part One) shows that despair over a worldly object is despair over oneself.

The second result, the original connection of suffering and enjoying, consubstantial with every life and Self, clarifies this torment. The Self is nothing but a relation to itself, and a relation to oneself is impossible in exteriority. What relates the Self to itself, making it a Self, is life's pathic immediacy, this original connection of suffering and enjoying. What then does despairing of oneself mean? The Self is given to itself in the radical passivity of "self-suffering" that constitutes its essence. From the impossibility of overcoming this passivity, of escaping the weight of its own being — "the burden of existence" — arises its suffering, identical with the experience of this burden. In this suffering and in the structure of its suffering arises a will: to escape its suffering, to be rid of itself. To despair of oneself means "vouloir se débarrasser de son moi, ne pas vouloir être soi-même" (pp. 153–154, Kierkegaard). But to be rid of oneself, to break the bond attaching it to itself, is what the self cannot do. To separate from oneself would mean self-destruction, and that is impossible. So it is superficial to say the despairing person destroys his self, since, as Kierkegaard notes, "c'est ce dont à son grand désespoir [...] il est incapable." Kierkegaard's definition of the self ("a relation that relates itself to itself") is given its phenomenological content: the relation is pathos, not reflection. "Burden of existence" recalls Heidegger's *Last* in the analysis of mood (§ 29), here grounded not in thrownness into the world but in self-givenness.

The self's inability to be rid of itself is what, in despair, gives it to itself, makes it an experience. Despair is an experience because its effort to separate from itself remains within it in principle, occurring in the sphere of radical immanence where life embraces itself without ever detaching from itself. The will to be rid of oneself proper to despair thus collides within it with the inner cohesion of its being, the unbreakable essence of life. Such is "l'atroce contradiction du désespoir." Despair is "la maladie mortelle." Wishing to be rid of himself, the despairing one wants to die, but this will is contradictory and fails: to want to die without dying, to die one's death so that it is rather "living one's death," or, in Kierkegaard's words, "eternally dying" — that is the insurmountable contradiction of despair present at the bottom of every depression, unthought perhaps but lived in the inexorable givenness of pathos. This contradiction inhabiting the limit-tonalities of our existence brings the self's suffering to its paroxysm, setting fire in it to its very essence, "à quelque chose d'indestructible et qui brûle éternellement" (p. 154). The analysis reaches its sharpest point: despair is the attempt of a self to escape a self-givenness it does not control. Kierkegaard's "sickness unto death" — a sickness in which one cannot die — is read as the experience of life's indestructibility from within. The image of fire echoes Kierkegaard's "burning" of despair and the mystics' fire that does not consume.

Because, at the height of its suffering, despair is referred back to the primitive suffering in which every life comes into itself, life is still in it, more than ever. The greater the distress in which the self, despairing and wanting to be rid of itself, measures its impotence to destroy itself, the more violent and strong its experience of the invincible power that throws it into itself despite its will and independently of its power. But this power is life's; through its suffering, life's irruption into itself is accomplished. "Au fond du désespoir il y a l'absolu, l'autorévélation de la vie, le rassemblement et la profusion de sa Parousie" (p. 155). That is why no tonality remains blocked in itself: given to itself only in life's coming into itself, it inevitably enters history — not the world's history and events, but the "historial" in us of absolute Life, the incessant passage of suffering into enjoying from which our action originates and on which our life is built. The phrase *l'historial* borrows Heidegger's term (*das Geschichtliche* as distinct from the merely historical) and reassigns it: our true history is the movement of affect within life. The clinical point: no affect is a fixed state.

Because despair makes us feel the invincibility of living in us, it also opens us to it, and, by a paradoxical dialectic, tips over into beatitude. Hence Kierkegaard can say that "le désespoir est la maladie que le pire des malheurs est de n'avoir pas eue" (references to the Ferlov–Gateau translation, pp. 73, 75, 83). Because of this paradoxical dialectic hidden in life's arcana, in the presence of the gravest illnesses — those affecting not only bodies but life itself — a therapy is always possible, one that, instinctively or deliberately, will seek support in the tireless movement by which life constantly gives itself to itself in giving us to ourselves. Because, in Eckhart's unheard-of words, life, which he also calls God, "s'engendre comme moi-même," every affective self-enclosure, caught up in this endless generation, can always be broken. The therapeutic conclusion is careful: therapy is "always possible," not guaranteed. Its ground is that the depressed self is still being given to itself by a life it does not produce; the same movement that holds it in suffering can carry it to joy. Eckhart's formula, already cited in essay III (p. 69), here grounds a clinical hope.

It is said that medicine is not a science — that is, not only a science. On a ground of ever-new knowledge allowing continuous progress, something else is at work in whatever involves a concrete intersubjective relation, never reducible to objective knowledge, however elaborate. "Le regard médical est un regard transcendantal" (p. 155): through a scientific datum it aims at a phenomenological life that is always singular. Even in so-called somatic medicine — which never quite is — examining an X-ray somehow passes through the image to take in a suffering that must be removed or made bearable. All the more in psychiatry, the life of individuals is always the true theme of research, theory and therapy. Interdisciplinarity is not a conventional term or pious wish here but the daily work and bread of psychiatrists and philosophers united by one goal: to restore a sick life — sick perhaps with the sickness unto death — to its power and to the happiness of living, to the life of which Kafka says "qu'elle est là, point hostile" (p. 156). The closing redefinition of the medical gaze as "transcendental" reverses Foucault's "clinical gaze" (*Naissance de la clinique*), which constitutes the patient as an object: for Henry the physician's gaze, through the object, aims at an invisible singular life. Kafka's words ("it is there, not hostile") come from the *Diaries* and close the essay on a note of trust in life.

## VIII. The Problem of Touch

The problem of touch refers to that of the other senses by a double relation of similarity and differentiation: each sense is specific, yet all appear as functions of one body. Each sense's status therefore depends on that of this body — the own body of each living individual. Since the question of touch leads back to that of the body, the body must first be examined philosophically and generally, by way of introduction to the debates. The text opened a 1992 colloquium on touch at the Montpellier Faculty of Medicine. Its brevity and audience make it a summary of the Biranian argument of essay I, restated in the late vocabulary of life and pathos, and a dry run for the analyses of *Incarnation* (2000).

The first philosopher to consult is Descartes, to whom is attributed the dualism of soul and body that still governs how we usually think of our relation to the body — in philosophy, psychology, medicine, psychoanalysis and common sense. On this schema man is double: soul or mind on one side, body as material thing on the other, the difficulty being to understand how an unextended spiritual "thing" can act on a material body or vice versa. Henry states the received Descartes in order to set against it the other Descartes he always claims.

Yet Descartes stated another, astonishing proposition about the body, capable of calling this dualism into question. To Gassendi, who objected: you say "I think, therefore I am," why not "I walk, therefore I am"?, Descartes replied, quite unexpectedly, that one can perfectly well say "I walk, therefore I am," provided one means the immediate experience of walking, walking reduced to what it really is for the one who walks, the pure subjective experience of the act. So reduced, walking is nothing but a *cogitatio*, and as such grounds what I am, the *sum*, as much as "I think" does, being only a mode of "I think" in Descartes's sense — what experiences itself immediately (Descartes: the inference "I walk, therefore I am" is not good "except insofar as the inner knowledge I have of it is a thought"). With this idea of walking, a bodily experience, as *cogitatio*, Descartes rose in a lightning intuition to the idea of a subjective body that overturns the traditional conception of the body as object. Unfortunately he did not develop this new view, holding instead to the body-thing, extended thing (*res extensa*) — a conception taken from antiquity, reintroduced shortly before by Galileo as the foundation of his physics and of modern science generally. If body is extended thing with figures, it is knowable by geometry, and once geometry received mathematical formulation with Descartes, the physico-mathematical approach to material nature, modern science, was founded (p. 158). The Gassendi exchange (Fifth Objections and Replies) is Henry's central Cartesian text on the body, as *Passions* § 26 is for affectivity (essay V, p. 118). Walking "reduced to its pure subjective experience" is precisely the Biranian "I can" in Cartesian vocabulary.

Yet our body as we live it — that walks, feels, desires, works in pain or joy — has nothing to do with the body of physics and its particles, nor with the biological organism of molecules, acid chains, neurons. That is why the decisive intuition of a subjective body glimpsed for an instant by Descartes was taken up by another French philosopher of genius, Maine de Biran, who in the early nineteenth century developed an elaborate theory of the subjective body. Meditating on the cogito — abandoned by the great post-Cartesian metaphysicians, Spinoza, Malebranche and Leibniz — Biran made his extraordinary discovery of the body as pure subjectivity. For him the cogito is not an "I think" in our sense — I think something, I imagine something — but an "I can." I am a kind of primordial force, a power deployed in each of my actions: motor actions like grasping, rising, walking, or the action inhabiting each sense, by which I move my eyes to direct my gaze as I wish. It is precisely in touch that this force at work in each sense can be recognized in its essentials (pp. 158–159). The line from Descartes to Biran, bypassing the great rationalists, is Henry's genealogy of the subjective body (*Philosophie et phénoménologie du corps*). The emphasis on the motor component of each sense (eye movements) anticipates the claim that touch is the paradigm.

Recall Biran's admirable critique of Condillac's theory of the knowledge of the own body. For the great eighteenth-century sensualist, our body resolves into a set of subjective sensations; what invites us to cross this purely subjective, impressional layer to experience, behind it, the real being of our body is touch. Concretely, the movement of the hand over the own body apprehends it as something that resists its pressure, so that the hand moving continuously over the body brings forth its reality, structures and forms, as it will for external bodies. The exposition repeats essay I (p. 29).

This very interesting account of the genesis of our knowledge of our own body implies a dissociation between two bodies: a known body and a knowing body. The known body is the real being whose contours and configurations are traced and recognized by the hand as it moves, the body touched and grasped by it. The knowing body is the hand itself as it draws, in its successive movements, this body that is the object of its touch — touch itself raised, as an act, to the rank of a fundamental power of knowledge, of our own body first, then of external bodies. The distinction of known and knowing body is the core of essay I's argument against Condillac (p. 29).

If we ask about the relation between knowing and known body — a relation internal to touch itself, holding between the touching touch and the object touched, which is first my own body as object of touch — and ask which is more fundamental, we are led to the first, the touching touch without which nothing touched would ever exist for us. The body-object thus refers, by an unavoidable subordination, to the body-subject that constantly grounds it. The priority of the touching over the touched is the transcendental priority of the condition over what it conditions.

The discovery of a subject whose essence is touch itself as touching — and, more generally, the subjective body and the power inhabiting each of its senses, touch especially — is an essential discovery. This bodily power is a subject wholly different from the one classical philosophy placed at the origin of experience: consciousness, in which the understanding, thought reduced to it, appeared as the mind's fundamental capacity, while the world, correlate of this rational subject, was interpreted as intelligible, structured by rational regulations to which sensible phenomena were subject. A more or less strongly affirmed hierarchy between intelligible and sensible ran through Western thought from Greece to the present. Henry gives the "body-subject" its full credit before showing its limit.

Placing our own body, now the true subject, at the principle of our experience of the world not only transforms the classical conception of the knowing subject; the world itself, dependent on this subject's nature for its structures, is transformed too. It becomes the world-of-the-body, the life-world, Husserl's *Lebenswelt*: first a sensible world, while the conceptual regulations it is subject to, scientific idealities especially, necessarily refer to the sensible layer of this passively pre-given world without which they would strictly have no meaning. An incarnate subject relating to a sensible world: this is the concrete situation that phenomenology, Merleau-Ponty's especially, constantly opposes to the classical description of an intellectual consciousness dominating a world of scientifically known objects (pp. 160–161). Henry accepts the *Crisis*'s founding of scientific idealities on the life-world and Merleau-Ponty's primacy of perception. The hyphenated "world-of-life" will receive in essay X a meaning Husserl did not give it: a world of life, not of perception.

These pertinent analyses, in which the world is inseparable from a carnal subject, leave open a gaping question that only Biran had the genius to see. Return to the critique of Condillac and the two bodies united by touch, to the hand moving over our body to recognize its forms: an immense difficulty arises. The question is no longer how the body-subject knows the body-object by touch, but how the touching touch — the hand's power of moving and grasping — is originally in possession of itself so as to be able to move, grasp and touch. The decisive problem is not how the body-subject relates to the body-object, but how this touching body relates to itself and experiences itself as touching — this experience being a very particular knowledge, not a knowledge at all but a power. How is the subjective power of grasping in possession of itself so as to do all it does? The question is essay I's (pp. 27–28). The specification that self-possession is "not a knowledge but a power" refines the earlier formulation: the body's self-relation is not cognitive but practical.

Biran's genius was to understand that the type of experience in which touch relates to itself as touching has nothing to do with that by which touching touch relates to whatever it touches. The thesis is the hinge of the essay and of Henry's entire phenomenology of the body: two relations, heterogeneous in kind.

Touching touch relates to what it touches by an intentionality: it surpasses itself toward what it touches and reaches it as what resists it, so that what resists stands outside the force that breaks against it. In the touching touch's relation to itself, by contrast — the relation that lets it possess itself and act — there is no intentional relation, no relation of exteriority. Touching touch experiences itself immediately; its self-relation is "un pathos sans distance, une affectivité pure" analogous to that by which each feeling relates to itself, each fear or anguish experiencing itself immediately in its affectivity and through it alone. The body-subject thus relates intentionally, by an external aim, to what it touches, while in its internal relation to itself there is no intentionality but only "ce pathos écrasé contre soi, immergé en soi-même," in which touching touch is one with itself — and only on this condition does it possess itself, able to act, move and touch (pp. 161–162). Biran's resistance (the *continu résistant*) is the paradigm of intentional touch; the effort that meets it is given to itself non-intentionally. The analogy with fear and anguish brings the body under the general theory of affectivity: effort is a feeling (the "feeling of effort," p. 163).

The touching touch's relation to itself shows what our body's relation to itself is in general — not to what it knows through its motility or senses, but to itself: what Henry calls "la corporéité originelle." Original corporeity designates the way the body dwells in itself, coinciding with itself and each of its powers, so as to be able to exercise them. Touch knows what it touches by touching it, in an act that runs up against it as something external — a form of intentionality. "Mais le toucher touchant ne touche pas lui-même. Ce n'est pas par le toucher que nous connaissons le toucher" (p. 162). How then? In the mute experience in which it experiences itself immediately, whose phenomenological matter is a pure affectivity. What experiences itself immediately, without distance, in a self-feeling whose stuff is this pure affectivity, "une chair affective," is life — not the biologists' life, whose elements belong to the world and are known objectively, scientifically. The life of the living body, ours, with which we coincide, is phenomenological life, absolute phenomenological life, whose whole essence is self-experience. This life inhabits touch, the touching touch, and makes it what it is: a power we possess and exercise in the "feeling of effort" Biran speaks of — the very mode in which our body is given to itself, our original corporeity (pp. 162–163). "Touching touch does not touch itself" is the volume's most direct rejection of Merleau-Ponty's reversibility (the hand touching the other hand), and the core of the critique in *Incarnation*: what Merleau-Ponty describes as touching oneself is a touch of one's body as object, which presupposes the non-tactile self-givenness of the touching. "Affective flesh" names the result.

Touching touch should not be interpreted as a singular act or a series of particular successive acts. Each act of touching or grasping is only the actualization of a force, a pre-existing capacity, and the problem was how this capacity is in our possession as something we can use at any moment. Because it is in our possession, we can exercise it. The problem of touch is thus that of this permanent capacity, this "being-constantly-in-possession" that resides in and defines original corporeity. This capacity dominates time. There is an "Archi-Présence" of the body to itself, an original presence by which my body is always already there as what I coincide with and can therefore use at any instant. This immersion of each in his own body depends on life's immersion in itself — a life that, for the living one, is never past or future or even present in the way an object is present to us, but escapes past, future and every objective present, because it is always there, embracing itself in a pathic experience no force can break (p. 163). The shift from acts to capacities repeats essay II's analysis of the hand's past movements (pp. 54–55): powers do not pass. "Arch-presence" names the non-temporal presence of the living present (essay VI, p. 142: "the living ego has neither past nor future nor present").

Because the original subjective body is constantly present to itself in life's arch-presence to itself, the world it opens to us by each of its powers, touch especially, also exceeds the actuality of my perception at any moment. It is a world given to me potentially, because I can deploy the powers that give me access to it, because the paths to the world are traced out in me in advance and I can travel them when I wish. I can touch things at any moment; this power is one with me, so that the totality of things composing the world is accessible to me in principle insofar as I carry this power and coincide with it (pp. 163–164). This is Henry's version of Husserl's "I can" (*Ich kann*) as the source of the world's horizons (*Ideas II*, *Cartesian Meditations*): the world's potentiality is grounded in the self-possession of bodily powers. The world's horizonal structure — always more than is given — is thus traced back to life, not to ek-stasis.

Reflection on touch thus leads to a chain of problems of the highest interest and difficulty, once we stop naively treating touch as an objective behaviour taking place among the phenomena of the world. Grasped in its subjectivity, as an inner possibility of our original, subjective body, touch refers to a constellation of fundamental phenomena that concern us at the deepest level — in our relation to ourselves, to the world and to others. The colloquium's task is to elucidate this vast field. The final mention of "others" points to essay XI and to *Incarnation*'s third part, where the erotic relation is analysed as an attempt to reach the other's life through touch — an attempt bound to fail because touch reaches only the body, not the other's self-feeling flesh.

## IX. Incarnation

"Incarnation" designates first the condition of a being that has a body or, as the word says more precisely, a flesh. Are body and flesh the same? Like every fundamental question, this one refers to a phenomenological foundation from which alone it can be elucidated: the pure appearing presupposed in all that appears to us, which must appear first if anything is to appear in turn. Phenomenology is not the "science of phenomena" but of their essence, of what lets a phenomenon be a phenomenon — a science of pure phenomenality as such. Other words can express this theme: monstration, unveiling, pure manifestation, pure revelation, or, in its absolutely original sense, truth. These key words of phenomenology are in many respects those of religion and so of theology. The text was unpublished during Henry's life and summarizes the argument of *Incarnation* (2000). The remark about religious vocabulary announces its strategy: the phenomenological concepts of revelation and truth will be shown to coincide with the Johannine ones.

There are two fundamental modes of appearing, two decisive ways in which phenomenality phenomenalizes itself: the appearing of the world and that of life. The duality of essays III–VII is again stated as the premise.

In the world everything shows itself outside us, as exterior, other, different. These properties do not belong to the thing itself; it presents itself so only because it shows itself in the world. Because the world, understood in its pure appearing, consists in a primordial exteriority, the "outside-itself" as such, everything showing itself in it is already thrown outside, given before us outside us as "object" or "facing." Among such things is the body, and therefore our own body. "Quelque chose comme un corps n'est possible que dans un « monde » : tout corps est un « corps extérieur »" (p. 166). The world, considered not naively as the sum of things or bodies but as the mode of their appearing, lights up in the opening of the horizon of pure exteriority Heidegger also calls "Ek-stasis," so that the coming-outside of this Outside "pro-duces" the space of light in which all we can see becomes visible, whether in sensible or intelligible vision. The thesis that every body is an "external body" is the starting point of the distinction between body (*corps*) and flesh (*chair*): "body" names what can appear only in exteriority. The hyphenated "pro-duces" exploits *pro-ducere*, to lead forth into the open.

In life, the difference between appearing and what it makes appear, between pure phenomenality and the phenomenon, does not exist. This unusual identification requires that life be understood in its proper sense — not as a "thing," a set of inert material processes as in modern biology (which, after the Galilean reduction eliminated sensibility, subjectivity or consciousness, no longer speaks of life at all: Jacob again, and "only the anachronistic retention of the Greek title of a science that has wholly changed its object permits the confusion"), but as phenomenological through and through, pure phenomenality and, further, the most original mode in which phenomenality phenomenalizes itself, making every other form possible. Though it founds the world's mode, life's mode differs fundamentally from it; to avoid equivocation Henry calls it "revelation" (pp. 166–167). The terminological decision is fixed here: "appearing" (*apparaître*) for the world, "revelation" for life. The claim that life founds world-appearing (essay IV, p. 92) is restated.

Life's revelation is opposed point by point to the world's appearing. The latter unveils in the outside-itself, so that what it unveils is exterior; the decisive feature of life's revelation, which bears no gap and never differs from itself, is that it reveals only itself. "La vie se révèle. La vie est une autorévélation" (p. 167). On the one hand, life performs the work of revelation; it is anything but blind. On the other, what it reveals is itself. Revelation and revealed are one. The formulation repeats essay VII (p. 145) word for word.

This extraordinary situation is found wherever there is life, in its simplest mode, the impression. Take a pain, ordinarily taken as "physical pain" referred to a part of the objective body (headache, backache, stomach-ache). Reduce it to its pure impressional character, the "painful as such," the purely affective element of suffering. This pure suffering "reveals itself": suffering alone lets us know what suffering is, and what it reveals is itself. That the world's outside-itself is absent from this self-revelation is shown by the absence of any gap between suffering and itself: driven back against itself, crushed by its own weight, it cannot put any distance between itself and itself, any line of escape from itself and its oppressiveness. Without distance, no gaze can be directed at it. "Personne n'a jamais vu sa souffrance, son plaisir ou sa joie. La douleur, mais cela vaut de toute impression, est invisible" (p. 167). The phenomenological reduction of pain, presented in essays III and VII, is generalized to every impression, preparing the definition of flesh as a totality of impressions.

The invisible is thus nothing negative, nothing to be thought from the world's visibility in a purely privative way, if it is wholly foreign to it and owes it nothing — if it designates the primitive and positive mode in which an impression experiences itself in insurmountable passivity toward itself, and so as it is, in the impressional immediacy constitutive of its reality. Yet no particular impression reveals itself of itself, by its own force. Only in life's self-revelation, accomplished in its absolutely original immanence, is any impression placed in itself, impressing itself so as to be what it is. That is why this self-revelation, in which each impression passively experiences itself, belongs to no particular impression but concerns all — all our impressions, which are only the changing modalities of one and the same life. The second half of the paragraph sets up the essay's argument: the impression's self-givenness is not its own achievement, so it refers to a life that is the same in all impressions — and, later, to absolute Life.

This ever-changing impressional totality is our flesh. "Car notre chair n'est rien d'autre que cela qui se souffrant, se subissant et se supportant soi-même et ainsi s'éprouvant soi-même et jouissant de soi selon des impressions sans cesse renaissantes, n'est cependant possible, comme chacune des impressions qui la composent, que dans la vie" (p. 168) — a life whose unity in its immanent self-revelation is identically that of all these impressions, making them one flesh. The definition of flesh is affective and temporal: not a material substrate but the continuous self-undergoing of impressions. Its unity is not a synthesis of impressions (Husserl's passive synthesis) but the unity of the one life in which each is given.

Body and flesh are therefore opposed by the radicality of an original phenomenological dualism. The body, lacking in itself any power of manifestation, must ask its manifestation of the world's outside-itself and is thus constituted as a worldly body — the intuitive forms of space and time and the representational categories under which it is subsumed being only modes of the process of exteriorization in which it is phenomenalized. Flesh, self-impressing in life's process of self-revelation, holds its revelation from life alone. In the world every body is possible, but a flesh never arises elsewhere or otherwise than in life. Before asking what phenomenological properties flesh receives from life, one must analyse the relation of body and flesh phenomenologically — ask which of the two is more essential, giving access to the other (pp. 168–169). Henry introduces a "phenomenological dualism" to replace the Cartesian dualism of substances: not two things, soul and body, but two modes of appearing, life and world, under which the same human being is given as flesh and as body.

In the world the body appears first as extended, with shapes and figures, available to geometrical knowledge. But a worldly body is not only extended; it is sensible, with an impressional texture — red, dark, resonant, painful, nauseating — that exteriority alone does not explain. According to Galileo's essential analysis, taken up by Descartes in the wax analysis of the second *Meditation*, extended body is in itself neither coloured, sonorous, odorous, agreeable nor disagreeable, beautiful nor ugly. Its sensible, axiological and affective stratum comes to it from elsewhere than its ek-static structure. The wax passage is used for its Galilean premise: secondary qualities do not belong to extension. Henry's twist: they do not belong to ek-stasis either, so they must come from life.

Hence this evidence: every sensible body — felt, seen, heard, touched or moved — presupposes another body that feels, sees, touches or moves it, operations of a second body that constitutes the first and makes it possible: a transcendental, constituting body, a body-subject or "subjective" body without which the body-object of the world would not be. Of this transcendental body, principle rather than object of experience, one must now ask: to what mode of appearing does it ultimately belong? The two-body argument of essays I and VIII is generalized to all the senses.

In contemporary phenomenology, Husserl's and Merleau-Ponty's especially, the transcendental body is still cut out against the world's horizon and secretly subordinate to it. It is not reduced to an object of perception — it becomes perception's worker, the bodily subject replacing classical understanding. But as long as it is interpreted as an intentional body throwing us into the world, a making-visible whose phenomenality arises with the coming-outside of the Outside, nothing changes in the traditional conception of phenomenality or of the body that depends on it. On the contrary, our body is now of the world in a double sense: not only an object in the world but, opening onto it in its ek-static appearing, still belonging to it and radically subject to it. A note: this double belonging, as seer and visible, as "touching" and "tangible," makes the late Merleau-Ponty say of the body located in the world while opening us to it that "il en est" (*Le visible et l'invisible*, p. 178); for a radical critique, Henry refers to *Incarnation*, §§ 21 and 31 (pp. 169–170). "Il en est" — the body "is of" the world's flesh — is the formula of the chiasm. Henry's charge is that Merleau-Ponty's late ontology does not escape the world but makes the body fully worldly, in both its sensing and its sensed aspects.

Is this not so, one will ask? The operations of the transcendental body that give colours, sounds and smells are those of our senses, "distance senses" that join us to things and can do so only insofar as the "transcendence" of the world's ek-stasis operates in them. The objection is the Heideggerian one of essay I (p. 33): the senses are senses of distance because of transcendence.

Here the aporia of every worldly theory of the body bars the way. In the world's outside-itself no impression, no flesh, is possible, since flesh experiences itself only in life's immanent self-revelation. Our body may rise toward the world, this bundle of intentionalities surpass itself toward things' sensible qualities; these operations of the transcendental body, of our senses, are accomplished only insofar as they are impressionally given to themselves in life's self-givenness. How could we see anything outside us if our vision were nothing in itself, if hearing were not as such a "phenomenon," if touching and grasping were not living operations lived in themselves, able to guide themselves in their concrete performance? The phrase "able to guide themselves" adds a functional argument: a sensing that was not given to itself could not adjust its own exercise (focus, explore). Self-givenness is the condition of perceptual competence.

The aporia of the worldly theory of the body is only another name for the one besetting intentional phenomenology generally: how does intentionality, which makes everything visible, reveal itself to itself? Husserl avoided the classical circle of infinite regress only by abandoning transcendental life to anonymity. Essays III (p. 61) and V (pp. 112–114) are compressed into a sentence.

Moreover, our transcendental body is not limited to our senses, nor do these merely surpass themselves intentionally toward things. The body is also the seat of original immanent movements — not only the orienting movements of the senses, indispensable to their exercise. This body moves itself originally in itself. What appears externally in the world as the objective displacement of our hands, for instance, is in itself the self-movement of a power of grasping that remains in itself in its performance, given to itself in life's impressional self-givenness. Considered more attentively, the transcendental body, far from reducing to an intentional body, presupposes in its very intentionality — and a fortiori in its self-movement — this primitive auto-impressionality excluding all exteriority. Only on this condition is any power possible: impressionally given to itself like a pain or a suffering, one with itself and never separated from itself, so put in possession of itself, it can unfold itself from itself and act (pp. 170–171). "Self-movement" (*automouvement*) is the Biranian effort, now described as auto-impressional. The comparison of a power's self-givenness to a pain is striking: the "I can" is given to itself in the same way as suffering, by undergoing itself.

If all our body's powers can act only in life, we are forced into a complete reversal of the traditional conception of the body. It is not our worldly body, located in or open to the world, that defines our body's original reality, but our flesh, in whose auto-impressionality all powers are placed in themselves and so able to act, that gives us access to the body — the sensible bodies of the universe, our own objective sensible body, and the intentional body itself. But flesh gives access to this body and through it to the world's only because it first gives us access to itself, giving itself impressionally to itself where all self-givenness occurs, in and through life. One must therefore return to the initial question: not the features of the worldly body, but the essential phenomenological properties flesh holds from life. The "reversal" is the programme of *Incarnation*: flesh is not a special kind of body; the body is what flesh constitutes and gives access to.

From what life? As our impressions and the flesh they compose cannot bring themselves into themselves, being given to themselves only in life's self-givenness, so this life, insofar as it is ours, does not itself perform the self-givenness that makes it life. Only an absolute Life, bearing the capacity ours lacks in principle — that of bringing itself into itself, generating itself in the process of its self-revelation and in the form of it — can make it so that there is life somewhere and, in it alone, in this unique, absolute Life that alone has the power to live, all the lives that are alive only in it. Only the properties of this absolute Life — not factual properties but transcendental possibilities included in its self-generation — can account for the phenomenological properties of every conceivable life, and so of our flesh, insofar as it holds them from life (pp. 171–172). The regress from impression to life to absolute Life is the argument of essay III (pp. 67–68), now applied to flesh. "Factual properties" versus "transcendental possibilities": the properties of flesh are not features found in it but conditions of its being given.

This absolute process in which Life comes into itself must be examined, however briefly. Living means "experiencing oneself." To the absolute process in which Life comes into itself, engenders itself in experiencing itself and so revealing itself to itself, belongs in principle the Ipseity without which no self-experience is possible. Since Life is never first a concept but a real, phenomenologically actual life, the Ipseity in which it experiences itself is also real and actual: a real Self, "le Premier Soi vivant en laquelle la Vie se révèle à soi, son Verbe" (p. 172). Because this Self, its Word, belongs to Life's most radical possibility, a Self belongs to every conceivable life, and to everything that finds its condition in life's self-revelation: every flesh and every impression. No flesh without a Self, such that the Self implied in this flesh's self-givenness is the Self of this flesh as it is the flesh of this Self. "Pas de chair qui soit une chair anonyme, impersonnelle, la chair du monde." Nor any impression: no pain, suffering or joy that is no one's. The phrase "flesh of the world" names Merleau-Ponty's anonymous *chair du monde* and rejects it. The principle that every impression is someone's grounds the singularity of flesh in the ipseity generated in Life, as in essays III and VI.

We say "flesh," "impression." But why must life's self-givenness take, in us, in our finite life, the form of a flesh and the many impressions forming its continuous fabric? Have we not said that all the features of our flesh come from life, ultimately from absolute Life, the only Life that exists and from which no life can be separated without ceasing to experience itself and so to live? How then does absolute Life come into itself so as to be the origin of our flesh and its properties? The question is why the finite mode of life is carnal, impressional.

In its own immanence, Henry answers. Because in the immanence of its self-revelation Life remains in itself in an unceasing auto-affection, this is also the property of our flesh, from which nothing ever separates us, whose untearable fabric knows no break. But this immanence of our flesh cannot be posited speculatively, like that of substance in Spinozism; it must be understood phenomenologically. How do we experience it, so that it is nothing but the revelation of absolute Life in us, the way this Life experiences itself in our flesh, which in the end experiences itself only in this Life? The rejection of Spinoza is significant: immanence is not a metaphysical relation of modes to substance but a phenomenological one, to be shown in experience.

What experiences itself without distance or gap, not through a sense, is in essence Affectivity, or pure pathos. Every suffering is given to itself in its affectivity — not its own, nor ours, but absolute Life's. The Affectivity in which absolute Life comes into itself, experiences and enjoys itself, is not a fact, state or property among others; it is the ultimate principled possibility of a process ceaselessly being made and never unmade: "le procès éternel en lequel cette Vie s'éprouve et s'aime éternellement elle-même en son Verbe qui s'éprouve et s'aime éternellement en elle" (p. 173). This "Archi-possibilité" is an "Archi-passibilité," whose phenomenological effectuation is absolute Life's pure phenomenological matter, the original Affectivity in which all auto-affection, all life, all flesh consists. Only because our finite life does not itself hold arch-passibility — the capacity to bring itself into itself in a pathic phenomenological effectuation — because it is passively given to itself only in absolute Life's arch-passibility, is it a flesh such as ours. In absolute Life's arch-passibility every flesh is "passible," and in it, possible. "Notre chair à vrai dire n'est rien d'autre que cela, la passibilité d'une vie finie puisant sa possibilité dans l'Archi-passibilité de la Vie infinie" (pp. 173–174). The Trinitarian formula (Life loves itself in its Word, which loves itself in Life) gives arch-passibility its theological content: the eternal self-affection of the Father in the Son is love. The answer to the question of finitude: flesh is the mode of a life that receives its self-affection rather than performing it. Flesh is finitude as receptivity to oneself.

What phenomenological properties does flesh hold from life? Not particular properties or a set of them, however essential, but its very condition of flesh — the suffering and enjoying auto-impressionality that constitutes the pure phenomenological substance of any conceivable flesh. Flesh, holding its condition from life, is not limited to a multiplicity of impressions or sensations, nor to a set of powers. The analysis of powers had already forced a change of level, from the phenomenological status of intentionality to that of self-givenness. Since none of the powers of our flesh brought itself into itself — each delivered to itself without willing it, independently of its power — each meets within itself what it has no power over and against which it can do nothing: an absolute non-power. Only in absolute Life's "hyperpuissance," not of itself, is each given to itself, as is the flesh of which it has become a power. To Pilate, boasting of his power to release or crucify him, Christ's brutal answer: "You would have no power over me unless it had been given you from above" (John 19:10–11) (p. 174). The "non-power" at the heart of every power is the late form of the passivity analysed throughout the volume. The Pilate verse, a favourite of Henry's (*C'est moi la vérité*), grounds every power, even political power, in a givenness the holder does not command.

That this "giving from above," life's arch-giving to every life, bears the arch-passibility in which every self-givenness of a life seeks its final possibility can be observed again. What is given to every power in this arch-giving is not a semblance of gift or of power, nor this or that particular power (to grasp, to move), but what, having placed this power in itself and put it in possession of itself, makes it a true power, a "pouvoir pouvoir," able to exercise itself as often as it wishes, freely. "Car la liberté n'est pas une idée mais l'exercice effectif d'un ensemble de pouvoirs concrets, en fin de compte ce pouvoir pouvoir qu'aucun pouvoir ne tient de soi, qu'aucun homme ne peut revendiquer comme le sien" (p. 174). The doubled "pouvoir pouvoir" (the power to be able) names the hyperpower of essay VI (pp. 135–136). Freedom is real, but not self-founded.

How can one recognize that the arch-giving in which the self-givenness that makes every power true and free is held draws its phenomenological effectuation from absolute Life's arch-passibility? By the fact that this self-givenness constitutive of every power finds its phenomenological matter in affectivity. Two admirable analyses in modern philosophy make this evident: Biran's problematic of the cogito interpreted as an "I can," whose phenomenological possibility lies in pathos, since every effective action is an effort and every effort a "feeling of effort"; and Kierkegaard, who radicalized this intuition of Affectivity as the ultimate phenomenological condition of all action — grasped not as the exercise of a power but explicitly as a possibility of power, as in the crucial proposition of *The Concept of Anxiety*, "… the anxious possibility of being able," on which Kierkegaard's theory of eroticism and sin rests (p. 175). Kierkegaard's "anxious possibility of being able" (the state of Adam before the fall) is the affective experience of one's own freedom as given; *Incarnation*'s third part builds its account of sin and the erotic on it.

Absolute Life's arch-passibility is not a concept to be invoked whenever convenient; it must be grasped where it is, in the absolute process in which Life comes into itself, as its ultimate phenomenological possibility. That our flesh, in its pathic auto-impressionality, receives its possibility from this arch-passibility leads every phenomenology of the flesh back to a phenomenology of incarnation in a radical sense. Incarnation can no longer mean only man's incarnate condition, with its problems — the body, its relation to flesh, all behaviour in which flesh is involved, action in general with its affective "motivations" (that is, its essence), eroticism. "In-carnation" no longer designates this factual flesh, taken as the paradigm of all facticity. It designates "la venue dans une chair," the process from which flesh comes and in which it remains, so that, constantly experiencing itself in the extreme passivity and passibility of its finitude as unable to give itself to itself, it necessarily refers to that process, to absolute Life's arch-giving in its arch-passibility (pp. 175–176). The hyphen "In-carnation" turns a state into a coming: flesh is always a coming-into-flesh. The phrase "paradigm of all facticity" targets the Heideggerian and Sartrean use of embodiment as the very figure of brute facticity.

There is thus a "Before-the-flesh," the Before of In-carnation, residing in Life's arch-passibility. In its reference to this Before, our flesh shows a strange affinity with the other essential determinations of the living. It ceases to appear as a mysterious, contingent addition to its condition as living, a sort of empirical appendix, like our objective body, and joins a network of properties belonging to an a priori older than the world. This secondary situation of flesh relative to Life's arch-passibility is strictly parallel to that of the Self (and so of the "me," the "ego"), and of the living in general. In each case, understanding what is at issue — the living, its ipseity, its flesh — requires placing oneself before them, in a dimension of origin. This dimension is the same for each: there is life only in absolute Life, a Self only in the Ipseity in which absolute Life comes into itself, flesh only in the arch-passibility in which this coming is accomplished — "une Vie pathétique donc, une vie qui, en sa jouissance originaire de soi, est une Vie d'amour" (p. 176). The "Before" of essay VI's birth is now the Before of flesh. The three determinations — life, Self, flesh — share one origin.

That the "Before the Self" and the "Before the flesh" are one, both designating Life's arch-passibility — since in it Life experiences itself in the Ipseity of its Self and of every conceivable Self, as in it every flesh is joined to itself — casts a singular light on the human condition. If one and the same pathic experience makes the Self a Self and flesh a flesh — the ipseity of the first and the auto-impressionality of the second — then Self and flesh go together: man is "ce Soi charnel vivant" that has nothing to do with the definitions that fabricate, in various forms, a composite of mind and matter, soul and body, "subject" and "object," of which it is impossible to understand anything, today as much as at its first steps in Greece or elsewhere. The definition of man as a "living carnal Self" dissolves the mind-body problem: what is called mind and what is called body are two names for one self-givenness. The answer to essay I's title question is here in its final form.

Not only the human condition is illuminated in Life's arch-passibility; that arch-passibility itself is, and for that reason alone our condition, which is never explained from the world but only from Life. These premises of the human condition find their dazzling statement in John's Prologue. At its centre is the Word, appearing twice: once in relation to Life, once in relation to flesh. The unconditional affirmation of this double relation overturns the horizon of Greek thought, still largely ours. In the Greek world the relation of *Logos* to life is conceived only as an opposition — decisive because it paradoxically supplies a definition of man, who is distinguished from animals by the *Logos* they lack. *Logos* means both reason and the ability to speak, to form ideal meanings. If one adds that we speak of things, predicating of them, only insofar as they show themselves to us, one finds an original connection by which the Greek *Logos* rests on, and is identical with, the world's (or "Nature's") appearing. The history of Western thought will discover that this specific differentiation of *Logos* and living encloses an insurmountable difficulty (pp. 176–177). The difficulty is the one *Incarnation* develops: the definition of man as *zōon logon echon* places his specific difference (logos) on the side of world-appearing and his genus (zōon) on the side of the biological, so that life itself is never thought.

Greek thought revealed this difficulty in its encounter — rather, its gigantic confrontation — with Christianity. Christianity escapes the aporia because its *Logos* is no longer the *Logos* of the world but the *Logos* of Life, and its conception of the body is no longer the Greek one of a worldly body but that of a flesh that comes only in life — ultimately in the *Logos* of Life that is God's Word. The confrontation of the Fathers with Gnosticism and Hellenic philosophy over the flesh of Christ is the historical material of *Incarnation*'s second part.

Two abrupt propositions convey John's initiatory revelation: "In the beginning was the Word" and "And the Word became flesh." The first has been accounted for, if the process of God's self-revelation (explicitly defined by John as Life) in his Word does not generate the Word at its end but in itself, as what the process consists in: "In the beginning." The second (v. 14) shines with brilliant light if flesh is self-impressed only in the arch-passibility of absolute Life in its Word. If flesh draws its ultimate condition of possibility from the Word of Life, the Incarnation of the Word, far from being absurd as it was to the Greeks, is rooted in the depths of things. Conversely, flesh's capacity to receive the Word from which it comes is grounded. In Irenaeus's language and brilliant intuition: "God can vivify the flesh," "flesh can be vivified by God," and "flesh will be found capable of receiving and containing the power of God" (*Against Heresies*, French trans. Rousseau, pp. 576–577) (pp. 177–178). Irenaeus is the hero of *Incarnation*'s patristic section: against the Gnostics, he affirms that flesh is capable of salvation because it was made by the Word. Henry's phenomenological reading makes this capacity a consequence of the structure of flesh as received self-impression.

Was the Incarnation of the Word necessary? So admirable that it would have occurred even without sin? Every finitude presupposes the infinite, which owes it nothing; the coming of the Messiah can only be a gratuitous act of the power that governs all. But the Incarnation of the Word is not wholly contained in Christ's historical coming. The Word made flesh in Christ is God's eternal Word, in whom everything was created — not only the world, but everything foreign to it: our flesh, the ipseity of our Self, our life. When God breathed his life into a particle of clay, making it the living carnal transcendental Self that each man is, its body was only matter, dust indeed; but its flesh was wholly life. The coming of each living one in its carnal ipseity belongs to life's immanent generation. Only incarnation understood from this generation lets us understand creation, distinguishing in it the process of exteriorization in the world from Life's pathic embrace. "C'est dans la lumière éblouissante du Prologue que s'éclaire la Genèse" (p. 178). The scholastic question (Duns Scotus's thesis that the Incarnation would have occurred without the Fall) is answered with a distinction between creation (exteriorization: the body from dust) and generation (life breathed into it: the flesh). Genesis 2:7 is read through John 1: the breath of life is the generation of flesh in the Word.

How the Incarnation is the Revelation of God — according to Christ's own words about himself, taken up by his disciples and become the reiterated thesis of the Fathers and so of "Christianity" — belongs to an unforeseen "Archi-intelligibilité" if Incarnation is no longer understood naively as coming into an opaque body but into a phenomenological flesh; if this flesh, in the passibility of its unbearable finitude, does not merely experience itself in the play of its ever-changing (that is, ever the same) impressions; if every flesh comes to itself, in its auto-impressionality, only in the arch-passibility in which Life eternally embraces itself in its Word. "En toute chair alors, au fond de sa Nuit, c'est l'Œil de Dieu qui nous regarde. Toute chair sera jugée" (p. 179). That is why, with singular lucidity — following Paul and John — the Fathers grasped flesh as the place of both perdition and salvation: perdition of a flesh become idolatrous of itself, taking itself for the principle of its pleasure, adoring itself in this principle and its effects; salvation of a flesh given to itself in its generation in the Word, loving in itself only this Word that joined it to itself in the beginning, which it receives as its essence in the Eucharist (pp. 178–179). The "Eye of God" in the Night of flesh is Eckhart's formula ("the eye in which I see God is the same eye in which God sees me"), reread as the presence of the generating Life in self-impression. Perdition is defined phenomenologically: flesh forgetting its origin and taking itself as the source of its own enjoyment — the "transcendental illusion of the ego" of *C'est moi la vérité* applied to the flesh. The Eucharist appears as flesh receiving its own essence.

"Arch-intelligibility," because the power of revelation at work here is foreign to every form of vision or "e-vidence," sensible or intelligible — the second letting Greeks (and Moderns) grasp the content of the first adequately. The degrees of such knowledge are many; its "kinds" can be classified and its pertinence assessed: it is the knowledge of sages and scientists. That the power of revelation is entrusted to flesh, and that this carnal power of revelation is that of the absolute — life's pathos devolved to each living one as a privilege no one can take from it, the sign of its election and the reality of life — that is new. Yet it is this invincible revelation, "inscrite en lettres de feu dans l'invisible de notre chair," that is given in it, in each of its impressions, in each of its powers, "in the giving from above" (p. 179). "Kinds" of knowledge glances at Spinoza's three kinds and the Platonic divided line. The essay ends by uniting the volume's two vocabularies: the "from above" of John 19 and the auto-impressionality of flesh are the same giving. The theological reading is not superimposed on the phenomenology; it is what the phenomenology of flesh, carried to its origin, discovers.

## X. Philosophy and Phenomenology

Like every philosophy, phenomenology is a production of thought, with a specific method and language. But for phenomenology neither is first: both serve to express a prior object, "the thing itself," and only the delimitation of this object can orient the search for an appropriate method and language. The essay appeared in 1998 in the *Encyclopédie philosophique universelle* (vol. IV, *Le discours philosophique*). Its subject — method and language — makes it the volume's reckoning with phenomenological methodology (Husserl, Fink) and with Heidegger's thought of language.

Phenomenology's object is not things, "phenomena," but their phenomenality, the way they show themselves. But this phenomenality is what gives us access to phenomena, lets us know them and speak of them. Hence a first evidence: "l'objet de la phénoménologie constitue identiquement sa méthode et son langage" (p. 182), because the appearing of the thing opens the path on which it comes to meet us. Pure appearing thus traces the method and is the origin of language: only because things are discovered to us can we analyse them and produce the predications that compose our discourse and knowledge. The identity of object, method and language holds for world-appearing; the essay will show that it breaks down for life.

This intuition leads to a formidable paradox: method and language become useless if phenomenology's "thing itself" has already brought itself before us as the first illumination outside which no phenomenon exists. The famous § 7 of *Sein und Zeit* ran into this paradox in defining phenomenology by the two Greek components of its name, *phainomenon* and *logos*, its object and method. It became clear that the *logos* makes visible all it says of the phenomenon in the phenomenon's own coming to light. But if everything that shows itself is reached in its being by a method identical to this showing and by a saying that presupposes it — if one may "call phenomenology every showing of beings" — what task can phenomenology claim? "What is it that phenomenology is to 'let us see'?" (*Sein und Zeit*, p. 35). Heidegger's own question in § 7c is taken as the paradox's best formulation.

Must one suppose that in the phenomenon flashes a power unnoticed by common or scientific thought, so that phenomenology's task is to make manifest what in manifestation does not manifest itself? An original method would then be required, demanding not a tautological consideration of the phenomenon but a conversion of our gaze on the world, in which unknown phenomena would be discovered. Phenomenology's object would no longer precede its method; method would disclose its object — not a new region of being but the source of all being and every being. This conversion, which tells phenomenology "what it has to let us see," is the phenomenological reduction, the core of its method. Heidegger's answer in § 7 (what phenomenology shows is the being of beings, which mostly does not show itself) and Husserl's (the reduction) are placed side by side.

Setting aside his reservations, Henry first sets out this method in a systematic order proposed by Jean-François Lavigne. The reliance on Lavigne signals that the following seven steps are a neutral, standard reconstruction, not Henry's.

(1) Phenomenological method, defined against the natural attitude, begins with a description of it, of the world's immediate appearing. Significantly, this description of the world is a description of the I's experience by itself, an empirical self-description. Henry flags the starting point: method begins from a self-description that is already a seeing of oneself.

(2) A description free of presuppositions is not enough; a critique of knowledge is required. The task is not to explain phenomena but to understand in its matrix the act of knowing — the relations, procedures and structures in which knowledge proves its validity. This elucidation concerns lived experiences of knowing, grasped in their essential structures. The epistemological motive of the 1907 *Idea of Phenomenology*.

(3) Experiences are no longer apprehended psychologically, in relation to ourselves, as objects of conviction and belief, as in inner experience — strictly parallel to outer experience, where the thing is placed before us as an object of belief. The reduction begins when every belief in the reality of this self or of things is understood as a problematic transcendence and suspended. The parallelism of inner and outer experience is the point Henry exploits: inner experience is as ek-static as outer.

(4) If the reduction gives us experiences stripped of their sense of reality, what do we now seek to know in them? Husserl's whole effort is to show that in ordinary psychological knowledge concepts, essences of another order than natural existence, are at work, and that we have an intuition of these essences, which delimit the new field of research. The turn from facts to essences.

(5) This intuition of essence is defined by showing how it is accomplished in the method of variations: the systematic, arbitrary production of fictive images resembling an original leads to the invariant preserved through all variations, without which none could be recognized as an instance of its kind. The identical thus emerges and can be intuited for itself. Henry will later stress that the variations require an original.

(6) Beyond eidetic intuition, phenomenological method is intentional analysis. It is not an analysis of data but rests on the fundamental knowledge that all consciousness aims at something that is always much more than what is actually aimed at. It discloses all the potentialities implied in and exceeding phenomenological actuality, on the noetic side of the multiplicities of constitution and on the noematic side of the clarification of the objective meaning aimed at (pp. 183–184). The structure of horizons (*Cartesian Meditations* § 20).

(7) Here phenomenological method unfolds fully and avows its ambition. Every constituted objectivity, corresponding to its essential type, refers to the essential form of the multiple, real or possible intentionality constitutive of it. All conscious life is governed by a universal constitutive a priori whose examination is transcendental phenomenology's task. All objective being and all truth being grounded in transcendental subjectivity, a truth about subjectivity itself is no exception: it too is grounded in this subjectivity. Phenomenology appears as an elucidation of every possible form of being in a systematic self-elucidation of transcendental life. The last step sets the trap: the self-elucidation of transcendental life must be a constitution of that life by itself.

In the *Sixth Cartesian Meditation* (French trans. Depraz, 1994), where Fink radicalizes method to the point of making it phenomenology's content, the theme is that the being of transcendental subjectivity, unable to be given in the reduction like a being, must itself be reduced: it splits into an ego constituting the world and the ego of the "phenomenologizing Spectator" who observes it. This "reduction of the reduction" finds a remarkable illustration in the question of a phenomenological language. Fink's text (1932, revised with Husserl's annotations) is Henry's privileged case because it draws the consequences of a method of seeing applied to subjectivity: subjectivity must be divided.

The Spectator, observing constitution without taking part, acquires transcendental knowledge which he translates into propositions forming the content of phenomenological science. In what language? Language is a transcendental faculty, the production of meanings. But insofar as meanings are constitutive of the world, every language, despite its transcendental origin, is a natural language, aimed at beings and conveying worldly concepts. The Spectator can express his knowledge only by an analogizing use of natural expressions; but analogy itself belongs to the natural attitude, working from being to being, so it too must be reduced. Fink's solution, borrowed from Husserl: by returning to the living evidence of subjectivity, the Spectator must verify the pertinence of his predicative results. Phenomenology's language returns "to the things themselves" (pp. 184–185). Henry notes the circularity: the language is verified by returning to the very evidence it was meant to express.

A difficulty remains. According to Natalie Depraz (Introduction, p. 41), Fink "shifts the thematic accent from the constituting ego to the phenomenologizing ego." Because the Spectator, placed outside constituting activity, no longer constitutes, he can no longer speak either, if language consists in the intentional formation of meanings. "If phenomenologizing activity is brought into play immediately after the reduction, at this initial stage it is not only without concepts but in principle without language [...] The Spectator has not the least possibility of expressing his theoretical insights predicatively" (Fink, pp. 150–151). The Spectator's speechlessness is the logical consequence of Fink's construction: a non-constituting gaze cannot form meanings.

To overcome the aporia, a transcendental motivation must be found in the Spectator that re-engages him in the processes of world-constitution. It will be, for each monad come to itself in the reduction and wishing to communicate with others, the objectification of its phenomenological knowledge, anchored in the process of "mundanization" linking the transcendental to the natural attitude. To the "primary mundanization" of the constituting ego, inserting it into the nexus of beings it has constituted, is added a "secondary mundanization" of the phenomenologizing ego, drawn into the former and bringing itself into the world by the objective expression of its knowledge. Fink's "mundanization" (*Verweltlichung*) of the phenomenologist is the world-appearing's recapture of the subject that escaped it.

According to Husserl, in this theory of method "the opposition between constituting ego and phenomenologizing ego is too accentuated" (Fink, p. 223). Should the critique not be radicalized? In its gaze on the constituting ego, the Spectator may stop participating in constitution, but does his gaze stop being a constituting operation? Fink can affirm it — "the unveiling of constitutive becoming is not itself constituting" — only by specifying, in three additions to the manuscript, that the constitution escaped by phenomenologizing is "constitution in the sense of world-constitution" (p. 75, nn. 44–45). Identified with a non-constituting operation, the Spectator's gaze would reduce to an immediate, naive evidence, whereas in Husserl evidence arises in a process of intentional elucidation; as the immanent seeing of transcendental reflection, it presupposes the original, especially retentional, syntheses of time-constitution that supply its object — the intuition of life, the *Selbsterscheinung* of the flow, being only life's self-constitution. With this major difficulty: since only the constituted phases of the flow offer themselves to its gaze and to the Spectator's, the constituting phases remain plunged in the anonymity that method and language were meant to overcome (pp. 185–186). Henry radicalizes Husserl's own objection: the Spectator's seeing is itself constituting (temporal, retentional), so it too sees only the constituted. The anonymity of the constituting is not overcome but reproduced at a higher level.

One must return to the question of pure phenomenology, of transcendental life. On the one hand, the phenomenological language meant to transmit transcendental knowledge presupposes its acquisition, a self-givenness of life, to which, on the other, it must refer to verify its "analogizing" predications. To try instead to produce this primitive givenness through method and language, which constantly presuppose it, is to move in a circle — the product of a conception of phenomenality as positing-outside-itself, whose ultimate possibility is inner splitting, division, of which the reflexive split of the reduction and the objectification of language are only derivatives. The Greek concept of phenomenon takes phenomenology back under its control, dictating its method and language, and, applied to life, can only denature it. Fink: "In carrying out the reduction, transcendental life puts itself outside itself; producing 'the Spectator,' it splits itself, divides itself. But this division is the condition of possibility of transcendental subjectivity's coming to itself" (p. 76, Fink's emphasis) (p. 186). Fink's sentence is the perfect counter-thesis to Henry's: for Fink, self-division is the condition of self-givenness; for Henry, self-givenness excludes division.

Before showing how the project of disclosing transcendental life by putting it outside itself rests on the impossibility of reaching life this way, and how this impossibility led Husserl to a complete, though clandestine, reworking of his method, Henry examines the two phenomenological instances framing the whole problematic: life and the ego, constantly presupposed but never grasped in their inner possibility. The "clandestine reworking" is the substitution of noematic essences for real life (two units below).

That inner possibility lies in the generation of the ego in absolute phenomenological life's self-generation. In this process life constantly comes into itself, experiencing itself in an original Ipseity whose phenomenological effectuation is a singular Self, the "transcendental ego" in question. How can natural man, lost in the world, find it again and reach true life? A badly posed question: "dans la vie, nous y sommes toujours déjà" (p. 187). How? In the very movement by which life comes into itself. This is the decisive intuition that blocks phenomenology's methodological project: there is no way to reach life except in the movement by which life reaches itself; and in this self-reaching of absolute life each "ego," each transcendental Self, reaches itself and is alive. "Always already in life" is Henry's answer to the reduction as conversion: no path leads into life, because we have never left it. The formula reverses Heidegger's "always already in the world."

Thus the reduction's claim to give access to life by putting it outside itself — in the outside-itself that is the world's appearing — comes undone. Such appearing turns from itself so forcefully that whatever it shows is given as exterior, other, different: never as a Self. If life experiences itself, no life is possible in the world, nor any Self. The world, Difference, is the site of a hetero-affection to which thought entrusts every conceivable affection. But life is accomplished as auto-affection: acosmic, non-ek-static, invisible. "Personne n'a jamais vu la vie." The phenomenality of this auto-affection is a pathos, the original affectivity in which life is given to itself: an arch-revelation whose pure phenomenological substance is a Flesh — neither constituting nor constituted, not relating intentionally to itself, foreign to the world and its ek-static temporality, revealed to itself at each point of its suffering and enjoyment. Eternal play of Life and the Living, life constantly comes into itself as the living Self it is each time. But this reciprocal phenomenological interiority of Life and the Living is revealed only in Life, in its own mode of revelation; it escapes thought as it escapes the world, "parce qu'elle ne se laisse jamais voir en aucun dehors" (pp. 187–188). "Neither constituting nor constituted" places flesh outside the whole Husserlian framework of constitution. "Reciprocal interiority" of Life and the Living is the Johannine "I in the Father and the Father in me" (John 14:10), stated phenomenologically.

It escapes all method, phenomenological method included, as the method's history overwhelmingly shows. In its first formulation, the reduction claims to establish transcendental life's existence in an evidence that, keeping to what it really sees, accomplishes an absolute givenness of this life, then called *cogitatio*. Since life escapes seeing, its supposed absolute givenness turns into the perpetual vanishing of a Heraclitean flux, suddenly declared ungraspable. This evanescent life must then be replaced by its transcendent noematic essence — the thematic turn in which phenomenological method changes its object. Behind this substitution of objects (on the pretext that knowledge of singular *cogitationes* cannot yield a universal science) is hidden the substitution of one mode of givenness for another: of thought's seeing, culminating in the reduction's evidence, for life's pathic arch-revelation. But if a transcendent essence suits thought's evidence, this noematic essence of life must still be constructed, which can be done only from real life, which escapes every such evidence. Husserl may say, in a text where the eidetic reduction seems to support the phenomenological one, that in the absence of real life its images will take its place; one may ask how they are possible without recourse to the original. We are thus led back to the presupposition of an original givenness as the condition of all method and all language concerning it (p. 188). The history of the reduction is told as a drama: from the 1907 promise of absolute givenness, through the Heraclitean flux (*Ideas I* § 75), to eidetics. The objection to imaginative variation is decisive: images of life presuppose life given to itself. This is the core of Henry's critique in *Phénoménologie matérielle*, "La méthode phénoménologique."

This presupposition was Heidegger's in § 7 of *Sein und Zeit*. In Heidegger the phenomenological reduction has disappeared, replaced according to him by the Difference of beings and Being; for Jean-François Courtine, anguish takes its place. Behind these analyses lies the thesis that method constitutes no primordial instance, since the path to the phenomenon lies in its phenomenality. It is concerning language, in *On the Way to Language*, that this interpretation shows its force (pp. 188–189). Heidegger's ontological difference as a substitute for the reduction is his own claim (GA 24, § 5); Courtine's thesis on anxiety as the Heideggerian reduction comes from *Heidegger et la phénoménologie* (1990).

It is accompanied by statements that seem formal. First, speech (*la parole*, *die Sprache*) precedes all human speaking. It is not we who speak but speech. Speech is everywhere already present. "Speech itself must [...] say itself and give itself to us." Only because speech addresses us can we understand a word of it and speak in turn. It would invert the order of things to imagine that, now, we speak about speech and theorize it, when, believing we speak about it, we always "let it say to us its own unfolding." This precedence of speech over human speaking determines their relation and makes man what he is: "the human being is borne by speech into its own"; "the Saying [...] has already struck the human being full on"; hence "the human being rests in speech" (French trans., pp. 160, 175, 34, 181, 228). The citations assemble "Die Sprache spricht" from the essays of *Unterwegs zur Sprache*. Henry presents the thesis sympathetically: a word that precedes our speaking is close to his "word of life." The divergence lies in its phenomenology.

These relations owe their consistency to their phenomenological content. The saying of original speech is nothing but Greek appearing, the coming-outside in which what we see and can speak of is given. "Saying, *sagan*, means to show, to make appear, to free into a clearing…" The assimilation of saying to the world's appearing is constant: "from the saying in which the letting-appear of the world is set forth." It relies on a precious etymology: German *zeigen* (to show) and Old High German *sagan*, whence modern *sagen* (to say), say the same — a showing that means saying, a saying that means showing (pp. 185, 193). The etymology *Sage / zeigen* is Heidegger's in "The Way to Language." It confirms Henry's reading: Heidegger's speech is the world's appearing.

This reciprocity must be specified. What speech makes visible and speaks of is beings; but making beings visible is making visible something other and higher, the coming-into-presence in which beings come to us. "The word [...] is no longer merely [...] an instrument for naming something already there, already represented [...] a means of exhibiting what presents itself on its own. On the contrary: only the word grants coming-into-presence, that is, being, in which something can appear as a being" (p. 212). The quotation (from "Das Wort") is the strongest form of the thesis: the word grants being.

These references become precise when Heidegger asks whether there is a word for this other reign of the word, in which it renounces saying beings to say being. "The oldest word for the reign of the word thus thought, for saying, is *Logos*: *die Sage*, the Saying — which, by showing, lets beings appear in their 'it is.' The same word *Logos*, as the word for saying, is at the same time the word for being, that is, for the coming-into-presence of what is present" (p. 223). Henry needs this passage to fix the identity of Heidegger's *Logos* with the Greek *phainesthai*, against which the Johannine *Logos* will be set.

If appearing and speech are so linked that the former is what is experienced in the latter, if givenness itself gives itself in speech so that "speech speaks inasmuch as it says, that is, shows" (p. 241, Henry's emphasis), this connection of pure phenomenality and the saying of speech sets before us an abrupt evidence: everything about speech — what it says, how it says it, to whom — depends on how pure phenomenality phenomenalizes itself. The argument converts Heidegger's thesis into a conditional: if speech is appearing, there are as many kinds of speech as kinds of appearing.

If as world, speech is the speech of the world. What it says: unfolding as the world's coming-into-presence, it says the world and everything showing itself in it, the beings it predicates in many ways. It says it by unfolding thus, in the clearing of Difference. To whom? To those it strikes full in their being, making them humans capable of speech. The three questions — what, how, to whom — will be answered differently for life.

The whole set of relations organized around speech is overturned when life's phenomenality replaces the world's. As self-revelation, Life brings appearing into appearing, accomplishing the arch-revelation (the appearing of appearing) without which no coming-into-presence would occur. Because only Life reveals itself, only it speaks, and its speech is its own revelation. In its speech Life says itself. This speech belongs to each modality of life. Thus "la souffrance dit la souffrance, sans mot dire," without the intentional formation of a meaning (the meaning "suffering"), without any world unfolding or any being appearing. Suffering says itself without inner splitting; it comes to itself without putting itself outside itself in the transcendental reflection of the reduction — and on condition that it does not. How then does it speak? In its untearable and invincible pathic flesh, in its suffering and as suffering. But within this suffering, Life has already spoken otherwise, in an older suffering, the one in which it embraces itself in love and self-enjoyment — the suffering that inhabits every modality of Life, suffering or joy, because it is in each what gives it to itself, since in it Life gives itself to itself. Thus life originally speaks, by saying itself (pp. 190–191). "Suffering says suffering, without saying a word" (*sans mot dire*, the idiom "without a word") is the essay's formula for the word of life. The "older suffering" in which life speaks is the primitive *se souffrir* of essays II and VII, now interpreted as the original speech.

And to whom does it speak, in giving itself to itself and saying itself? To someone. But it is not enough to affirm that speech addresses someone or to postulate an affinity between them. This "someone" is a living transcendental Self; but in the world where the world's speech flashes there is no such Self, nor any Life, only an empty horizon and a dead being. The third question is answered negatively for Heidegger: his speech "strikes" humans, but its phenomenality contains no self to be struck.

In Life, on the contrary, the relations implied in all speech find their final possibility. A one-sentence transition, as in the text.

Engendering itself in its essential Ipseity as a living Self, Life has engendered the one to whom it speaks. No one is there before its speech. If it does not speak in a desert, it is only because the one it draws out of nothingness by its formidable call, the call to live, is precisely this living Self self-given in Life's self-givenness. The relation of the Word to the one it addresses is that of Life to the living, the generation of the Self in absolute Life's self-generation. That is why the Word of Life has already given the one it addresses the possibility of hearing it — "d'entendre à jamais en lui le bruit de sa naissance" (p. 191). The "call to live" answers essay IV's critique of Marion (p. 102): life's call has no addressee before it, because it creates its addressee by calling. "The sound of his birth" echoes "the murmur of life" in essay IV.

The Word of Life and the world's speech are opposed point for point. To the indifference of a speech that, giving in Difference, is separated from everything it gives and says, is added the unreality in principle of whatever comes to presence in it, so that this presence, gnawed by an older absence, is only "une efflorescence sur de la mort" (p. 191). This death strikes all human language, the most poetic and the most banal. When Trakl says, "When snow falls at the window, / long tolls the evening bell…," there is neither snow, nor window, nor sound, nor evening. When I say "I have a thaler in my pocket," I do not thereby own one. To such speech, unable to give anything, there remains at most the possibility of saying what is not — of deceiving. "Language, as the apostle says, is the universal evil" (pp. 191–192). The examples are calculated: Trakl's "Ein Winterabend," the poem Heidegger comments on in "Die Sprache"; Kant's thaler, the classic proof that existence is not a predicate. Heidegger's own poetic paradigm is used to show the unreality of the world's word. The "apostle" alludes to James 3:6–8 (the tongue as a world of iniquity, a restless evil).

The Word of Life, which as Life's self-givenness gives life, does not only oppose the world's speech, whose saying is Being, that is, Nothingness; it founds it. As the critique of the reduction showed, the formation of unreal noematic meanings about life presupposes life's givenness rather than producing it. "Being, that is, Nothingness" alludes to Heidegger's identification of being and the nothing (*What Is Metaphysics?*), turned into a charge.

From this ever-preceding life another speech is possible, still foreign to intentional language and with a far vaster field. Thus the cry that springs from suffering: a modality of life, whose "phonation" belongs to living subjective corporeity — a carnal act given to itself in Life's pathic flesh, not to the worldly body. So too each of our gestures and actions, "ces mains qui parlent quand nos lèvres se taisent," that "self-expression" Rousseau calls "the primitive speech," whose power Paul Audi has displayed. Even language in the usual sense — sensible words conveying ideal meanings — refers, in those meanings, to the life that produces them. "Ideology," in a decisive statement of Marx's, "is the language of real life." This ideology, this language, the immense sphere of gestural and bodily expression, of the living subjective body, depend first not on consciousness but on "living individuals," always "presupposed," already given to themselves in life (p. 192). The cry, the gesture and the "language of real life" (*The German Ideology*: "die Sprache des wirklichen Lebens") form a continuum from pre-intentional expression to meaningful speech. Paul Audi's *Rousseau, éthique et passion* (1997) is cited for the "primitive speech."

Many questions arise. If the phenomenality of phenomenology's method is the same as that of its object, *Logos* and Being suit each other, and philosophizing is possible as a work of elucidation on the ground of the very appearing it wants to bring to light. If, on the contrary, the method's ek-static phenomenality is heterogeneous to that of its object, life's pathic self-givenness, how can thought, philosophy, reach the latter? Are they not as impotent (unable to make life seen) as useless (life has always already revealed itself to itself) (pp. 192–193)? The objection is the volume's most serious: if life is invisible and always already given, a philosophy of life is both impossible and superfluous.

Impossible? It is precisely the impossibility of seeing life that led phenomenological method to substitute for real life its unreal transcendent essence. But what is the point of this detour? The answer to "impossible" is ironic: Husserl's method is the proof that life cannot be seen.

Useless, then? Archaic necessities answer the objection. Whenever relations were established among the living in the world, they had to resort to objective substitutes for their invisible life, to speak the world's language. When, at the dawn of time, these relations consisted in the exchange of products — which was only the exchange of human labours, subjective, living, invisible, resistant to all objective measure — these had to be replaced by ideal equivalents, so many hours of "abstract" labour, socially defined, "qualified" or not. The whole economic universe is only the set of these equivalents, "une sorte de double fantastique de la vie," one form of the world's language, which also includes all ideal knowledge. Husserl's phenomenological method, we see now, performed an analogous substitution from its first step, for similar reasons (p. 193). The parallel between economics and method is striking: both substitute ideal, communicable equivalents for an unmeasurable life. Philosophy, like the economy, is useful as a world-language of life, provided it does not take its substitutes for the original.

The general relation of the world's language and life's language must therefore be deepened. Two examples will serve as "variations" for disclosing an "essence" of language — though this essence must no longer be understood one-sidedly as a noematic transcendence but as a real essence, the innermost possibility of life in its concrete phenomenological effectuation. Henry uses Husserl's eidetic vocabulary ironically: the "essence" sought is not a noema but life itself.

Since the two languages refer to the two fundamental modes of phenomenality, imagine an extraordinary object given in both modes: would we not have two languages for it? Such an object exists: our own body. As an object of the world, it shows itself in the ek-static transcendence of an "outside," cashed out in the transcendental operations of the senses that open us to it as seen, touched, felt, grasped. But these operations are accomplished only given to themselves in life's pathic self-givenness. Thus, in accordance with the duality of appearing, we have two bodies, a worldly body and a living body, and two languages: one that speaks to us about it, the other in which it speaks to us (pp. 193–194). "A language about the body" and "a language in which the body speaks" map onto the body/flesh distinction of essay IX.

Certainly our own body, showing itself in the world (or another's), is perceived as a living body, with "eyes that see," "hands that grasp." Are these meanings not constituted in an intentional consciousness? Is the language about the subjective body not analogous to that designating worldly beings, making visible what it speaks of in order to speak of it? But phenomenology is about possibility. The content "grasping with the hands" would never be thrown before the mind if this grasping were not originally in me the invisible effort of a corporeity pathically joined to itself in Life's flesh. Once more, Fink's phenomenologist communicating his transcendental knowledge does not hold it from a prior reduction but from life, where it is accomplished. That is why not only the phenomenologist knows what "grasping" means. The last sentence is a dry rejoinder to Fink: ordinary speakers understand "grasping" without having performed the reduction, because they live it.

When, in his texts of 1804–1806, Biran made the brilliant discovery of a radically immanent, non-intentional subjective body, he spoke of a "double use of signs." We say "see" of the eye, an organ of the objective body, and of our invisible subjective vision, which alone lets us know what "seeing" means; so the double use derives if not from an illusion at least from a "borrowing." The formation of the sign "see" in the intentional consciousness of language presupposes, far from explaining, vision's original revelation in life. For life, speaking of itself therefore means two things: signifying itself in the many predications it can form, from itself — its effort of grasping, its suffering; and so, from much further away, "de ce lieu sans nom où la vie donne la souffrance à elle-même et à chacun son propre Soi" — where it is life that speaks and says nothing but its ineffable coming into itself (p. 194). Biran's "double emploi des signes" is Henry's model for the relation between the two languages: the worldly word "see" borrows its meaning from the lived seeing it cannot show.

The second example matters because it occupied the best minds and formed the core of their culture for more than two millennia. In the Scriptures the two languages cross. On the one hand, a narrative of events in the historical existence of Christ, punctuated by quotation marks that break the fabric of facts: then Christ himself speaks, and, since he defined himself as the Word of God, God himself. Despite their stupefying power, are these words not taken up by the text, meanings carried by words, enclosed in the circle of their principled unreality? How can a word claiming to be God's validate this claim? The question is that of *Paroles du Christ* (2002): how can a text in worldly language authenticate a divine Word?

The Scriptures say we are Sons of God. In saying so, they speak like the world's speech, stating propositions about a reality different from them, an external referent — the condition of Sons of God, which they cannot posit in being. But this referent is what we are, we the living living in Life, generated in its self-generation, self-revealed in our transcendental Self in the self-revelation of this absolute Life, in its Word. In saying "You are the Sons," the Scriptures' worldly speech turns away from itself and points to the place where another speech speaks; it performs the displacement that leads out of its speech to the Word of Life. "Celui qui écoute cette parole des Écritures sait qu'elle dit vrai pour autant que s'auto-écoute en lui la Parole qui l'institue dans la vie" (p. 195). The solution is a phenomenological version of the Reformation's "inner testimony of the Spirit": Scripture is verified not by external evidence but by the self-hearing of life in the reader. Worldly language points beyond itself to what it presupposes.

The hidden reference of the world's language to life's is not limited to these examples. It was at work wherever we looked: in the very possibility of phenomenological method, which could not have taken possession of the object whose essence it wanted to construct if life, giving itself, had not supplied it and so the original elements of its construction; likewise, phenomenology would have no adequate, or analogical, language if the "Logos of Life" (1 John 1) that speaks in every word and language did not entrust it with what it has to say and the primitive mode in which it says it. The world itself, not only as sensible world but as "monde-de-la-vie" in an absolutely fundamental sense, holds its "content" only from life. Colours exist only as pure impressions, so that, in Kandinsky's intuition, "dead matter is living spirit." And action, dissociated from objective worldly processes, is tied and develops exclusively in the immanent pathic corporeity from which it draws its possibility and reality (pp. 195–196). The life-world receives here its Henrian sense: a world of life, whose content is impressions and actions. Kandinsky's formula is the guiding thought of *Voir l'invisible*.

That the Word of Life founds the world's language does not prevent it from differing totally, to the point of escaping it. If, as Levinas repeatedly affirmed, there is an essential Saying irreducible to the said, it is because the speech in which the *logos* says being is not the original speech. The absolute, which has thrown each into himself and holds him in this religious bond, holds him ready for ethical recovery. "« Autrement qu'être » parle la vie" (p. 196). The final sentence answers essay IV's thesis ("otherwise than being means appearing otherwise," p. 101) with a reconciliation: Levinas's Saying (*le Dire*) and his "otherwise than being" are claimed for life. The "religious bond" (*religio*, binding) is the relation of each Self to the Life that holds it, and its ethical consequence is "ressaisissement," a recovery of self — the theme of Henry's late ethics.

## XI. Them in Me: A Phenomenology

What does it mean to treat the experience of others phenomenologically? Phenomenology's originality lies in its object: where other sciences study specific phenomena — physical, chemical, biological, historical, legal, social, economic — phenomenology asks what lets a phenomenon be a phenomenon, pure phenomenality as such. But in twentieth-century phenomenology, in Germany and then France, this central question received a one-sided answer that Henry does not share. The conception of phenomenality guiding these analyses is the one ordinary thought first offers, traditional philosophy's oldest and least critical prejudice: one borrowed from the appearing of worldly objects and finally from the world's appearing. The lecture was given at an international psychiatry colloquium in Porto in September 2001, three months after essay VII, and shares its framing and its closing reflection on medicine. It is the volume's only text devoted to intersubjectivity, the theme of the last part of *Incarnation* and of *Phénoménologie matérielle*'s final chapter, "Pathos-avec."

In Husserl, phenomenality is traditionally understood from consciousness, but consciousness is essentially intentional, nothing but the movement by which it throws itself outside, so that phenomenality arises in and through this coming-outside. To reveal in such a coming-outside, by setting at a distance, is to make visible; the possibility of vision lies in the distancing of what is placed before seeing. Essay III (p. 61) in two sentences.

Heidegger brought the world's appearing to its highest elaboration. From § 7 of *Sein und Zeit* the phenomenon is understood in the Greek way, from the root *pha-*, *phōs*, light, so that appearing means coming into the light, "that within which something can become manifest, visible in itself" (*das, worin etwas offenbar, an ihm selbst sichtbar werden kann*, p. 28). The world is the ek-static horizon of visibilization, and the second division explicitly identifies it with exteriority, temporality being "the original 'outside itself' in and for itself" (p. 329) (p. 198). The repetitions of essay III (p. 62) are close to verbatim; Henry reuses his standard exposition before an audience new to it.

Three decisive traits belong to the world's appearing. First, since it consists in the outside-itself, the coming-outside of an Outside, everything shown in it is shown outside, as exterior, other, different. Second, the appearing that unveils in the world's Difference not only makes different what it unveils but is in principle totally indifferent to it: like the light of Scripture that shines on the just and the unjust, it lights everything "sans faire acception des choses ou des personnes, dans une neutralité terrifiante" — victims and executioners, acts of charity and genocides, rules, exceptions and exactions, wind, water, earth, all standing before us alike in the ultimate mode we express by "it is," "there is." Third, this indifference, which makes the world's appearing anything but a Father to his Sons, a brother to his brothers, a friend to his friends, hides a more radical poverty: it cannot confer existence on what it unveils. This incapacity explains the indifference; indifference and neutrality mean impotence. Heidegger, who first thought the world as pure appearing, recognized both the indifference (anguish, in which everything becomes indifferent) and the impotence: unveiling unveils, discovers, "opens," but does not create (*macht nicht*, *öffnet*). Thus appears "l'indigence ontologique de l'apparaître du monde, incapable de poser par lui-même la réalité" (pp. 198–199). The three traits of essay III (pp. 62–63) take on a new weight in a lecture on others: the "Father," "brother" and "friend" that world-appearing is not are precisely the relations at stake in intersubjectivity. An appearing that is indifferent cannot ground any bond.

Life's revelation is opposed point for point. The world unveils in the outside-itself, so everything it unveils is exterior, other, different; the first decisive trait of life's revelation, which bears no gap and never differs from itself, is that it reveals only itself. Life reveals itself; it is self-revelation. This means, first, that life performs the work of revelation and is "tout sauf un processus anonyme et aveugle"; second, that what it reveals is itself. The revelation of life and what is revealed in it are one. "Anonymous" anticipates the critique of the "one" and of anonymous intersubjectivity below.

Wherever there is life this extraordinary situation is found, even in the humblest pain. Since a pain is ordinarily taken as "physical pain" referred to a part of the objective body, perform the reduction that keeps only its painful character, the purely affective element. This "pure" suffering "reveals itself to itself": only suffering lets us know what suffering is, and what is revealed is suffering. That the world's outside-itself is absent is shown by the absence of any gap between suffering and itself; driven back against itself, crushed under its own weight, it cannot establish any retreat or line of escape. Without any inner gap, no gaze can be directed at it. "Personne n'a jamais vu sa souffrance, son angoisse ou sa joie." Suffering, like every modality of life, is invisible — which designates not unreality, illusion or some fantasmatic back-world, but its opposite, reality (pp. 199–200). As in essays III, VII and IX.

Two different and heterogeneous modes of appearing have thus been distinguished. When philosophical reflection meets an essential question, its first theme must be the fundamental phenomenological presupposition that serves as support and final possibility for the reality in question, since the conception of any reality changes entirely depending on whether its support is the world's ek-static appearing or life's self-revelation. Take the body: in the world it is an external body, the body-object of common sense, philosophical tradition and science; in life it is a subjective body constituted by the totality of our impressions and powers, themselves subjectively experienced and deployed. In passing, the experience of the other is established on the plane of flesh, not only of objective bodies — as is the case of relations with others in general, whose original understanding refers to life's self-revelation, not to the world's appearing (p. 200). The methodological rule (first ask what mode of appearing a reality depends on) frames the thesis: intersubjectivity is a relation of flesh to flesh, not of bodies seen. The empathy problem (how do I perceive the other's body as animated?) is displaced.

A coherent, systematic problematic of the experience of others referring its final possibility to life's self-revelation would first have to show why the phenomenologies of the world failed to account for it, though they made it their warhorse. That critique cannot be given here. Henry limits himself to one major objection: since the experience of others is nothing but a relation among a plurality of selves, the inner possibility of each self — the ipseity that is its essence — must be established first. And this ipseity cannot be built in the world's exteriority (pp. 200–201). The full critique (of Husserl's fifth *Cartesian Meditation*, Heidegger's *Mitsein*, Sartre's look) is in *Phénoménologie matérielle* ("Pathos-avec"). The single objection is prior to all: an account of relations among selves needs an account of selves.

The self is a relation to itself. In exteriority every relation is a "relation to," to something external, even a relation between two terms external to each other. In exteriority no relation to self is possible, as the analysis of the world's appearing showed. How such a self-relation, constitutive of every Self, is conceivable only in life and occurs only in it can, on the contrary, be shown. Living is "experiencing oneself," as the phenomenology of pain, of "pure suffering," recognized. In itself suffering experiences itself without reference to anything else. But this "self-experiencing" has nothing to do with "la tautologie morte du A = A du sujet formel et vide de l'idéalisme allemand," nor with Husserl's "I am I." For suffering, experiencing oneself means coming into oneself, being loaded with one's own content, undergoing oneself, suffering oneself in a suffering "plus fort que toute liberté" (p. 201). Fichte's I = I and Husserl's "Ich bin Ich" (cited in essay III, p. 68) are dismissed as formal self-identities. Self-relation is not identity but undergoing.

This coming into itself is not effected by suffering in its particular content: it occurs equally in pleasure, satisfaction, joy or "envie" (as in essay VII, p. 147, "ennui" is perhaps intended). In each modality, and making each possible, it is life that comes into itself. Life is a process, the eternal process in which, coming into itself, it takes hold of itself, grows from itself, experiences and enjoys itself — a radically immanent movement moving in itself, in which a life gives and reveals itself to itself without ever leaving or shedding itself. In this immanent coming of life into its self-experience an Ipseity is built, without which no self-experience is possible. Since this is no concept of life but a real, phenomenologically actual life, the Ipseity is also actual: a real Self, co-belonging in principle to every actual life as its innermost possibility. "Pas de vie sans un Soi vivant, en lequel cette vie s'éprouve elle-même, mais pas de Soi sinon dans la vie qui l'engendre comme ce en quoi elle devient la vie" (pp. 201–202). The repeated doctrine (essays III, VII) here serves as the first premise of the theory of intersubjectivity.

Thus the first condition of any experience of others is laid down — if such experience is always that of one self of another self like it, an *alter ego*, and if every self, as self-relation, is possible only in the accomplishment of this radically immanent process of life, in its generation by it. The condition is stated: no "alter ego" without an "ego," and no ego except as generated in life.

Twentieth-century phenomenologies of the world often speak of a plurality of selves. But the self is always taken for granted, in a pre-philosophical naivety that never asks about its specific phenomenological possibility, ipseity as such, which is constantly presupposed or rather immediately misunderstood. In Husserl, where the ego functions as the source-point of intentionality, its essence is reduced to that of intentionality, the outside-itself. In *Sein und Zeit*, Dasein is said to be "always mine" (*je meines*); when considered, however, the essence of this "mineness," ipseity, is reduced to the process of self-exteriorization in which Dasein finds itself delivered to the world to die (p. 202). Henry's criticism of *Jemeinigkeit* (§ 9) is that Heidegger names mineness but grounds it in being-toward-death, an ek-static structure: Dasein is individuated by its ownmost possibility, which is a horizon.

Behind these interpretations lies a ruinous confusion that dominates Western thought: between the ipseity of the Self and the individuality of a thing. A thing is individualized in the world's exteriority, according to its phenomenological categories — space, time, the idea of the thing — categories legible on any identity card: born on…, at…, son of such a man and such a woman… Here, as in the positivist ideology triumphing before our eyes, man is only a thing, at best a biological process. The identity card is an apt image: it individuates by worldly coordinates (place, time, parentage), i.e. by the categories of exteriority. It fixes the person as an individual among others, not as a self. The *principium individuationis* of essay II (p. 51) — owing nothing to exteriority — is the counter-thesis.

Back to essentials. If the Self relates to itself by experiencing itself in life's absolute immanence and there alone, phenomenology still asks what the phenomenological reality of this original mode of phenomenalization consists in, the pure phenomenological matter of this original self-revelation. It is a transcendental Affectivity that precedes and makes possible every feeling, whose original phenomenological tonalities are suffering and enjoying, in which life's self-experience, and so every possible Self, is accomplished. "La Vie est un pathos. Tout moi est un rapport pathétique immanent à soi-même" (p. 203). The definition of the self as an immanent pathic self-relation prepares the next step: relations among selves will be affective.

If so, every relation among selves is ultimately an affective relation, whose laws have their principle not in the course of worldly events but in the original phenomenological tonalities of suffering and enjoying in which self-experience is accomplished. These relations decline the affective potentialities inscribed in this original suffering and enjoying, and so display an affective dichotomy, an apparently mysterious division between malaise and satisfaction, unpleasant and pleasant, pain and pleasure, suffering and joy, despair and beatitude. Henry's social phenomenology is affective: love, hatred, resentment, sympathy are modes of the same polarity that governs individual life (essay VII). The thesis is the basis of "Pathos-avec."

But even granting that relations among humans necessarily involve living transcendental Selves marked by life's decisive traits — ipseity and affectivity — the hardest question remains: the possibility of the relation itself. How can each Self go out of itself to meet others? How is it with them, or how are they in it? Does going out of oneself not mean opening to the world so as to be with them in it and, as it were, through it? Thus twentieth-century phenomenology sought in the world the possibility for each Self to escape its solitude — in the world and its exteriority, where no Self is possible. The irony is sharp: phenomenology looked for the bond between selves in the very medium that makes selves impossible.

If each Self draws its possibility from life and its actual reality lies there — its own as well as all the others' — then the relation is accomplished in life, and the life that is the condition of each Self is also that of the relation. But which life? In which life is the Self given to itself if it must be able to find all other Selves in it? The question leads to absolute Life, as in essay III.

Examine again the self that I am. "I am I" (*Ich bin Ich*), says Husserl in a manuscript of the 1930s. One cannot stop at this observation, important as it is. I am myself, but it is not I who gave myself to myself; my life experiences itself, but it did not bring itself into the condition of experiencing itself, which depends neither on its power nor its will. That is why my life, the ipseity of this singular self, each of my impressions, are given to themselves in a radical passivity that nonetheless confers on them "cette condition merveilleuse de s'éprouver soi-même et de jouir de soi, de vivre" (p. 204). The argument repeats essay III (p. 68). Passivity is called "marvellous": the givenness that limits the self is the gift that makes it live.

If not I, nor my own life, what life has the power to give each life to itself, each Self to itself, making it a living transcendental Self? Only a Life able to bring itself into itself, carrying the power to live, can give life to whatever lives. In this absolute Life's self-givenness every finite life is given to itself, each Self joined to itself to be forever the Self it is. The regress to absolute Life, as before.

Why does absolute Life confer on the living one an irreducibly singular Self, if not because in its original coming into itself it generates the First Self in which, experiencing itself, it becomes Life? Because the process of absolute Life's self-generation as generation within it of a First Self is repeated in every living one, every living one also bears the Self without which no life, no living one, is possible. The "repetition" of the generation of the First Self in each living one is the phenomenological version of the generation of the many sons in the one Son (Romans 8:29; essay III, p. 68).

Pushed to its limit, phenomenological analysis of the Self leads back to a "Before the Self," to what stands in it before it: its coming into its condition, its immanent generation in absolute Life, whose process is repeated in it as it comes into itself. Then "la condition transcendantale de possibilité de tout Soi et de tout moi concevable, à savoir sa génération immanente dans la Vie absolue, se révèle être identiquement la condition de possibilité transcendantale de la relation de chaque Soi à tous les autres, la condition de l'expérience d'autrui" (p. 204). To understand such a relation one must never start from an autonomous Self or self. So considered, the Self is only a monad, each enclosed in itself, experiencing its impressions and emotions as given to it alone — "what is given to one alone," in psychology's language — and unable to reach another. Only by reasoning from analogy, of which Husserl's "appresentation" is merely a subtle disguise, could it imagine what the other feels, analogous to what it feels in similar circumstances. But experience of the other cannot be reduced to a "constitution" in Husserl's sense, a play of intentionalities whose correlate is only an unreal noematic meaning. If it forms the fabric of our life and belongs to it, the experience of the other must be a real experience. "L'autre doit « être en nous »" (pp. 204–205). This is the essay's central thesis. The identity of the condition of selfhood and the condition of relation is what solves the problem of solipsism: each self is given to itself in the same Life in which every other is given. The charge against the fifth *Cartesian Meditation* — that appresentation (pairing, analogical apperception) is reasoning by analogy in disguise — is the standard objection to Husserl, but Henry gives it a new ground: whatever is constituted intentionally is unreal, so the other constituted as noema is not the real other. "The other must be in us" gives the title its sense.

How? In Life's self-givenness each Self is given to itself; in its self-revelation it is revealed to itself; in it, in this knowledge that is life's, it knows what it is. But the other Self is in the same situation: revealed to itself in life's self-revelation, it too is in life before being in itself. Thus both are in life and know it before being and knowing themselves. As life leads each Self to itself, a Self that is itself only in life, so this same life leads it to the other, who is himself in himself only in this one and the same Life. They are thus originally with one another, being first both in the same life and so in it one in the other, in a kind of "intériorité phénoménologique réciproque" whose site is the "Before" that precedes both and yet remains in them as long as they are living Selves (p. 205). "In life before being in itself" is the decisive formula: the relation to the other is older than the relation to oneself, because both are grounded in a prior givenness. The "reciprocal phenomenological interiority" applies to selves the formula used for Life and the Living (essay X, p. 188).

Thus every conceivable community arises and is formed in its original phenomenological possibility, with certain essential features. The first concerns what is common in it, its content: transcendental life. Such content is therefore not originally (and so not necessarily) "rational." Reason, as usually understood, does not originally gather; it isolates as well. Rightly it has been said that the madman is one who has lost everything except his reason. Not only individually can reason prove destructive: today we can measure how far reason left to itself, to pure objectivism, to the calculating abstraction of modern technology, can strike man at the heart of what is most his own, threaten his "humanity," and lead all humanity to ruin. The content of every community is in reality everything that belongs to life and finds its possibility in it. Suffering, joy, desire or love, even resentment or hatred, carry a gathering power infinitely greater than that attributed to "Reason," which has strictly no power to gather, since one cannot deduce from it the existence of a single individual, nothing of what is to be gathered in a "community" (pp. 205–206). The madman who has lost everything but his reason is Chesterton's aphorism (*Orthodoxy*), apt before psychiatrists. Henry's thesis against the rationalist foundation of community (from the Stoic *logos* to Habermas's communicative reason) is that reason cannot generate a single individual, so it cannot ground a community of individuals; affects, even hostile ones, already bind.

Because what is common in every community is life, community has a second essential feature: it is a community of living transcendental Selves, since only in it are such Selves possible and, reciprocally, it is impossible without them — without the primordial Self in which Life comes into itself and which contains the potential, indefinite multiplicity of all possible selves. The "First Self" (the Word) is said to contain all possible selves potentially — the Johannine "in him was life" read as containing every living self.

But the third feature must be recalled: before defining the content of what is common, Life in its original Ipseity constitutes the transcendental possibility of being-in-common as such, of relation as such, of "being-with" in its precedence. Heidegger's *Mitsein* is reclaimed: being-with is not an existential of being-in-the-world but a structure of life.

Because it has its site in life, every community is by essence invisible. Like our life, our Self, our flesh, it has its "appearing in the world," but here again this appearing is only an appearance cut off from reality. Thus, even more than our visible life, Self or flesh, the visible community carries the possibility of pretence and lying. Is it not the place where indifference and all other unavowable feelings are constantly masked by social ritual? The invisibility thesis has a critical edge: the visible forms of community (institutions, rituals) can belie its reality.

Invisible, foreign to the world and its phenomenological categories, space and time, the reality of community opens a domain of paradoxical relations that Kierkegaard brilliantly intuited. A real relation can be established between transcendental Selves who have never seen one another and belong to different epochs. A man's life can be overturned by reading a book from another century by an unknown author. An individual can become the contemporary of an event that occurred two thousand years ago (p. 207). Kierkegaard's "contemporaneity with Christ" (*Philosophical Fragments*, *Practice in Christianity*) is the model: real relation across time, possible because the relation is not in time but in life.

The concept most lacking in Western philosophy is that of immanence. In classical thought it is found thematically only in Spinoza, which makes him an exceptional thinker, but there it still has only a speculative function. Only in a phenomenology of life does immanence gain its revolutionary meaning and full power of intelligibility. On one side, by excluding the outside-itself from life's phenomenality, immanence leads to defining life as pure pathos, the transcendental Affectivity that is immanence's phenomenological reality, its pure phenomenological matter. On the other, it allows the generation of every living one in absolute Life to be grasped as an immanent relation. Thus arises a wholly new concept of relation, removed from the reign of exteriority. The Self's ipseity occurs in Life; it is in Life as Life is in it, in reciprocal phenomenological interiority. Because Life's immanent relation to the Self it generates extends to every living Self, the relation of Selves to one another is itself transformed. Entrusted no longer to the world's exteriority, where every relation is an external relation between external terms, but to life, it holds from life its possibility and its reality: "c'est en elle que je suis originairement avec les autres, c'est en elle qu'ils sont originairement en moi" (p. 207). The paragraph summarizes the volume's philosophical contribution: a new concept of relation — immanent relation — of which the relation of Life to the Self and of Selves to one another are instances. The judgement on Spinoza (essay IX, p. 173) recurs.

In this philosophically new conception of intersubjectivity as the reciprocal interiority of living Selves in life, is the phenomenology of life not quite isolated? But philosophy is not condemned to refer only to texts of a canonical philosophical corpus fixed once for all. Essential intuitions are found elsewhere — in poetry, literature, spiritual, religious or sacred texts. In John's initiatory texts, for instance. Does his famous Prologue express anything but this process of life's self-generation as generation in it of a First Living Self in which it experiences and reveals itself? Is this process, placed wholly under the sign of radical immanence, not reproduced in the generation of the many transcendental Selves we are — their transcendental birth in life — so that it remains in them as they remain in it? So that this immanence of Life in each Self constitutes not only the transcendental possibility of all conceivable Selves but identically that of their reciprocal phenomenological interiority as an immanent relation in life (pp. 207–208)? The appeal to non-canonical sources defends the method of *C'est moi la vérité*: Scripture as a phenomenological witness.

Astonishing, philosophically innovative statements in the rest of John's Gospel repeat the theses of the Prologue, deciphering the immanence of Life's relation to its Word in that of the same Life to all the living. To Philip, Christ says: "Do you not know that I am in the Father and the Father is in me…" And: "That all may be one, as you, Father, are in me and I in you [...] that they may be one as we are one: I in them and you in me" (p. 208; the references printed as John 14:9 and "7, 21–22" are to John 14:10 and 17:21–23). The prayer of John 17 is the scriptural formula of "them in me": the relation of believers to one another is grounded in the reciprocal indwelling of the Father and the Son.

"Others in me…," "Them in me…": no less astonishing is the title of the Porto colloquium of 2001. Is it intelligible anywhere but in a phenomenology of the radical immanence of Life and of the relation instituted in it, between it and all the living, so as to make possible the relation of the living to one another? The essay's title is the colloquium's, and Henry claims it for his doctrine.

Still more remarkable is that this theme was chosen by an eminent association of physicians. Medicine is said not to be a science — that is, not only a science, but more than one. Since Galileo and Descartes, the new science is geometrico-mathematical knowledge of the material universe, which has made dazzling progress in biology and so in medicine. Yet in medicine something else is at work: the intersubjective relation, without which no medicine is conceivable. "Le regard médical est un regard transcendantal irréductible à un simple savoir objectif." Examining an X-ray or any objective datum passes through it to take in a suffering that must be eradicated or made bearable; the one who bears it cannot be excluded from diagnosis or treatment. "Eux en moi, cela est vrai aussi du médecin dans son lien avec ses malades." The phenomenological life of individuals is the ultimate theme of research, theory and therapy. Interdisciplinarity is not a conventional term or pious wish here but the daily work and bread of those, physicians and philosophers — "physician-philosophers" would be better — united by one aim: to restore a sick life to its power and to the happiness of living (pp. 208–209). The conclusion reprises essay VII's (pp. 155–156) almost verbatim, with one addition that applies the essay's thesis: the doctor–patient relation is itself a case of "them in me," a real relation in life that the objective datum serves but does not replace. The volume ends, as it began with the soul, on the singular living self — now grasped in its relation to every other in the one Life that gives each to itself.

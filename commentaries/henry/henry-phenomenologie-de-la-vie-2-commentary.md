# Michel Henry, *Phénoménologie de la vie II: De la subjectivité* — A Commentary

Citations are to the pages of the PUF "Épiméthée" edition (2003). Quotations are given in Henry's French.

Three misreadings beset these essays and are guarded against throughout. First, self-affection is not reflexive self-consciousness: it is not a consciousness *of* oneself, not introspection, not a doubling of the subject before itself, but the immediate undergoing of oneself that every reflection presupposes and cannot reach. Second, immanence is not solipsistic interiority or a private "inner space" set against an outer one; it names a mode of appearing (appearing without distance) and not a region of being. Third, Henry's "affect" is not an empirical emotion that psychology could catalogue; it is the phenomenological matter of appearing itself, the way life is given to itself. A fourth trap is specific to the polemical chapters: Henry's critique of Kant, Husserl, Heidegger and Freud is not a reproach that they missed some particular fact, but a single thesis applied to each — that they identify phenomenality with the distance of representation, ek-stasis or intentionality, and so cannot think the self-givenness on which that distance rests.

## Foreword (Jean-Luc Marion)

Marion opens with Henry's death on 3 July 2002, which delivers him to a "double éternité": that of the Christian he was from the start and that of his work, as the philosopher he was through and through. Henceforth we have access only to the work (p. 7). The pairing is not a pious flourish. It anticipates the late Christian trilogy (*C'est moi la vérité*, *Incarnation*, *Paroles du Christ*), in which Henry identified life's self-affection with the Johannine Life, so that the "two eternities" name one conviction seen from faith and from philosophy.

The work must stay available, or become so, as completely as possible. The Épiméthée series will keep reissuing the major titles it published from the outset: *L'essence de la manifestation* (1963), *Philosophie et phénoménologie du corps* (1965), *Généalogie de la psychanalyse* (1985) and *Phénoménologie matérielle* (1990); PUF's Quadrige series has already taken over *La Barbarie* (1987) and will soon add the Kandinsky essay, *Voir l'invisible* (1988) (p. 7). Marion writes as director of the series. The list sketches the canonical spine of Henry's oeuvre — the founding treatise, the Biran book, the Freud book, the methodological essays — against which the collected papers are to be read.

Conservation of the books does not suffice, since a striking number of texts from every period lie scattered in journals, collective volumes and conference proceedings, as free interviews or formal lectures. Their importance "ne saurait être surestimée." They reveal that behind the self-referential appearance of an unbroken totality that marks the major works, Henry's inventiveness in fact developed through close debates and countless privileged encounters, "sur un mode étonnamment dialogique" (p. 7). This surprised those who had stopped at a superficial understanding of self-affection. Self-affection "n'avait rien d'un autisme": it was always exercised through and in a deep affection, in both senses of the word at once — the affection by oneself let itself be affected by all the other self-affected consciousnesses that friendship drew to it. Marion quotes Henry's own definition of community from *Phénoménologie matérielle*: "la communauté est une nappe affective souterraine et [...] chacun y boit la même eau à cette source et à ce puits qu'il est lui-même." So concepts, painting, music, politics, economics, psychoanalysis and theology kept provoking "nouvelles figures de l'immanence" (p. 8). This is the editor's answer to the standard objection that Henry's immanence is solipsistic — the misreading this commentary guards against throughout. Marion's argument is double: biographical (Henry's work grew in dialogue) and doctrinal (self-affection is the ground of community, since each living self draws on the one Life it does not give itself). The present volume bears him out formally: every essay is a reading of another thinker — Descartes, Kant, Husserl, Heidegger, Schopenhauer, Nietzsche, Freud, Ricoeur.

Making the essentials of these texts available is therefore indispensable. With Anne Henry's help and approval, Paul Audi agreed to gather and select them with discernment and scruple, not aiming at exhaustiveness, at least at first, which would have blurred the reading more than clarified it. From this difficult work came four collections published under the common title *Phénoménologie de la vie* — the two terms "se rendant mutuellement problématiques et chacun, tour à tour, déterminant l'autre": I. *De la phénoménologie*, II. *De la subjectivité*, then III. *De l'art et du politique*, IV. *Du christianisme* (p. 8). The gloss on the title is exact: phenomenology must be redefined by life (its object is no longer the ecstatic phenomenon but life's self-revelation), and life must be understood phenomenologically (not biologically, as a mode of appearing). Footnotes add that Épiméthée is also publishing Henry's 1943 *diplôme d'études supérieures*, *Le bonheur de Spinoza*, edited by J.-M. Longneaux, and thank B. Stiegler and R. Calin for revising the notes and references of this second volume. The Spinoza thesis matters to the present volume's themes: Henry's first work already sought a beatitude immanent to life.

These texts are all decisive, Marion concludes, either announcing the fundamental works *in nuce* or sealing crucial consequences and recapitulations of them. Only with this reading can an overall appraisal begin — the reception of a work that belongs beyond doubt to the history of phenomenology "parce qu'elle en recèle une possibilité encore à peine aperçue" (p. 8). The final clause states Marion's own estimate of Henry: not a marginal mystic of interiority but the bearer of a possibility internal to phenomenology itself — the possibility, argued in Chapters II and V of this volume, that phenomenality has a second, non-ecstatic mode. Marion's own principle "the more reduction, the more givenness," which Henry adopts in Chapter V, is the point where their two projects meet.

## I. The Critique of the Subject

Henry opens by registering the dominant consensus: the critique of the subject passes for the main achievement of late twentieth-century thought and much of the earlier half. He does not survey its forms but names two roots, one philosophical (Heidegger) and one extra- or para-philosophical (the human sciences, chiefly Marxism and Freudianism, crowned by structuralism and linguistics). The pairing matters: it lets Henry treat a fundamental ontology and a set of positive sciences as variants of one gesture, which the essay will show rests on one unexamined concept of appearing.

The second paragraph locates the convergence. Whatever their differences in aim and level of reflection, all these approaches end at the same point, the critique of the subject understood as "l'homme conçu comme une réalité spécifique et autonome" (p. 9). Henry immediately signals that "specific" and "autonomous" must be heard in the sense the philosophy of the subject gives them, not in a commonsense one; this qualification governs everything that follows.

That sense is now fixed. Identified with the subject — Henry flags the passive construction as provisional, deferring the question of who performs the identification — man is not merely a superior entity homogeneous with others. He receives an "exorbitant privilege": there is being only "par rapport à lui, pour lui et par lui" (p. 10), since he is the a priori condition of possibility of all experience. The subject of modern philosophy is thus a transcendental function, not a large thing among things; the critics, as Henry will argue, attack it while mistaking which of these it is.

From this privilege follows the picture of man as a "super-entity" to which all beings have entrusted their being. The subject does not dispose of that being arbitrarily — which would leave room to respect or fear it — but as what is subjected to it in principle, by its ontological condition: each being becomes an *ob-ject* "dont l'être est le sujet" (p. 10). The hyphen in *ob-jet* marks the etymology Henry relies on: the object is what is thrown before, and its objecthood is the subject's doing.

Henry declines to rehearse the well-known descriptions of this situation except to add one remark: they are descriptions of our world, of the devastation of the Earth by Technology. Technology's principle is the unconditional subjection of the whole of beings, become Object, to man become Subject — the Object "dis-posé devant lui et par lui, disponible donc" (p. 10), liable like a serf to the new lord. The vocabulary is Heidegger's (*Gestell*, standing-reserve) and is cited only to be turned against him a few pages later.

He then sets aside a question that this picture provokes: how could an illusion — man taking himself for Subject and master — have the power to determine things in their actual reality and confer being on everything? More urgent problems take precedence. The question is not abandoned but suspended; it returns when Henry argues that for Heidegger the "error" of the subject is the work of being itself.

The common trait of all the critiques is now stated. It is not that they credit the illusion of the subject with power to change the face of the Earth — that "illusory conception of illusion" is Heidegger's alone, while the other critics hold the subject to be an illusion without effects, or with merely "ideological" ones. The common trait is more serious: they know nothing of the being of the subject they dismember, or wholly misunderstand it. Hence two questions that structure the essay: what is the being of the subject to be "evacuated," and who, contesting both the right and the existence of this subject, carries out the elimination?

Henry answers by returning to the two thinkers who gave "subject" a rigorous sense, Descartes and Kant, the greatest and most decisive, such that any critique not standing in the light of the *Meditations* and the *Critique of Pure Reason* "serait vide de sens" (p. 11). He begins with Kant, reversing chronological order for a reason he gives at once.

The reason is a conceptual paradox. It is precisely in Kant, who refers the being of every entity to the Subject, that the Subject becomes the object of a radical contestation denying it any possible being. At the very moment philosophy understands itself as philosophy of the subject, the foundation it explicitly elucidates escapes it and "bascule dans le vide de l'inanité" (p. 11). The philosophy of the subject is its own first critic — a thesis that removes from contemporary critiques any claim to originality.

The image of the Analytic's rich developments vanishing into the desert of the Dialectic "tel un oued soudain privé de force" (p. 11) makes the point concrete. The reversal of positive into negative occurs exactly when the being of the subject is in question: whether it exists and what it is. The critique of the paralogisms is a radical critique of that being; whatever one affirms of it involves a paralogism, and if one must speak of it at all, it is only "une représentation intellectuelle."

Henry draws the consequence. "I think" amounts to "I represent to myself that I think": the being of the subject is assimilated to the object of a representation. But such an object presupposes the subject and, as represented, never contains reality — just as representing a thaler does not put one in one's pocket. The foundation of all conceivable being is struck with ontological indigence. Kant does not eliminate the subject, as "les tartarins d'aujourd'hui" do, but reduces it to "une simple proposition" (p. 12) that we may utter without being given any reason. The allusion to the hundred thalers ties the indigence of the subject to the Kantian thesis that being is not a real predicate: what representation gives is never reality.

Returning to his two questions, Henry identifies the subject thrown out of existence as the subject of representation, and declares "subject" and "representation" tautological. The subject is not something that additionally has the faculty of representing; it is nothing but representation itself, "le pur fait de poser devant en tant que l'ouverture d'un Dehors, lequel est le monde comme tel" (p. 12). Hence the subject is opposed not to the object but to the entity: it is what makes the entity an ob-ject. Henry's formula — "La subjectivité du sujet, dans la philosophie du sujet, est objectivité de l'objet" (p. 12) — is confirmed by the fact that Kant's analysis of the subject's structures is an analysis of the structures of objectivity (space, time, causality). This identification is the hinge of the whole volume: the classical subject is a name for the outside.

Why does the subject unfold this outside? So that the entity may show itself and be something rather than nothing — so that it becomes a phenomenon. Representation is "l'essence de la phénoménalité." Henry lines up Kant's equivalents — I think, pure manifestation, pure consciousness, pure experience, representation — and concludes that if experience confers being, representation is the essence of being. The chain of identities is meant to show that Kant's thesis is not a theory of a mental faculty but an ontology of appearing.

So the subject expelled from being is the essence of being itself understood as the structure of representation. And the one who expels it is the subject itself, as the whole *Critique* shows: because the subject draws its essence from representation, whose structure is intuition plus concept, and because we have neither intuition nor concept of the I think, we can know nothing of it; it is not and cannot be a phenomenon for us. The philosophy of the subject deconstructs itself — "une autodéconstruction, une autodestruction" (p. 13) — by applying its own presuppositions to the essence of the subject. Henry's answer to "by whom?" is therefore: by representation, applied to itself.

This brief account of the self-destruction has one decisive consequence: the essence of the subject, that is, of being, cannot consist in representation, since representation does not rest on itself and cannot found itself, and since "être ne veut pas dire être représenté" (p. 13) when what is at stake is an actually existing being. The question becomes whether there is an essence of the subject that does not succumb to its own presuppositions — epistemologically put, whether a philosophy of the subject can think a subject other than representation, whose being does not destroy itself.

Henry turns to Descartes, founder of the philosophy of the subject and, it is said, of modern thought, and isolates two decisive traits. The first is a relentless effort to contest the being of the subject, to shake it and even deny it, an attempt "sans exemple, sans précédent et sans suite" (p. 13). In the first two Meditations what is in question is therefore the being of the subject and, with it, being as such. Any reading that turns the cogito into an entity or super-entity is nonsense: Descartes does not first ask what kind of being the subject is, but whether it is and how — he interrogates the "how" of all possible being, its pure essence. This sets Henry's Descartes against Heidegger's, for whom the cogito installs the subject as a privileged *subiectum*.

The second trait is that founding the being of the subject requires, as an unavoidable condition, putting representation out of play: first everything represented or representable, then the structure of representation itself. I can doubt the represented globally only if representation as such is doubtful. It is the medium of representation, the light in which I see things and eternal truths alike, that is deceptive if what I see in it most evidently — that 2+3=5, or that if I think I must be — can and must be held false. Henry reads the hyperbolic doubt as a doubt about the light of evidence itself, not about its contents; this reading is developed at length in Chapters III and V.

The Cartesian problematic thus presents itself as a reduction: what can still be when all representation is barred, when being is neither the represented nor representation? Henry notes in passing that this bar reproduces the situation of Kant's paralogism. If representation floats in the void when it turns to the being of the subject — a mere form without content, "pas même un concept" — this is because the subject is not by representation but on the ground of representation's "anti-essence," which is the essence of the subject. Kant's failure is thus read as indirect evidence: representation, applied to the subject, finds nothing because the subject is not of its order.

The anti-essence is posited, abruptly but incontestably, in article 26 of *The Passions of the Soul*. Descartes again practises the radical epoché of the world by imagining a sleeper lost in his dream. Everything he represents is illusory, but if in the dream he feels sadness or anguish, "ce sentiment *est* absolument" (p. 15), even though the representation is false. The feeling is not by representation but independently of it — without being placed before, and, if representation is false, on condition of not being so. Article 26 is the textual anchor of Henry's whole Cartesian reading and recurs in Chapters III–V.

How then is the feeling posited? It is not posited at all, if positing means what representation does: an action inscribed in the dehiscence of an Outside and made possible by it. Because feeling is not posited, Descartes calls it a passion, defining its being from the start by an undergoing foreign to all action and, before that, to all exteriority. Henry asks what this undergoing is when it is no longer undergoing something other but undergoing oneself, and answers: feeling undergoes its own being "dans son affectivité et par elle" (p. 15). The Cartesian word *passion* is thus given an ontological rather than psychological sense.

If affectivity — undergoing oneself, experiencing oneself immediately and without distance — defines the being of feeling, and so the being of whatever subsists after the reduction, then this affectivity, which Henry now names *pathos*, is the essence of the subject, its subjectivity, and the essence of all possible being. Only when the philosophy of the subject returns to this original essence, as happened for a moment in Descartes, can the "subject" become a genuine philosophical theme. Henry concludes that the century-long critique of the subject has proceeded in near-complete ignorance of what it was talking about.

The most striking error is Heidegger's explicit and repeated identification of "I think" with "I represent." Henry grants that a great thinker may interpret predecessors in his own way and that the critique of representation retains eminent value as a critique of representation as such, which governs Kantianism and all modern idealism. But concerning the being of the subject — the essay's theme — Heidegger's problematic, which grounds the entire present-day critique avowedly or not, "n'en a pas moins perdu [...] toute signification philosophique possible" (p. 16).

Henry now questions the critique's pertinence even regarding representation. It does strike the structure of representation in one respect: it contests the right of a subject setting itself up as the Subject to reduce the whole of beings to the condition of ob-ject at its disposal, exploitable to the point of being nothing but the object of exploitation, as in modern technology. The concession is real but limited, and the next unit removes its ground.

The "throw" that sets the entity before the Subject does not occur ex nihilo; it must be possible, it must be. What being allows it? Henry answers: Heidegger's own being — the transcendence of *Being and Time*, the ecstatic dimension of the *Letter on Humanism*, the *Ereignis* (event of appropriation) of the late work — a being whose coming into being consists in the original unfolding of exteriority. Being means appearing, and appearing appears as "l'extériorisation de l'extériorité, Ouverture de l'Ouvert" (p. 16), which is the light of the world. Representation takes place in this opening as one of its modes; its object is the Greek phenomenon. Hence "l'essence de la représentation est l'essence de l'être tel que le comprend Heidegger" (p. 17), and Heidegger's critique of the subject merely repeats what it criticizes. The argument works at the level of the essence of manifestation, not of any particular attitude: Heidegger can distance himself from representation as a historical mode, not from the ek-static appearing that makes it possible.

Henry anticipates the objection that the Greek entity is not in representation. Granted: the Greek does not throw a *Gegenstand* (object) before himself as a Subject he is not; he belongs to the whole of beings and lets them come toward him as what faces him (*Gegenüber*). But in the *Gegenüber* rules the same *gegen* — the "against," the facing — on which representation will rise and which makes the *Gegenstand* possible. The morphological point carries the argument: one prefix names the single structure of distance common to Greek *physis* and modern objectivity.

Heidegger's whole philosophy not only admits this ultimate identity between the essence of being and the essence (not the historical form) of representation; it affirms it. The philosophy of the subject is the metaphysics of representation, which belongs to the history of metaphysics, which is the history of being itself: being sends itself as *physis*, Platonic *idea*, Cartesian *perceptio*, Leibnizian and Kantian representation, Nietzsche's will to power, modern Technology. So it is not the philosophy of the subject that errs but being that leads it into error. The Heideggerian critique thus misses the true being of the subject, which can be thought only "contre la représentation, contre toute Différence" (p. 17), and is doubly absurd: it has nothing to oppose to the subject's being except that same being, and the error it denounces is one of being's own "turns." Here the suspended question from the start is resolved: the illusion of the subject can transform the Earth for Heidegger only because it is being's own sending.

Freud's critique of the subject remains naive but proves more useful, opening new paths. It too repeats its target. The subject Freud criticizes is the subject of representation, which he calls "consciousness," legitimately if representation is the essence of phenomenality and if consciousness means not what is conscious but "la qualité d'être conscient," Freud's *Bewusstheit* (consciousness as a quality). Henry notes that Freud appeals explicitly to the philosophical tradition to define this starting point and quotes him identifying the conscious with the representation present to consciousness and perceived as such, "le seul sens du terme 'conscient'" (p. 18, citing the 1915 *Metapsychology*). The footnote's three references (the *Introductory Lectures*, the *Outline*, the *Metapsychology*) show the definition held constant across Freud's career.

The first consequence: this 1915 text contains the explicit foundation of the philosophy of the unconscious. There is an unconscious because there is the non-represented — memories I am not now thinking of. The unconscious is posited by reference to consciousness understood as representation; non-phenomenality claims to replace phenomenality, understood as the opening of an Outside, in defining the law of being. The subject of "I represent to myself" is expelled from the problematic because there are many things in its being that it does not represent.

Henry corrects himself: the subject is not excluded but maintained. It is because I understand my being as "I represent to myself" — and so as "I represent to myself what I am" — that I must admit I do not represent all I am, that my consciousness is not coextensive with my being, that I am not master in my own house. The philosophy of the unconscious is a sequel of the metaphysics of representation and belongs to it. Freud's famous humiliation of the ego presupposes the very ego-concept it humiliates.

The second remark makes this plainer. What is not conscious, being unrepresented, can become conscious, being representable — childhood memories, for instance. The unconscious designates only the virtuality of what, actualized, would be representation. It does not oppose representation but designates it as the law of all that is; it happens only that, under this law, almost everything escapes the subject and is "unconscious." The Freudian unconscious is thus the shadow cast by a narrow concept of phenomenality, not a refutation of phenomenality.

A conception of analytic therapy rests on this metaphysics: becoming conscious, bringing into actual representation a non-conscious secretly homogeneous with it — an unconscious made of "unconscious representations," not yet represented and ontologically, if not existentially, asking only to be. Henry's verdict is ironic: "La psychanalyse est appelée de tous ses vœux par la pensée classique" (p. 19). The theme is developed in *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*, of which this paragraph is a compressed restatement.

A different subject rises once the unrepresented is also unrepresentable, once the original being of the subject is the anti-essence of representation. Descartes glimpsed it, and Freud runs into it when facing an unconscious that is no longer provisional, no longer a phase in the history of representation. The history of our representations refers to a force that permits or forbids their actualization, and this force is irreducible to representation: it "s'écrase contre soi" (p. 19) in so radical an immediacy, so submerged by itself, that it leaves no room for any Difference or distance in which it could perceive or represent itself. The capitalized Difference is Henry's general name for the distance of ek-stasis, aimed at both Heidegger and Derrida.

The experience of our body should suffice to show that force escapes representation. The original body with which we do everything, the body that is an "I Can" (*Je Peux*), is not represented; it can act only on condition of not being so, never separated from itself or its powers. Everything we do, we do outside the light of consciousness, in a kind of hypnosis — if hypnosis means absence of representative consciousness. But this hypnosis is not an unconscious: it is "l'immédiation de ce qui s'atteint soi-même hors représentation," an original embrace that is the first phenomenological given, "le pathos par lequel l'Être est sans représentation" (p. 20). The appeal to the body draws on Henry's Maine de Biran book (*Philosophie et phénoménologie du corps*), and the reference to hypnosis anticipates the reading of Freud's early hypnotic practice in *Généalogie*.

At the bottom of the Freudian unconscious one finds affect, which Freud says is never unconscious. Henry radicalizes: affect is not only never unconscious but defines a kind of absolute consciousness and absolute certainty, since feeling experiences itself at every point of its being, being wholly what it is just as it experiences itself. At consciousness's blind spot, where the philosophy of consciousness stalled, the critique of the subject reverses into "une subjectivité absolue" (p. 20) from which the question of the subject must be posed anew. The Cartesian structure is preserved: affect has the certainty the cogito sought, but without evidence.

Psychoanalytic therapy unknowingly rests on this absolute consciousness of the unconscious. The cure is not a becoming-conscious or a recovery of memories, which are unverifiable, inefficacious as representations, and wholly dependent on drives and affects; one must act on the latter to make the former emerge. The cure deals only apparently with representations. It seeks the unconscious where it is and as it is, as force and affect, organizing the repetition of the traumatic transference as force actually acting and affect actually felt. In their immanent actuality "c'est l'inconscient lui-même qui est là, comme l'absolument conscient" (p. 21). Henry names this an "Arch-Revelation" (*Archi-Révélation*) of life attested in every gesture and passion, which must be grasped as constitutive of life's essence — "comme ce qu'on ne voit jamais." The subject must be understood from it. The term marks the shift from Freud to Henry's own phenomenology of life, where revelation precedes and grounds every showing.

At the very moment Freud uncovers the most original dimension of being — unrepresentable force directing representation, affect as the phenomenological given of that force — he succumbs again to the presuppositions of the metaphysics of representation: because they cannot be represented, force and affect again become "the unconscious." Freud's discovery and his misinterpretation of it occur in one movement.

The subject is thus led back to its true being — being that means appearing and is exhausted in it, as Nietzsche says when he asks what he could say of any being but the attributes of its appearance — only to be immediately stripped of that being and of what gives its concept sense. Henry defines the subject without remainder: "ce qui, faisant apparaître l'apparaître, fait du même coup être tout ce qui est" (p. 21). The Nietzsche citation (*The Gay Science*) anticipates Chapter VIII and legitimates the equation of being and appearing on which Henry's phenomenology rests.

The critique of the critique of the subject does not aim at its "return" as of a past reality tired of being neglected. It has shown that the being of the subject was never recognized; what it announces is not its return "mais sa venue pour la première fois" (p. 21). Henry thereby separates his project from any restoration of humanism or of the classical subject.

One question cannot be avoided: if Descartes glimpsed the subject as the anti-essence of representation, why has nearly all subsequent philosophy, Heidegger last, misread the cogito so grossly? Did the mistake arise in Descartes himself by chance or for essential reasons? The answer will locate the error in philosophy's own procedure.

Philosophy is an approach to reality that habitually takes itself for reality, confusing the process of thought with the process of reality. In the "cogito" one must distinguish the thought-process of the first two Meditations — a series of implications in the form of a text, ending in the evidence that if I think I must be — from what it is about. Taken as evidence, the cogito passes for the completed form of representation: I think means I think that, I think that I think, I represent that I represent. The distinction between the cogito as text and the cogito as reality is the methodological key to Chapters III–V.

As evidence, the cogito is the first truth and the prototype of all truth. Descartes himself, by moving before the end of the Second Meditation into a problematic that seeks to found knowledge and science, without marking the break, is largely responsible for the coming blunders. A malaise hangs over the transcendental theory of knowledge that will rule modern thought: how can the cogito, born of the radical critique of all evidence, itself be an evidence — and a "certain" one on which everything rests? All that remains is to treat the "cogito" as a text and submit it to logical or historical analysis in search of faults or unconscious presuppositions. Henry thus accounts for the whole modern genre of cogito criticism as a consequence of misidentifying the cogito with a proposition.

Taken as reality, the cogito has nothing to do with a thought-process, with thought, or with the text of the Meditations. "Cogito veut tout dire, sauf 'je pense'" (p. 22). It designates, within pure appearing (which Descartes calls thought), what appears immediately to itself. Subjectivity is "l'immédiation pathétique de l'apparaître comme auto-apparaître" (pp. 22–23): without this embrace of appearing in its self-appearing, no appearing — the ek-static appearing of the world included — would ever appear. I see something only by representing it against the ek-stasis of the World, but that ecstatic opening would not appear unless it affected itself in its very ek-stasis. This self-affection of ek-stasis is fundamentally different from its affection by the world: the latter consists in the Difference the former excludes. Descartes' *sentimus nos videre* ("we feel that we see") is intelligible only if, seeing being doubtful, the original feeling in which seeing feels itself contains no seeing. "Le voir n'est — n'apparaît — que sous la condition d'un non-voir" (p. 23). This is the formula of the duality of appearing that runs through Henry's entire oeuvre since *L'essence de la manifestation*, and *sentimus nos videre*, the Cartesian counterpart of the Second Meditation's *videre videor* ("I seem to see"), becomes its emblem.

Descartes recoils before the phenomenality of this non-seeing. Although everywhere — in passion, sensation, seeing — affectivity is named as immediacy and the original essence of subjectivity, he here interprets it as a disturbance introduced into subjectivity by a foreign cause. Why? Because thought is light: the light of representation, of the world, in which things and their geometric figures shine — "la lumière grecque" (p. 23). At its birth the aborted philosophy of the subject carries the flaw that all the critiques of the subject would push to its extreme: absolute objectivism, whether the naive objectivism of the sciences, especially the human sciences, or the ek-stasis of being that unknowingly grounds them. The Greek privilege of light is thus the common root of Descartes' hesitation, Heidegger's ontology and the human sciences.

The lesson of the critique of the subject is that it repeats what it criticizes. Once this repetition is seen, a philosophy of the subject becomes possible. It need neither blush at its past nor turn nostalgically toward it: "elle n'a pas de passé" (p. 23). Its tasks lie ahead of it. The closing sentence restates the earlier claim — the subject's coming for the first time — as a programme, which the next chapter begins to carry out.

## II. Philosophy and Subjectivity

Henry opens with a definition: by subjectivity "nous entendons ce qui s'éprouve soi-même" (p. 25). Not something that additionally has the property of experiencing itself, but the fact of self-experiencing taken in itself. To experience oneself is to appear to oneself in such a way that this appearing is an undergoing (*épreuve*); and since what appears, in subjectivity, is nothing but appearing itself, subjectivity builds itself inwardly only as appearing's appearing to itself. The definition removes the substrate from the start: subjectivity is not a thing that feels but the feeling-itself of appearing. The French *épreuve* carries both "experience" and "ordeal"; Henry exploits the second sense later when self-experience becomes suffering.

I constantly experience the world, the ground under my foot. But the ground does not experience itself; it "n'est rien pour lui et ainsi il n'est rien" (p. 25). Only in me and for me does the world appear. Subjectivity gives me the world at every instant and, if the world is the whole of what is, it is the foundation of all things, the absolute to which they refer. The step from "nothing for itself" to "nothing" is the classical idealist inference, which Henry accepts here only to relocate its foundation in what follows.

Insofar as subjectivity experiences the world, it is not only mute self-affection in which there is nothing for it but itself; there is also the world for it. We experience the world only on the ground of a first self-experience, absolute subjectivity. But why is there a double experience, of the world and of oneself — two affections — and how are they related? Only a radical elucidation of the concept of affection can answer: what "to experience" and "to be affected by" mean in each case, and, since both consist in appearing, what the nature of appearing is. The duality of affections announced here is the essay's culminating thesis (p. 52).

This defines the task of phenomenology. Its object is not the set of phenomena the sciences study but what lets them be phenomena, their mode of givenness — subjectivity. Philosophy thereby gains its proper object as philosophy of subjectivity and is distinguished from all other sciences, especially psychology, which also claims to speak of subjectivity but, treating it as something that is (related to the organism, the external world, the social milieu) rather than as "la condition de tout être possible," misses it in principle (p. 26). Henry's antipsychologism is Husserl's, directed against any treatment of subjectivity as a region of entities.

The interpretation of the ground of all things — *to hypokeimenon*, the underlying — as subjectivity first occurs with Descartes, whose cogito inaugurates modern philosophy. The ancient Sceptics already pointed toward subjectivity when they reduced the "objective" world to relative, shifting subjective modes of givenness, and the effort to defeat scepticism gave ancient thought its drive to found knowledge apodictically, though not yet as subjectivity's self-legitimation. Henry's use of the Greek term prepares his later counter-reading of Heidegger's history of *subiectum* (p. 48).

Descartes' extraordinary discovery in the first two Meditations does not reach its ultimate consequences. Making subjectivity a soul — a reality distinct from body yet ultimately juxtaposed to it — he ceases to treat it exclusively as givenness itself, source of all reality, and reduces it to a fragment of reality. Insofar as he keeps its donative function, as the cogito for which all *cogitata* are, he does not explore it systematically, in his haste to reach scientific knowledge of the world. Subjectivity was recognized in its transcendental meaning without becoming a fully developed theme. The reproach is the opposite of Heidegger's: Descartes errs not by making the subject a foundation but by not dwelling in that foundation long enough.

Such a thematization defines the phenomenological project at its historical start in Husserl. It is achieved by the phenomenological reduction, which aims to "pro-duire" subjectivity as a theme for exhaustive investigation — the hyphen stressing the bringing-forth. In the *Crisis*, Husserl's "testament," the reduction has two movements. The first is the epoché of the Galilean world of science, which Descartes found before him: the view that, despite the relativity of subjective appearances, a true world in itself can be exhibited by setting aside sensible qualities and retaining only the geometrically determinable forms of space-time. A "supra-subjective" nature arises, of which the intuitive world is merely subjective presentation. Henry quotes Husserl on Galileo's abstraction from "les sujets en tant que personnes" (*Crisis* §10, pp. 27–28). The world of spirits, obtained by abstraction from Galilean nature, is not its symmetrical counterpart but founded on it, in corporeality; in every case "la subjectivité est bien l'apparence qu'il s'agit de réduire, et la science est cette réduction" (p. 28).

Husserl's reversal of these theses, which underpin the scientistic ideology of our time, is among the major analyses of philosophy. The world of mathematical idealities cannot account for the subjective-relative world of everyday interests because it rests on it: it is an idealization of the sensible-practical world and presupposes it as ground. Scientific idealizations have sense only with respect to the life-world and presuppose the subjective operation that produces them. Far from reducing subjectivity to appearance, science finds in it its permanent condition of possibility. And the nature on which spirit is built is not the world of science but the life-world, intuitable and experienceable in its sensible subjective givenness. Galileo's illusion was to take the mathematical instrument of knowledge for the real world, "qui n'est qu'éprouvable et intuitionnable dans une subjectivité" (p. 28). Henry's own *La Barbarie* draws its critique of Galilean science from this same analysis.

Subjectivity, then, does not merely produce the idealizations of science; "elle produit d'abord ce monde de la vie lui-même," being nothing but the set of procedures and modalities by which it is offered to us — its fundamental methodology (pp. 28–29). We are always thrown into a pre-given world that contains the totality of what can be for us and is the horizon of every action. But neither beings nor the world are there immediately, like substrates of qualities; they are such only in the changing modes of their subjective appearance, the "how." The immediate is the immediacy of this givenness. Henry endorses the constitutive thesis but quietly sets up the question he will press: in what mode is this givenness itself given?

That we pay no attention to these modes of givenness and usually have no suspicion of them does not affect their tireless operation, which proceeds by synthetically linked modalities. No act of givenness is isolated: perceiving one face of a cube implies past and future perceptions of the others, inner and outer horizons, memories and expectations. Each actual validation of the world presupposes the infinite horizon of implicit validations co-functioning in the flow of absolute subjectivity, forming the context of "un horizon vivant" (p. 29). The univocal world of science is merely a higher-order subjective formation resting on all these. The emphasis on horizons prepares Henry's objection that horizon-structure governs transcendental experience too (pp. 37–38).

These subjective performances remain mostly hidden because natural life, theoretical or practical, goes out toward beings and their ends and loses itself in them in a kind of ecstasy — "égarement," Husserl says — forgetting itself (pp. 29–30). The naive conception of a world in itself, reproduced in science, comes from the natural attitude, in which life is always life in and for the world. Henry's later critique turns on this word "ecstasy": he will argue that Husserl's cure for self-forgetting is itself ecstatic.

Yet it is for life that the world exists, in the totality of performances by which life gives it sense and being. Subjectivity, which is this constitution, does not know it. The first epoché led from the scientific world back to the life-world; the life-world is the correlate of a universality of linked synthetic operations. But this constituting life is not encountered in the natural attitude. A second epoché is needed which, abstracting from our immediate living in the world — our belief in it — takes this "living-in" itself as theme. A conversion of the gaze passes from the life-world to its pregivenness seen for itself, "de l'étant à sa donation" (p. 30). This thematic displacement, the return from the real world to the transcendental subjectivity that constitutes it, is the transcendental reduction proper.

This is a total upheaval in the history of humanity: it discovers a wholly new dimension of being, phenomena hitherto unnoticed yet decisive. These "purely subjective phenomena" are not juxtaposed to the others but ground them, being in them what makes them phenomena — their phenomenality. That this sphere stayed unknown for millennia is surprising, as is the reduction that ends this immemorial night and casts "le fond caché de l'être" into full light (pp. 30–31). The light metaphor is Husserl's triumph and, for Henry, its undoing: what is cast into light is, by that very fact, no longer the ground.

Under what condition? The epoché must be understood as what discloses subjectivity as the *Urregion* (primal region) in which all others are founded. The first epoché remained on the world's terrain. In the natural attitude I perceive the house and am inattentive to my perception; I always have consciousness of the world, never consciousness of my consciousness of it. But there is also a natural reflection, and turning back on consciousness in a thematic aim still treats it as something that is. So with humanity's usual self-apperception, even as spirit: it is only "l'auto-objectivation de la subjectivité transcendantale et sa déchéance au rang d'un étant" (p. 31). Only putting the world and all being out of play, by preventing objectification, preserves subjectivity as the ultimate constituent, "l'ego absolument unique fonctionnant en dernière instance" (*Crisis* §55). Henry stresses that Husserl himself saw the danger of objectifying subjectivity; the question is whether his remedy escapes it.

The discovery thus occurs in a transcendental, not natural, reflection, which frees my constituting life by placing me above it. The category of "above" runs through Husserl's descriptions in a double role. It indicates that the phenomenologist has suspended belief in the world and no longer lives in immediate world-life; this elevation alters lived experience but shows it in its truth, its functioning as giving the world its sense of being. And the phenomenologist sees in subjectivity the world it constitutes, holding it in view as the *cogitatum* of its *cogitatio*. The reduction loses nothing; it opens us to the world considered in its transcendental genesis, passive and active. Henry's account is faithful; the spatial metaphor "above" is what he will contest, since an elevation is a distance.

These themes confront us with a paradox. The subjectivity that constitutes original appearing — the source of all appearing — does not itself appear; it does not reach phenomenality of itself. It needs another principle and a specific method, the reduction. But who performs the reduction, who sees in it what it sees, if not subjectivity itself? If subjectivity, as immediate living in the world, is ignorant of itself, if it has not already brought itself into phenomenality, how could it do so afterwards? In transcendental reflection, one says. But reflection is also a modality of the immediate life of absolute subjectivity and must carry the same impotence: "comment donc pourrait-elle bien s'acquitter de cette tâche après coup ?" (p. 32). The argument is a regress: any method that brings subjectivity to appearance presupposes a subjectivity already self-given, so self-givenness cannot be the method's result.

Reflection in fact doubles the impotence. Not only does it remain, as a subjective modality, blind to itself; what it claims to bring before its gaze cannot be subjectivity at all, since before us, in the light of ek-stasis, there is only the world. Ignorant of subjectivity in itself, reflection manifests only the exteriority of a world. Entrusting the discovery of subjectivity to the reduction is thus "deux fois impossible" (p. 33): first, if reflection repeats subjectivity's incapacity to reveal itself; second, if it seeks subjectivity before it, in a world, where it never is. The two impossibilities organize the rest of the essay, taken in reverse order.

Starting with the second: subjectivity's impotence to reach itself before itself is not easily recognized in Husserl, whose explicit presupposition is the reverse — that subjectivity can seize its own being in the perfect intuition of evidence. Henry cites *Ideas I*: "le regard d'une perception intuitive peut se diriger sur tout vécu réel et vivant en tant que présence originaire" (p. 33). This is the thesis of the principle of all principles applied to the immanent sphere, which Henry will dismantle from within Husserl's own texts.

Husserl conceives philosophy as the genuine science, reaching full self-understanding in an ultimate self-legitimation, which requires a set of absolutely evident data. Hence a problematic of evidence: adequate evidence, where signitive intentions find intuitive fulfilment, and apodictic evidence, whose content can only be what it gives itself as — indubitability. The return from world to subjectivity is motivated not only by constitution but by the fact that subjectivity offers apodicticity "le lieu exclusif de sa réalisation" (pp. 33–34). World-experience is inadequate in principle, an induction always exposed to later discordance and to the conceivable non-being of the world. Immanent perception of lived experience is different: reflection on my experience seizes an absolute whose existence cannot be denied — "Je suis, cette vie est, Je vis : cogito" (*Ideas I*, p. 34). Husserl's claim that immanent perception is apodictic is the target; Henry will show that his own later texts undermine it.

The gaze on this indubitable life is the transcendental inner experience delivered by the reduction. Yet this experience will undergo a critique serious enough to challenge, first, the possibility for reflection, or any gaze, to reach absolute subjectivity in its reality, and second, Husserl's dissociation of transcendent from immanent experience. The privilege of subjectivity as a sphere of absolute, indubitable existence is gravely threatened. The critique Henry means is Husserl's self-critique in the *Cartesian Meditations*, which he exploits as internal evidence.

In the *Cartesian Meditations* the critique of transcendental experience sounds "la portée de l'évidence apodictique" and reaches, in §20, the surprising statement that the possibility of a phenomenology of pure consciousness "semble a priori assez douteuse" (p. 34). Subjective phenomena present themselves as a "Heraclitean flux" without "ultimate elements," which cannot be fixed in concepts as the natural sciences fix theirs. One way out remains: these modes, however fluid, are not arbitrarily variable but bound to "une structure typique" that cannot be broken (*CM* §21, p. 35). Phenomenological inquiry thus slides from the reality of subjective modes to the typology they obey. Eidetics takes over from a mode of givenness that claimed to exhibit the thing itself as absolute and proved incapable of it. The slide is Henry's central diagnosis: the turn to essence compensates for the failure of reflection to reach actual life.

The slide is not occasional; it recurs strikingly in the *Crisis*. §52 says that even the individual philosopher in the epoché cannot fix "ce flux insaisissable de la vie," repeat the same content and secure its *quid* and *quod* in solid statements; yet "la pleine factualité concrète de la subjectivité transcendantale universelle est saisissable scientifiquement en un autre sens," through the eidetic task of investigating the essential form of transcendental performances — "Le *factum* n'est déterminable ici que comme *factum* de sa propre essence" (p. 35). Henry quotes at length because Husserl's own words concede the gap between the fact of life and its eidetic capture.

This inflection toward pure ideal possibilities is characteristic of Husserl and lets him extend the empire of apodicticity and a priori knowledge infinitely even where reality is no longer graspable in itself but only from its essence, as a possible exemplification. But a possibility read off an essence is not a reality; it is ideal and unreal like the essence that governs it. At stake is eidetic evidence's claim to deliver "la pleine factualité concrète" of transcendental subjectivity in person, in the unsurpassable singularity of each of its effectuations. Since singularity belongs to each modality in principle, the full reality of subjectivity escapes a mode of givenness that is no longer the one by which it gives itself to itself. The reduction is performed as an eidetic reduction; it makes phenomenology the a priori science of the pure possibilities of absolute subjectivity, "mais c'est l'essence réelle de celle-ci que manque cet eidétisme" (p. 36). The contrast between ideal essence and "real essence" is Henry's: the essence of life is not a general form but its actual self-affection, which is always singular.

That thematic grasp lets subjectivity's reality escape and can offer only an unreal representation in the form of its ideal essence should have given pause. It should have suggested that the way subjectivity originally reveals itself — its very subjectivity — is not the thematic grasp attempted in the reduction, nor any grasp founded in intuition, evidence, and so ultimately intentionality. With evidence and eidetics in question, Husserl's major presupposition drifts away, and with it the claim to identify the original givenness of subjectivity. Henry passes to his second point: the "paradoxical and ruinous" reduction of transcendental experience, supposedly immanent and indubitable, to an intentional and essentially transcendent experience — which amounts to "la confusion de l'expérience de la subjectivité avec celle du monde" (p. 36).

Before fleeing into eidetics, the critique of transcendental experience had for a moment targeted subjectivity's singular reality. In transcendental experience "l'ego s'atteint lui-même de façon originelle," but only as a living "core," beyond which stretches an obscure horizon including my past, faculties and habitualities. A disturbing analogy arises between transcendental and worldly experience: both consist in a punctual evidence, a "standing-there-before-a-gaze," gnawed at by the horizon of unfulfilled intentions. External perception gives the object itself, "in seinem Selbst da stehen," yet with an open infinity of unperceived possibilities; and, Husserl writes, "d'une manière tout analogue" the apodictic certainty of transcendental experience grasps my "I am" as implying "l'indétermination d'un horizon ouvert" (*CM* §9, p. 37). Husserl's own "analogous" is the textual lever: by his admission, transcendental experience has the structure of perception.

An open horizon of unfulfilled possibilities means inadequacy, which only intuitive fulfilment could remove. But fulfilment is always partial — each fulfilled intention raises the horizon of all those not fulfilled, each intuitive fragment trails "un sillage de non-être" in which this sliver of presence, reduced to punctual evidence and finally to an abstract present, appears derisory (p. 37). Husserl's remark that the *cogitatum* "ne se présente jamais comme définitivement donné" (*CM* §9) means that it enters presence only to withdraw at once. This not only invalidates the reading of Husserl's phenomenology as a "metaphysics of presence" — a direct reply to Derrida — but, since it also characterizes transcendental experience, threatens it with the same discredit as worldly experience, reducing it to a "Heraclitean flux" escapable only through typical structures, its ideal essence. The law of partial fulfilment strikes transcendental experience too; concordance there is no less problematic than in world-experience, and one could imagine with Hume that transcendental life sinks into chaos. Henry's point against Derrida is precise: Husserl's phenomenology is not too much presence but a presence perpetually hollowed by absence, because it is ek-static throughout.

The homogeneity of transcendental and worldly experience becomes evident once the reduction is complete. The phenomenologist's descriptions proceed in two directions: noematic, thematizing the determinations of the intentional object; noetic, considering the modalities of the cogito — perception, retention, memory — that make the object what it is. But the phenomenologist's noetic aim at the *cogitationes* is homogeneous with the relation by which the *cogitationes* refer to their *cogitatum*. Noetic experience repeats noematic structure "parce que la noèse est elle-même le noème de cette expérience" (pp. 38–39). In rigorous reduction we retain, "à titre noématique," the unlimited field of pure conscious life, and correlatively the world-phenomenon (*CM* §15). The formula "the noesis is the noema of this experience" is the essay's sharpest compression: the act becomes visible only by becoming an object.

Transcendental experience is not merely homogeneous with world-experience but identical with it and its very ground, as becomes clear when probed in its ultimate possibility. Reflection is never first or self-sufficient in Husserl; it can grasp transcendental life only because "cette vie est déjà là avant le regard de la réflexion" (p. 39). This being-already-there is self-temporalization: each phase slips into a retained immediate past, making attention's return possible. Temporalization is flowing itself, slipping into the just-past, "la première déhiscence extatique," the opening of an Outside where something can become visible and a gaze turn toward it. Self-temporalization of transcendental life is thus the hatching of the world, the world in its nascent state, and is exhausted in it. Henry locates the first ek-stasis not in perception of objects but in internal time-consciousness itself — the decisive move of *L'essence de la manifestation* against Husserl's immanence.

How does this world differ from the one left behind by the reduction? It is made of subjectivity's own modalities, impressions and intentionalities. The reduction separates the two worlds, as its first formulation in the 1905 *Lectures on Time* shows: it puts out of play transcendent apperceptions that posit, beyond actually heard sound-impressions, objects whose claimed existence exceeds what appears (the object posited as enduring after the sensation stops). What remains are immanent lived experiences — yet in self-temporalization each is already "jeté dans l'ek-stase d'un avant-plan de lumière," appearing as coming, present, just past, further past, so that the modes forming "internal time-consciousness," whose structure is intentionality, "ne nous livrent jamais qu'un objet" (pp. 39–40). The immanent object is still an object: the reduction exchanges one world for another of the same form.

Husserl recognizes and accepts the singular situation that results. The lived experiences of absolute subjectivity are the modes in which worldly beings are given. In self-temporalization these modes are given in turn, by the modes of internal time-consciousness — protentional, retentional and perceptual intentionalities constituting immanent duration. Experiences constituting the world and experiences constituting duration are all self-constituted in this duration "comme dans une première dimension de transcendance, dans ce premier monde qu'est la durée immanente elle-même" (p. 40). The phrase "first world" is the thesis of the paragraph: immanent time is a transcendence.

*Cartesian Meditations* §18 explicitly states that punctual evident grasp of an experience in attention and reflection presupposes a horizon, that of immanent time objectified in self-temporalization. That this first rising of a world is not only homogeneous with experience of transcendent objects but forms its first layer follows from the fact that immanent objects carry the rays of intentionality positing real objects as ideal identical poles of their variations — variations that are these immanent objects. Henry quotes Husserl on the "synthetic unities of constituting evidences" whose ultimate objective foundation is "la vie immanente, c'est-à-dire [...] la vie qui s'écoule et se constitue en soi-même et pour soi-même" (*CM* §29, pp. 40–41). Husserl's word "objective" for the foundational stratum is the admission Henry needs.

Self-temporalization is the self-constitution in which subjectivity constitutes itself in and for itself: it gives itself to itself, so its revelation is a self-revelation — but the task of achieving that self-revelation is entrusted to temporalization, the synthetically unified protentional, perceptual and retentional intentionalities of time-consciousness. In each case intentionality constitutes and makes visible — more exactly, the primitive gap opened by flowing, in which intentionality unfolds. Henry italicizes the conclusion: the power that makes subjectivity visible is "strictement parallèle à celui qui donne à voir le monde, ou plutôt il lui est identique," a constitution in the sense of a making-visible in the ek-stasis of time (p. 41). Husserl entrusted to a longitudinal intentionality running along the flow the task of reflecting its scattered reality back to it, in a self-synthesizing synthesis. Henry quotes *Lectures on Time* §39: the flow is so constructed that a self-appearance (*Selbsterscheinung*) of the flow must occur, and this requires no second flow, since "en tant que phénomène il se constitue lui-même" (p. 41). Henry accepts the self-reference but denies it is self-revelation: a flow appearing to itself through intentionality is still appearing at a distance from itself.

The reduction of subjectivity's essence — its capacity to bring itself into phenomenality — to intentionality runs through the work up to the *Crisis*, where it keeps its categorical tone. Of Descartes' Meditations it is said that the most significant moment, though undeveloped, is "l'intentionnalité qui forme l'essence de la vie égologique," another name for *cogitatio*, having consciousness of something; every *cogitatio* has its *cogitatum* and is in the widest sense a "believing" with some mode of certainty (§20, p. 42). Husserl's whole survey of the history of philosophy, especially in *First Philosophy*, adopts the perspective of intentionality. It motivates his critique of British empiricism, which placed subjectivity at the foundation of knowledge but, failing to see intentionality as the power producing phenomenality, reduced subjective givenness to "une inscription morte sur une table aveugle" (p. 42). Henry and Husserl read Descartes' cogito in opposite directions: for Husserl its essence is intentionality, for Henry its essence is what precedes intentionality.

This reduction shows itself when, in self-temporalization, subjectivity relates intentionally to itself to reveal itself. But in the self-appearance of the flow "le constituant et le constitué coïncident, et pourtant ils ne peuvent naturellement pas coïncider à tous égards"; the constituting phases "ne peuvent pas être identiques à ces phases constituées" (*Lectures* §39, p. 42). If the flow appears to itself only as constituting itself, in its phenomenally constituted phases, and if the constituting phases are given only so, through intentionality, then they are never given as constituting; intentionality never gives itself in its functioning — "ce qui fait voir n'est jamais vu" (p. 43). The formula condenses Henry's entire objection to Husserl and recurs throughout the volume.

What makes visible is subjectivity. That it is never seen, that the principle of all phenomenality never phenomenalizes itself, is the paradox of phenomenology, modestly called the "anonymity" of transcendental life. Phenomenology set itself the task of lifting it, and claims to do so in the reduction. But what the phenomenologist sees is only something constituted, "le champ libre et illimité de la vie pure de la conscience," retained only "noematically"; in its ultimate functioning his own reflection escapes him (p. 43). The anonymity is not a gap to be filled by better method but, as Henry will say (p. 51), the symptom of subjectivity's non-ek-static mode.

In the self-temporalization grounding reflection, the same situation recurs in its extreme form. The ultimate constituting power that constitutes itself does not in truth temporalize itself; it never enters, as temporalizing, the constituted flow of immanent temporality. The manuscripts of the 1930s say of this I "in its most original originality" that it is "not in time," "without extension," "without duration," "non-temporal," "supra-temporal" (p. 43). These are the late manuscripts on the living present, where Husserl himself meets the limit Henry names.

But then it is nothing; phenomenology cannot give it a status. Everything that is for the ego is given in the ek-stasis of intentionality; that is how the world "is." The ego too is only insofar as it is for itself, relating intentionally to itself in self-temporalization; and experience of subjectivity is identical with experience of the world. Both refer to an ultimate naturing instance, the I functioning in the last instance, of which a 1933 manuscript says, essentially: "Je ne suis pas seulement quelque chose pour moi, mais je suis moi (*Ich bin nicht nur etwas für mich sondern ich bin Ich*)" (pp. 43–44) — without being able to say more about this supreme instance, which is subjectivity itself. Likewise the critique of eidetics in certain manuscripts, questioning whether essence can determine the facticity of experiences since essences too are constituted, runs up against the absolute of this supreme *Faktum*. The 1933 sentence is, for Henry, Husserl's brush with self-affection: an ego that is itself without being something for itself.

This insurmountable difficulty affects method and explains why the thematization of subjectivity never takes subjectivity as its starting point. The intentional object always serves as "transcendental guide" for elucidating the syntheses that constitute it and the subjectivity reduced to them. Henry quotes *CM* §21: in describing the most general structure, intentional relation to the world, the intentional object on the *cogitatum* side plays "le rôle d'un guide transcendantal"; "Le point de départ est nécessairement l'objet 'simplement' donné" (p. 44). The doctrine of the object as guiding clue, standard in Husserl, is reinterpreted as a confession that subjectivity can be reached only from what it is not.

This regressive analysis — which one cannot help calling "reflective" — may sometimes seem to start from subjectivity itself, and Husserl expressly allows this (*CM* §21). But subjective modes then serve as guides to deeper layers only by offering themselves as intentional objectivities, immanent experiences self-constituted in self-temporalization. In every case the ascent to the constituting originates in something constituted, and the constituting gives itself only in the retentional relapse of its self-constitution — or else, apart from this "procès d'ontification," it remains a mere presupposition (pp. 44–45). "Ontification" names the process by which givenness is turned into a given; Henry's alternative is a givenness never so turned.

Husserl criticized Kantian idealism deeply, charging that its Copernican revolution — objectivism inverted into transcendental subjectivism — ignored the phenomenological presuppositions that make it possible. Thematizing what remains unthematic in natural experience, the a priori conditions of experience, requires bringing the performances of intuition and understanding into intuition. But Kant knew only empirical intuition of subjectivity in inner sense, which remains subject to those conditions and cannot exhibit them; he must either abandon subjectivity to empirical psychology or reserve it for a reflective metaphysics that cannot claim to be rigorous science. Husserl, however, is in the same position, since constituting subjectivity cannot reveal itself in evidence, where it is always constituted, however eidetic, categorial or a priori that evidence. The vast extension of the concept of intuition "n'a [...] servi à rien" for the question of original subjectivity (p. 45). The *Logical Investigations*' categorial intuition, usually taken as Husserl's decisive advance over Kant, is thereby declared irrelevant to the question that matters.

An involuntary community of destiny thus binds Husserl to Kant. As Husserl takes the "immanent" intentional object as guide for noetic descriptions, so the *Critique* pursues the analysis of transcendental subjectivity as that of the a priori conditions of any object — of objectivity as such. When subjectivity is reduced to the structure of objectivity, it is "rien de plus que l'essence de celle-ci" (pp. 45–46). The conclusion of Chapter I (the subjectivity of the subject is the objectivity of the object) is now shown to hold for Husserl too.

A decisive situation appears. Once thought overcomes the naive opposition of subject and object as two entities — one conscious and related to the other, one unconscious and related to nothing — once subjectivity is no longer the subject but its essence, the essence of experience and phenomenality, and the object no longer the entity but its objective condition, objectivity, the advance into presence as coming-before-us, then the essence of phenomenality, thought here as subjectivity and there as the a priori condition of objects, "est identiquement la même" (p. 46). The Kantian subject is exhausted in the relation to objects of experience, objectivity in its pure possibility; Husserl's subjectivity in intentionality, consciousness of objects, the opening of an Outside that conditions every "constitution." Precisely because subjectivity is nothing but objectivity — the horizon of transcendence in which everything appears as possible ob-ject — analysis of the intentional object can stand for analysis of subjectivity. Henry thus explains the transcendental-guide method by its ontology: it works because there is nothing in subjectivity, so conceived, that is not already in objectivity.

The situation is wholly general; Husserl and Kant are only examples. Throughout modern thought the "subject" is the condition of the object, what brings the entity into being-there-before-us; the subject's being is the object's being-object. But subjectivity designates phenomenality as such, so it is the structure of phenomenality, the essence of manifestation, that is interpreted as condition of the object. Thought through to the end, this condition is described as the ek-stasis of a transcendent and finally as "instance dans l'éclaircie de l'être — *Inständigkeit in der Lichtung*" (standing within the clearing of being). The *Zähringen Seminar* teaches that because philosophy of subjectivity had displaced this standing-in into the immanence of consciousness, it had to be returned to its place; for ek-static standing in the clearing rests on the clearing and presupposes it. Thus the Greeks understood the object not as what a subject throws before itself but as what comes into presence from itself, from a ground that is this self-emergence (pp. 46–47). The 1973 *Zähringen Seminar* (in *Questions IV*) is Henry's chosen text because it states Heidegger's relation to Husserl in his own words.

This critique only resumes the presuppositions of the philosophy of subjectivity and carries them to their final consequences. Heidegger's work, the seminar protocol says, "consista en la recherche des implications radicales de l'intentionnalité"; thinking intentionality through means situating it in Dasein's ek-static being, so that consciousness is "founded in Dasein" (*Questions IV*, p. 322; p. 47). Subjectivity as intentionality, as *ego cogito cogitatum*, becomes an improper formulation of the ek-stasis of Being — insofar as it is "rien d'autre que la Condition de l'Objet," and Being is that condition, and there is nothing else to think. With Husserl, the seminar adds, the object is saved as intentional object, but intentionality remains caught in the immanence of consciousness and the object with it, "comme dans une boîte." This immanence must be broken to restore the object to its free essence in the primordial Outside. Henry reverses the seminar's verdict: Heidegger does not overcome Husserl's immanence; he completes Husserl's identification of subjectivity with transcendence, of which "immanence" in Husserl was only a misnomer.

What motivates the displacement that perverted our primitive converse with Being — the shadow immanence casts on the pure radiance of what is there before us, the irruption of Cartesian subjectivity? Henry turns the question back: should one not attend to the fact, constantly repeated, that the cogito is reached through the radical epoché of the world — not only the life-world where men live outside themselves in a kind of stupor, but the subjective "world" of sensible data flowing in immanent time, the ideal world of essences, every possible world? Hyperbolic doubt sets aside not beings, nor the ideal empire of essences with its mathematical simple natures, but "le mode de leur donation, c'est la subjectivité elle-même en tant qu'évidence" (pp. 47–48). The world of nature and of mathematical truths (two and three make five, "to doubt I must be") is doubtful only because the medium of visibility in which it shows itself is doubtful. Doubt rejects this medium, the ek-stasis traversed by the gaze, the apodictic seeing of evidence, "le sol de la phénoménologie husserlienne et son ultime substructure heideggérienne" (p. 48). Henry thus makes Cartesian doubt a reduction more radical than Husserl's and Heidegger's together, since it suspends the very element in which they think.

What remains? "*At certe videre videor*," says the Second Meditation (AT VII 29): at least it seems to me that I see. What subsists after doubt is the subjective experience of vision, its self-revelation to itself — insofar as, being the revelation of seeing, it is not itself a seeing, or it would be doubtful and all certainty lost. The "seeming" in which it seems to me that I see, in which seeing feels itself — "sentimus nos videre" (AT I 413) — is not that in which seeing reaches its object, not the clearing of ek-stasis. This self-revelation is subjectivity itself, its original essence as ultimate ground. "La subjectivité hypokeiménale" — subjectivity as underlying — is subjectivity's self-revelation insofar as, excluding all transcendence and ek-stasis, occurring before the world and never in it, it essentializes itself "comme une immanence radicale" (p. 48). Interpreting this as the Ground on which everything rests — the *sub-jectum* in its essence, subjectity as subjectivity — is not an incomprehensible lapse but the moment the problematic matches its project in returning to the true Beginning. To ground seeing and secure all evidence, one must show how seeing settles into itself so that, in this primitive immersion where subjectivity touches every point of its being, "il n'y a ni voir ni ek-stase et, de cette façon, ni doute ni erreur possible" (p. 49). The *videor* reading is the core of Henry's Descartes interpretation and is developed in Chapter III. Henry turns Heidegger's word "subjectity" (*Subjektität*) against him: the Cartesian *subiectum* is not the represented ground of representation but the immanence that representation cannot reach.

This immediate self-possession is the *idea* in its original Cartesian sense: the idea of the mind, which differs from all other ideas (AT VII 443) in that, without objective reality and reduced to its material or formal reality, to pure *cogitatio*, it is the power by which *cogitatio* reveals itself to itself as *cogitatio*, as it is in itself — the revelation of thought itself and not of an otherness, a *cogitatum*, an objective reality. The formal reality of the idea therefore does not designate a mere form of a content outside it but, in the absence of all exteriority, thought's own reality, its "réalité matérielle," "la matérialité phénoménologique de la phénoménalité pure en tant que celle-ci ne consiste plus dans une ek-stasis mais dans l'immanence" (p. 49). Henry quotes the definition of idea from the Second Replies: "cette forme de chacune de nos pensées par la perception immédiate de laquelle nous avons connaissance de ces mêmes pensées" (AT VII 160). Cartesian ideas are thus first the radically immanent modalities of subjectivity — not the data of internal time-consciousness but what belongs, before any constitution, to the materiality of absolute subjectivity: sensations, feelings, passions, fears, volitions, "des idées qui sont seulement en notre âme" (AT IX-2 56). This rereading of formal reality as phenomenological materiality links the essay to Henry's "material phenomenology" (*Phénoménologie matérielle*, 1990), where hyletic and affective matter becomes the stuff of self-givenness.

The Cartesian reduction, if doubt may be so called, has nothing to do with the Husserlian; the latter is neither its extension nor its deepening but "une mésinterprétation ruineuse" (p. 49). Husserl suspends the world only to consider its givenness, consciousness of the world, intentionality, so that consciousness becomes consciousness of the world, *cogitatio* the relation to the *cogitatum*, phenomenality ek-stasis. What is lost is subjectivity's original coming-into-itself in the idea of the mind, a sphere of radical immanence without *cogitatum*, intentionality or world. The aporia on which Husserl's phenomenology broke — its inability to give phenomenological status to the I "functioning in the last instance" — results from ignorance of the most original dimension of phenomenality, which Descartes glimpsed as *cogitatio*, as "knowledge of the soul" that the Second Meditation sets before "knowledge of the body," consciousness of the world, and grounds. For seeing — the *inspectio* of the intellect, for instance — is possible only if it has already come into itself in its seeing (p. 50). The two reductions differ in what they keep: Husserl keeps the givenness of the world, Descartes keeps what gives itself without world.

Husserl's reduction also performs a thematic displacement by which thought turns from the world to itself, so that this self-perception is still intentional and what it perceives as its noema is again intentionality, a consciousness of the world, which is both the how and the content of transcendental experience. The phenomenologist no longer has any idea of the essence of original subjectivity. The deficiency appeared from Husserl's first efforts to grasp lived experiences: Henry quotes the *Logical Investigations* — whoever contests the specificity of intentional experiences, refusing to see that object-being consists phenomenologically in acts in which something appears as object, "ne pourra sans doute pas comprendre comment l'être-objet peut lui-même devenir objectif" (p. 50). Because the being of the subject — the "specificity of experience" — is for phenomenology only the object-being of the object, phenomenology repeats the implicit presuppositions of classical thought, which post-Husserlian phenomenology would raise to the absolute. The citation shows the confusion at the origin of phenomenology, not merely in its later development.

The Cartesian reduction does not stop at shifting from things to their mode of givenness; within givenness it draws a dividing line between the phenomenality of object-being, held doubtful, and "une révélation plus ancienne" (p. 51). With what appears (the entity) out of play, appearing itself is decomposed; original subjectivity designates how phenomenality brings itself into itself independently of any ek-static mediation. Hence the first two Meditations mark a decisive turning point: the subjectivity they introduce is no longer the Greek appearing of the object, the hatching of a Nature, the principle of knowledge and science, but what, making these possible, remains unseen in them. The "decomposition of appearing" is the methodological core of Henry's phenomenology: a reduction not of beings to appearing but of appearing to its two modes.

How phenomenality brings itself into itself cannot remain in shadow or be a mere speculative assertion, if this "how" is phenomenality itself in the irruption of its Parousia. A method that seeks to see everything in the evidence of intuition, in the horizon of a world, seems inadequate if this original phenomenalization does not occur in the world. Henry italicizes: "L'ultime anonymat sur lequel est venue achopper la phénoménologie husserlienne est le constat de la condition non extatique de la subjectivité absolue et sa simple conséquence" (p. 51). The anonymity is thus reread as a positive finding misread as a defect. The question of method this raises — how a philosophy that thinks can reach what is not given to thought — is left open and posed again at the essay's end.

Though it never rises before our gaze, subjectivity does its work in us: it is this gaze, or rather its primitive coming into itself, "la semblance première en laquelle il me semble que je vois et par l'effet de laquelle je vois en effet" (p. 51). This ultimate possibility of seeing — and hearing, touching, imagining, conceiving, remembering, willing — and the phenomenological mode in which it is realized are now in question. Subjectivity's coming into itself is its self-affection, which is neither a concept nor a presupposition but "le Fait primitif en tant qu'un se sentir soi-même" in which phenomenality spreads into itself, its first phenomenological effectuation (p. 52). What feels itself without the ek-stasis of a sense or thought is, in its essence, Affectivity. Affectivity is not something; as the primitive form of Affection it belongs to and constitutes the givenness of all that is. It is not a psychological determination alongside sensation, imagination, volition, doubt or certainty, but "le Comment fondamental" according to which each first comes to itself and experiences itself — the pure phenomenological materiality of self-experience and the essence of absolute subjectivity. Here the misreading of affect as emotion is directly excluded: affectivity is the how of every mode of consciousness, not one mode among them. "Fait primitif" deliberately echoes Maine de Biran.

Two affections, united in subjectivity, thus define its being. In the first, affection is by the world; its structure is intentionality, its phenomenality transcendental exteriority. Husserl's remark belongs here: the world is "effectivement éprouvé [...] dans un 'vivre là-bas'" (*Crisis* §63, p. 52). In the second, affection is by subjectivity itself, self-affection; its phenomenality is transcendental affectivity, the power of self-feeling identical with subjectivity's original essence, "qui fait d'elle la Vie." Revealing itself in the self-affection of its affectivity, subjectivity is not only life but in principle individual and singular, being "l'ipséité comme telle." The phenomenological method's abandonment of individuality and singularity — the eidetic turn — attests its failure. This paragraph answers the question posed on p. 26 and ties the two affections to the theory of ipseity that *C'est moi la vérité* will develop: singularity follows from self-affection, not from position in the world.

The philosophy of subjectivity begins with the dissociation of these two fundamental modes of phenomenalization, which nascent phenomenology overlooked despite aiming at the systematic elucidation of subjectivity. Failing to recognize what makes subjectivity subjective, it could only abandon the concept and lose itself, in its post-Husserlian developments, in a thought of the world. So the will to overcome objectivism — philosophy's eternal task and the only thing that preserves for it an object of its own against the sciences — ended "par une faillite" (p. 53), however masked. Henry thereby claims for his own position the anti-objectivist programme phenomenology announced and abandoned.

With few exceptions — Maine de Biran above all, the only one to ask thematically whether there is a mode of inner apperception — the history of thought offers only "figures" of subjectivity. They take singular form in doctrines claiming scientific objectivism. In Freud's 1895 *Project for a Scientific Psychology*, the nervous system is defined by its neurones' capacity to receive two kinds of excitation, from the world and from the organism. Since "excitation" represents affection, its exogenous and endogenous modes inscribe in the organism a double ontological receptivity: transcendental receptivity toward the world in the unfolding of ek-stasis, and the self-receptivity of absolute subjectivity as living (p. 53). The neural model is read as a disguised phenomenology — the method of *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*.

The two modes obey fundamentally different laws. In the finite place of ecstatic light the entity appears only as the rest of what is withdraws from presence; whatever shows itself in intuition does so caught in indefinite references to what does not show itself, before vanishing in turn. To the original self-affection of absolute subjectivity, by contrast, it belongs in principle "de ne jamais perdre ce qui lui est donné, à savoir elle-même" (p. 54): driven back against itself, against being, undergoing it in a passivity stronger than any freedom, it is what it is forever. In this pathos of being toward itself and its impotence to break the bond of primordial Suffering that binds it to itself as "suffering oneself" lies the essence of being as absolute subjectivity, the underlying that persists identical to itself. So in the 1895 *Project* the neuronal system, unconsciously figuring absolute subjectivity, cannot rid itself of endogenous excitations and presents itself as an ego "perpétuellement investi," because affection as self-affection never ceases and life cannot undo itself. The passage from the principle of inertia, which tends to abolish invested energy, to the principle of constancy, which merely lowers its level, "camoufle et exprime" the structure of absolute subjectivity in the language of 1895 science. The point is that the impossibility of flight from endogenous stimuli, which Freud registers as a quantitative fact, is the scientific shadow of life's inability to escape itself — the root, for Henry, of anxiety and of the drive.

The Nietzschean figure is more interesting still. Taking up Schopenhauer's dissociation of Will, which means immanence, from Representation, grounded in ek-stasis, and pushing it to the end, Nietzsche refuses from the start the classical reduction of subjectivity to consciousness of objects and conceives it as Life. He understands life as the original affection of being in its pathos (Dionysus), hence as Affectivity, determining every image of the world (Apollo). Installing himself in life to test its real possibilities, he discovers the history of Suffering and joy as the fundamental phenomenological categories of our experience of being, and glimpses their inner connection, "l'incessant passage des tonalités ontologiques l'une dans l'autre, avant toute production d'image" (p. 54). The Dionysus/Apollo pair is translated into Henry's two modes of appearing; Chapters VI–VIII return to Schopenhauer and Nietzsche at length.

The last figure of subjectivity is offered, paradoxically, by the philosophies of the Unconscious. In their contradictory and confused way they at least say that the Ground of Being is not the dimension of ecstatic visibility and that there is no intentionality in it — because, as the self-affection of absolute subjectivity, the life we are "n'a pas de visage et ne se laisse jamais rencontrer ni apercevoir dans un monde" (pp. 54–55). The positive core Chapter I found in Freud is restated as a figure of the truth he misnamed.

To the properties that determine what is according to its mode of givenness must be added that everything appearing in an Outside is in principle unreal, never the very substance of our life, which ignores the Difference of ek-stasis. Hence Husserl had to abandon this reality for the intuition of ideal essences; hence Schopenhauer, Nietzsche and even Freud, believing they opposed Descartes, denied thought any ontological claim. But the ultimate reasons for this situation must be established apodictically, as must the possibility for thought to grasp, apodictically, what escapes it in principle. Henry closes: "La philosophie de la subjectivité est à faire — et la phénoménologie à recommencer" (p. 55). The final demand — that thought ground with certainty its own incapacity — is the methodological problem Henry confronts directly in Chapters III–V and in *Incarnation*: how a thinking that sees can speak truly of what is never seen.

## III. The Cogito and the Idea of Phenomenology

Henry, speaking in a lecture (Osaka, 1983), declines to expound the idea of phenomenology for itself and proposes instead to read it at work in a philosophical fragment it determines: Descartes' cogito. The method is to find in the cogito "la téléologie immanente de l'idée de phénoménologie" (p. 57) — phenomenology is treated not as a twentieth-century school but as an idea whose aim can be read retroactively in a seventeenth-century text.

The cogito still fascinates because it seeks a Beginning in a radical sense. What begins radically, if not being, without which nothing would be had it not first unfolded its essence to gather all that is? And what is already there before anything when anything appears, if not appearing itself? Appearing alone constitutes the Beginning — not insofar as it shapes the appearance of what appears, but "en tant qu'il apparaît d'abord lui-même et en lui-même" (p. 57). Only so is appearing identical to being: as it lights itself, as the appearing of appearing that expels nothingness and takes its place. Being is "l'apparence de ce champ pur," the beginning — "non pas le premier jour, mais le tout premier" (p. 58). The equation of being with appearing, already in Chapter I, is now qualified: being is not any appearing but appearing's self-appearing.

Descartes calls appearing as such "thought." Because the initial unfolding of appearing in its self-appearing is identical with that of being, Descartes believed he had found the beginning: I think, I am. Henry's terminological thesis — Cartesian *pensée* means appearing, not thinking in the modern sense — governs the whole essay.

Henry first clears away misunderstandings, beginning with Heidegger's objection in *Being and Time* that the Cartesian beginning is not radical because it presupposes an at least implicit pre-understanding of being: how could I say "I am" without knowing confusedly what being is? But Descartes does not say "I am"; he says "therefore I am." His affirmation results from systematically working out the preliminary from which alone the proposition of being is possible — appearing, which he calls thought. Determining that preliminary is the content of the cogito: "Nous sommes par cela seul que nous pensons" (*Principles* I §8, Henry's emphasis, p. 58). The reply turns Heidegger's charge around: Descartes' "therefore" marks precisely the priority of appearing over any understanding of being.

That the affirmation of being results solely from appearing's self-projection into appearing, its settling into itself, is attested by a cutting reply to Gassendi: Descartes is surprised that Gassendi holds the idea of a thing in general cannot be in the mind unless the ideas of animal, plant, stone and all universals are there too — as if, to know I am a thinking thing, I had to know animals and plants (AT VII 362, Fifth Replies; pp. 58–59). Descartes knows what a thing in general is insofar as he knows what a thinking thing is — one whose substantiality and materiality "sont la substantialité et la matérialité de la phénoménalité pure comme telle" (p. 59). The reply shows that for Descartes the meaning of "thing" is learned from the *res cogitans*, not from the world — which Henry reads as the derivation of being from self-appearing.

The idea of phenomenology at work in this "Cartesianism of the beginning" — not that of the *Regulae*, but the unique moment when thought takes the Beginning into view and tries to match it — yields three components. First, the phenomenological reduction as reduction to pure appearing; second, its achievement by putting the entity out of play. The separation of appearing from entity is the Cartesian difference of soul and body. The soul finds its essence in appearing; it belongs to the body, in principle, to lack the power of manifestation — which is why all bodily determinations, the eyes for instance, are blind. Cartesian mechanism originally means only this radical heterogeneity of the entity to manifestation. Because the soul means original appearing, Cartesian "thought" has nothing to do with what we now call thought, "penser que," considering that — something like a *meinen* (opining). Doubt is thought in that sense, considering things doubtful; certainty is considering them certain. The cogito has nothing to do with doubt, certainty or their exchange, as the Seventh Replies show when Descartes reproaches Bourdin for substituting for the essential difference between thinking and non-thinking things the inessential difference between those that "consider that they think" and those that do not (AT VII 559). Most critiques of the cogito repeat Bourdin's confusion — for instance the Lacanian who thinks he refutes Descartes by saying "Je ne suis pas ce que je pense, je suis ce que je ne pense pas" (p. 59). Henry's reading of mechanism is striking: it is not a physics but a phenomenological thesis that matter does not appear to itself. The Lacanian formula (a variant of the one in "L'instance de la lettre") is dismissed as a confusion of cogitatio with reflective judgment.

A third component has already risen before us unnoticed; it alone matters. Henry's whole lecture is devoted to it, and it will be named only on p. 65: the phenomenological materiality of appearing.

The cogito finds its ultimate formulation in *videre videor*: it seems to me that I see. Descartes has performed the epoché of the world and of himself as man, his body, his eyes. What can seeing mean for a being without eyes? *At certe videre videor, audire, calescere* — at least it seems to me that I see, hear, grow warm. What remains is pure vision, abstracted from presumed eyes, body and world. But if pure vision subsists as "phenomenon," does not what is seen subsist too, as "mere phenomenon," the sensible appearances taken just as they appear? The Cartesianism of the beginning answers no. Those appearances are not as I believe I see them, since I believe I see real forms that may belong to the dream. And a vision not of the eyes sees something else — that 2 and 3 make 5, the properties of a triangle — which also falls under the reduction. If such clearly seen contents are false, "c'est parce que la vision elle-même est fallacieuse, c'est parce que le voir n'en est pas un" (p. 60). Henry separates himself here from Husserl, for whom the reduced phenomenon (the seen as seen) is the indubitable residue: for Descartes, on Henry's reading, the seen-as-seen is doubtful too, because seeing as such is.

The familiar path of the epoché gives way underfoot. Rejecting all that appears was supposed to free appearing itself as ultimate ground. Now this ground wavers: appearing, insofar as it is a seeing. Seeing is rejected because what is seen is not as we see it. What is seeing? To throw oneself outside oneself into a horizon of light that is this very exteriority — to ek-sist in the ecstatic dimension Descartes calls natural light, which, according to a famous statement of the first *Rule*, illuminates everything without being affected or modified by it. It is this light that is troubled. Doubt can overturn essences only if it first questions their medium of visibility. The reduction thus abandons the possibility of all seeing, ek-stasis itself. What remains? The hyphenated "ek-sist" assimilates Cartesian natural light to Heideggerian *Ek-sistenz*, so that doubt becomes a suspension of the very element of Heidegger's thought.

*At certe videre videor*. Descartes holds that this vision, however deceptive, at least exists. But to exist, for the Cartesianism of the beginning, means to appear; *videor* designates nothing else — the primitive "seeming" (*semblance*) in which vision manifests and gives itself to us originally, whatever its credibility as vision, whatever it sees or believes it sees. The crucial question of Cartesianism, and perhaps of all true philosophy, now arises: is the seeming that reigns in the *videor* — the original appearing by which *videre* first appears to itself — identical with the seeming in which seeing unfolds its essence and constitutes itself as seeing? "L'essence originelle de la révélation est-elle réductible à l'ek-stasis ?" (p. 61). Henry exploits the deponent *videor*, which means both "I seem" and, passively, "I am seen": the self-givenness of seeing is a "seeming" that is not itself a seeing.

An equivalent formulation is *sentimus nos videre*, "we feel that we see," the more interesting because it appears where Descartes opposes animal vision — animals see nothing at all — to "human" vision, effective vision, which is not mere *videre* but is such only if *sentimus nos videre* (letter to Plempius, 3 October 1637, AT I 413–414). The animal-machine thesis supplies the contrast: a seeing that does not feel itself is no seeing at all, since nothing appears in it.

What is feeling? Seeing is a mode of feeling, like hearing or touching. Moreover, feeling not only is homogeneous with seeing but leads back to it, if sensible vision and sensible experience generally rest on a transcendental seeing — not merely in the sense that perceiving men passing in the street with their hats presupposes the vision of the thinking substance (the Second Meditation's example), but in the sense that this vision itself is possible only in ek-stasis. The objection Henry raises against himself is serious: if feeling is a mode of seeing, *sentimus nos videre* would merely put one ek-stasis behind another.

Three theses of the Cartesianism of the beginning make it impossible to reduce the *videor* immanent to thought, its original seeming, to the *videre* of ek-stasis. The first, already expounded: seeing deceives. The second: the soul cannot be felt. What is excluded is not empirical feeling, already barred by the reduction, but "la seule pensée de voir et de toucher" — the pure structure of such thought, its ecstatic structure (p. 62). Henry reads the Cartesian refusal to make the soul an object of sensation as a refusal of ek-static self-givenness, not merely of bodily perception.

The third, irrefutable thesis was formulated when chance confronted Descartes directly with the question. For once rising above his crude sensualism, Gassendi stated, in his own language, the ek-static structure of all possible feeling: the fingertip does not touch itself, the eye does not see itself, the understanding does not conceive itself. The condition Gassendi demands for anything to enter experience is the oppositional structure of ek-stasis, coming outside oneself so as to stand before oneself. Why does the eye not see itself? Because "cette faculté n'étant pas hors de soi ne peut former la notion de soi-même." Why does the eye see itself in a mirror? Because "entre l'œil et le miroir il y a un espace." The same for the mind: "Donnez-moi donc un miroir et vous pourrez alors vous voir et vous connaître vous-même" (Fifth Objections, AT VII 292; p. 62). Gassendi's mirror is the most explicit premodern statement of the thesis Henry calls ontological monism — that all manifestation requires distance — and Descartes' rejection of it is the third thesis.

Descartes' radical rejection of these presuppositions, those of Western philosophy and of ek-static phenomenality in its claim to originality, finds positive expression in all his technical definitions of thought and idea, offered tirelessly and in vain. All thematize immediacy, appearing's original self-appearing which, excluding the mediation of ek-stasis, consists in thought's primitive feeling of itself: "Par le nom de pensée, j'entends tout ce qui est tellement en nous que nous en sommes immédiatement conscients" (Second Replies, Definitions I–II; *Principles* I §9; p. 63). Excluding the exteriorization of exteriority, thought essentializes itself as radical interiority. The definitions point to this interiority — "ce qui est tellement en nous" — and lead to "cette sorte de connaissance intérieure qui précède l'acquise," a proposition so often cited that it is perhaps time to understand it. The phrase comes from the Sixth Replies; Henry's "in vain" means that these definitions were never taken seriously, by Descartes' successors or by Descartes himself.

Inner knowledge is "knowledge of the soul," acquired knowledge "knowledge of the body." Neither concerns soul or body, still less one knowledge directed in turn at each. The body — the entity — has fallen under the reduction. Both expressions belong in quotation marks: they designate two pure modes of appearing. Knowledge of the body is ek-stasis, which also falls under the reduction; knowledge of the soul is the "inner" and "immediate" knowledge at issue (p. 63). Henry's reading makes the Cartesian dualism a duality of modes of manifestation rather than of substances — the same move as in Chapter II.

Among the inconsistent objections of Descartes' illustrious contemporaries is one genuinely philosophical question, Hobbes': granted that knowing "I exist" depends on knowing "I think," "d'où vient la connaissance de celle-ci : je pense ?" (Third Objections, AT VII 173; pp. 63–64). Hobbes asks for the mode of givenness of the cogito, which is exactly Henry's question.

The answer is the technical definition of idea: "cette forme de chacune de nos pensées par la perception immédiate de laquelle nous avons connaissance de ces mêmes pensées" (AT VII 160). Thoughts are appearing — "des cristaux d'apparaître" — and differ thereby from bodies, from the entity. But appearing appears only by appearing to itself; each thought is a thought only by revealing itself to itself, coming into itself. "Thought" for Descartes means not merely thought designated from outside, as for us, but that by which thought originally comes into itself and is thought. How? The definition of idea says, again: not by ek-stasis. The original coming that traverses every thought is a coming of each thought into itself, delivering it to itself — its self-revelation, not the revelation of an otherness or objectivity. The idea in this sense shows what its formal reality is: not the form of an external content but, absent all exteriority, its unity with the radically immanent content it is, identical with thought. That is why Descartes' examples of idea as thought's original essence are limited to its immanent modalities: "lorsque je veux et que je crains, ce vouloir et cette crainte sont mis par moi au nombre des idées" (Third Replies, AT VII 181; pp. 64–65). Sensations and feelings are "des idées qui sont seulement en notre âme." For this self-reference of appearing, "I think" seems too simple; an adequate formulation of the cogito must wait for Nietzsche, who says not "I am" but, more rigorously, "je suis ce que je suis" (*Genealogy of Morals* III §14), laying the foundation of the great analyses of his last period (p. 65). The definition answers Hobbes: I know that I think not by any further knowledge but by thought's own self-givenness. The Nietzsche tautology anticipates Chapter VIII, where "I am what I am" names the self-identity of affective life.

Descartes affirms not only that knowledge of the soul is irreducible to knowledge of the body but its primacy, which the Second Meditation is devoted to establishing. "Knowledge of the body," seeing as such — whether by the eyes or what remains after the reduction, touch, imagination or the mind's inspection — presupposes, as vision of what it sees, the self-appearing of this vision, the self-revelation of its ek-stasis "comme différente de celle-ci toutefois et comme son préalable" (p. 65). Only so does "la connaissance que nous avons de notre pensée précède[r] celle que nous avons du corps" (*Principles* I §11); were it identical with it in the essence of its appearing, how could it be its presupposition? The argument is a priority argument: what conditions a mode of appearing cannot share its mode.

Here the third component of the idea of phenomenology is named. Henry calls *material phenomenology* that which considers "la matérialité phénoménologique de la phénoménalité pure," phenomenality in its actuality (pp. 65–66). It does not deal with "phenomena" as contents of knowledge, asking which can be posited with what degree of validity, but solely with the mode of givenness and the pure phenomenality in which it consists. It is pure phenomenality itself that brings itself to appearance by its own power; material phenomenology seeks only to read, in this accomplished phenomenality, the structure of its mode of accomplishment, its phenomenological materiality. The opposition of *videor* and *videre* is philosophically grounded only if the two primitive modes in which phenomenality materializes differ in the very matter of their appearing. And if Descartes seeks not only a dissociation but a hierarchy — only one mode escaping the reduction — it must be shown how, in that primitive upsurge, everything that phenomenalizes in it shows itself as it is, in its reality, as a kind of "omni-exhibition de soi-même" that alone could warrant calling such manifestation absolute and indubitable. Did Descartes ever designate the phenomenological substance of appearing as self-attesting, self-presenting as it is, the ground of all absolute truth? This is the programmatic definition of material phenomenology later developed in the 1990 book of that name. "Material" is opposed not to "ideal" or "formal" in Husserl's sense but to the indifference of phenomenality to its own stuff: the question is what appearing is made of.

Article 26 of the *Passions of the Soul* answers. Developing a pre-scientific problematic — the body's action on the soul through nerves and animal spirits, the antipodes of the reduction — it abruptly returns to the reduction. Sleep and waking, indistinguishable, are evoked again. What the sleeper or waker thinks he sees or feels in his body is put out of play; seeing and feeling are again denied access to truth, while self-feeling, or rather its pure phenomenological materiality and substantiality — the affectivity of this original self-affection and all its modalities — "se trouvent marquées soudain du sceau de l'absolu" (p. 67). They reveal themselves, in their affectivity and by it, as they are, and no illusion has power over them. Henry quotes: one may imagine so strongly that one thinks one sees or feels things that are not there, "mais, encore qu'on soit endormi ou qu'on rêve, on ne saurait se sentir triste et ému de quelque autre passion, qu'il ne soit très vrai que l'âme a en soi cette passion" (pp. 66–67). Chapter I's reading of article 26 is now given its theoretical frame: affect is the answer to the question of what self-attesting appearing is made of.

The crucial opposition of *videor* and *videre* thus recurs, in material phenomenology, grounded by the phenomenological substantiality of the fundamental modes of appearing, as the opposition of passion and perception: one can be deceived about perceptions referring to external objects or parts of our body, "mais [...] on ne peut l'être en même façon touchant les passions, d'autant qu'elles sont si proches et si intérieures à notre âme qu'il est impossible qu'elle les sente sans qu'elles soient véritablement telles qu'elle les sent" (*Passions* §26, p. 67). Descartes' own wording — "felt" and "truly such as felt" coincide — is Henry's definition of self-affection.

As thought's ultimate possibility, affectivity reigns over all modes of thought and secretly determines them. Even in Descartes the reign of passion extends strangely. Passions in the strict sense relate to the soul; others relate to objects and to our body. They are delimited, admittedly, not only by their intrinsic affectivity but by the transcendent construction of the *permixtio* (the soul–body union). But the body falls under the reduction. Is the reduction provisional? Far from it: "en tant que variation eidétique, le doute libère ce qui demeure" (p. 67). As to the relation between affectivity and the Cartesian body, the reduction means that in the materiality of original appearing, which is affectivity, and in the dimension it constitutes, "'le corps' n'est pas compris et ne le sera jamais" (p. 68). Henry reads doubt as an eidetic variation — what survives the imagined suppression of the body belongs to the essence — and concludes that affect is not essentially bodily. This anticipates his later distinction between the original flesh and the objective body.

All attempts to explain affectivity are laughable, since they presuppose it as what they claim to explain. Whatever the dogmatic content of Cartesianism, passion extends far beyond its assigned domain, even taking in the infinite will, considered in its innermost possibility, its inherence in thought: "bien qu'au regard de notre âme, ce soit une action de vouloir quelque chose, on peut dire que c'est aussi en elle une passion d'apercevoir ce qu'elle veut" (*Passions* §19, p. 68). That even willing is felt passively in its self-perception shows that affectivity is the mode of givenness of action too — the root of Henry's claim that activity presupposes passivity.

Before this blinding intuition of affectivity as appearing's first coming to itself, the Cartesian gaze turns away. Affectivity is not the essence of thought, its phenomenological substance; it befalls thought not by virtue of what thought is but "comme un accident la contraignant de l'extérieur, comme son trouble" (p. 68), so that the principle of the reduction is again flouted. This is the recoil Chapter I located on p. 23: the passions are interpreted as effects of the body, reintroducing the reduced world as their cause.

Cartesianism then falls prey to an inexorable dialectic of general significance. If affectivity no longer builds appearing from within and gives it its phenomenological matter, where does the power of phenomenality lie? In ek-stasis, the light of exteriority, which "se pro-pose comme l'unique référence du concept de la phénoménalité" and of phenomenology itself (p. 68). The immanence prescribed by all the Cartesian definitions becomes a mere prescription, fragile once its phenomenological footing is gone. Light — of ek-stasis, of representability and *ratio* — supplies the footing and, slipping into the place of appearing's immediacy, takes the place that immediacy, in its invisible withdrawal from the world, always leaves free. So the beginning is forgotten and lost. The cogito is dismembered, the seeming of *videor* is absorbed into that of *videre*, Cartesian "thought" is no longer soul or life but its opposite, "la pensée des modernes, la connaissance" (p. 69). The dialectic is general because invisibility leaves a vacancy that visibility always occupies: whatever does not appear in the world is replaced by what does.

The end of the Second Meditation already shows the clouding of *videor* by *videre*, undermined by a deep contradiction. On one hand Descartes would show that knowing the soul is easier than knowing the body and that all the powers by which we know the body must first be known in themselves, within a more original seeming. On the other, under pretext of grasping this knowledge better, and precisely because it is immanent to knowledge of the body, it is the latter that is thematized. The soul's faculties become faculties of Knowledge. The wax analysis, which proves the mind better known than body only by analysing how body is known, illustrates the slide.

The Third Meditation accentuates it. First a decisive substitution: for the cogito, its relation to the *cogitatum* — or the *cogitatum* itself — becomes the theme. The aim is to extend knowledge but first to found it definitively, as though the cogito had not. Forgetting the *videor* as the immediacy revealing *videre* to itself as an irreducible seeing leaves room for another project: to legitimate it mediately through divine veracity, which must be read in the idea of God as *cogitatum*, referring to the inventory of *cogitata* and their status. The *cogitatum* must escape the reduction by itself — what is thought, represented, seen, taken just as thought, represented, seen, is indubitable. But being-seen as such, considered as a phenomenological condition apart from what is seen, is sight itself, seeing unfolding in its space of light. With its original immediacy unnoticed, the cogito is nothing but such a seeing, its appearing that of the *cogitatum* (pp. 69–70). Heidegger's reading in *Nietzsche II*, which explicitly reduces "I think" to "I represent," stands at this level, as do countless interpretations that reduce the cogito's phenomenality to the light of *Rule* IX — Guéroult's, for example, which makes the Cartesian cogito Kantian or even Fichtean. Behind these errors lies a wholly general presupposition: deprived of radical interiority and reduced to the condition of objectivity, "la subjectivité du sujet n'est plus rien d'autre que l'objectivité de l'objet" (p. 70). The formula of Chapters I and II returns as the diagnosis of the Third Meditation, and Henry thus reads Heidegger and Guéroult as faithful to a Descartes who had already betrayed himself.

What the Third Meditation performs is not a thematic shift from *cogito* to *cogitatum* but the reduction of the former to the condition of the latter. This reduction, in which the essence of life is lost, is not only surreptitious but claimed: the cogito becomes the criterion of all truth as "clear and distinct perception," and itself the first of these truths — the contrary of what it is, an evidence (p. 70). Chapter I's puzzle — how the outcome of the critique of all evidence can be an evidence — is resolved: Descartes himself turned the cogito into its opposite.

Yet appearing's immediacy, being also that of knowing, is not so easily forgotten, and Cartesianism in its most brilliant insights returns to it. A singular situation arises: all the key concepts — thought, idea, apperception, perception, clarity, distinctness, confusion, obscurity — refer in turn to the two modes of phenomenality and their specific materiality. From this constant amphiboly results a text "illisible sans les repères que peut seule lui fournir une phénoménologie matérielle" (p. 70). Not only the content of the concepts varies but their relational play, according to the dimension of appearing whose laws they fix. To circumscribe original, non-ecstatic phenomenality Descartes uses the words of ek-stasis and knowledge, subsuming two irreducible orders under one monotonous terminology. This explains the persistent difficulty of Cartesian vocabulary: one lexicon, two phenomenalities.

First, clarity is identical with confusion and obscurity when it designates the immediacy of appearing — a single essence, clear in that it accomplishes the work of phenomenality, obscure in that the matter of that accomplishment is affectivity. So in *Principles* I §46: "Lorsque quelqu'un sent une douleur cuisante, la connaissance qu'il a de cette douleur est claire à son égard," not to be confused with the false judgment locating the pain in the injured part, "encore qu'il n'aperçoive rien clairement que le sentiment et la pensée confuse qui est en lui" (p. 71). In the original essence of appearing, clarity and obscurity cannot turn into one another, being always the Same, the dimension where phenomenality unfolds in the invisible, "de telle manière que rien de ce qui grandit en elle ne s'en va jamais hors d'elle [...] de telle manière que ce qui est vivant l'est à jamais." Pain is clear (it is wholly given) and obscure (it is not seen); Descartes' apparently confused usage records the structure exactly.

Second, clarity opposed to confusion and obscurity is that of ek-stasis: a single essence, clear in opening the place where light concentrates, obscure in that this place is surrounded by shadow, the unthematizable horizon of all ecstatic ex-position. The entity is clear or obscure only as it comes into the condition of object, never in itself. Clarity and confusion here are pure phenomenological determinations prescribed by ek-stasis; together they build the phenomenality of the world, "dont la méthode cartésienne tente de façon pathétique d'exorciser la finitude essentielle" (p. 71). The horizonal structure Chapter II found in Husserl is thus already the Cartesian clear-and-distinct, and the method's ambition to eliminate obscurity is doomed by the essence of the light it relies on.

So *cogito* means not one thing but two, heterogeneous — so much so that their co-belonging at the origin is one of philosophy's major problems. Henry adds a striking case: the most Cartesian of Cartesians, Malebranche, said of the founding phenomenon exactly the contrary of Descartes — that the cogito is not the clearest evidence but "un abîme d'obscurité," not the first knowledge but that of which we have no conceivable knowledge; that the soul is not easier to know than the body but unknowable; that what we can know of it comes only by analogy with knowledge of the body; that the idea of the soul cannot ground knowledge because, for us, it does not exist. Most surprising, despite this term-by-term opposition, Malebranche offers "pour la première fois et peut-être la dernière une répétition radicale" of the beginning (p. 72). Malebranche's "sentiment intérieur," which gives the soul without idea, is thus the purest Cartesianism: by denying that the soul is clear (in the ek-static sense) he preserves its immediacy. The co-originarity problem announced here is the question of how self-affection and world-affection belong together, central to *Incarnation*.

The Cartesian definition of man as soul, that is, as pure phenomenality, poses grave problems; much of our culture proceeds from them and from the inability to solve them. Is it chance that current research in almost every domain defines itself by rejecting the cogito, while sharing a total incomprehension of it? The problems of a phenomenological definition of man remain, often looking insurmountable. "Seule peut-être une phénoménologie matérielle sera en mesure de les affronter" (p. 72). The close links the Cartesian beginning to the cultural diagnosis of *La Barbarie*: the misreading of the cogito is not an academic error but the root of the self-misunderstanding of a scientific culture.

## IV. On the Ego of the Cogito

A decisive question attaches to the cogito: the ego. What does it mean that the ascent to the beginning and universal principle of all things ends by positing an ego — a particular one, which says not "it is thought" or "there is" but "I think, I am"? Is it because the regress is carried out by an empirical individual, Descartes or anyone who repeats it? No: *ego* arises together with *cogito*, at the end of the reduction and through it, when there is no longer any empirical individual or world. The ego belongs to the Beginning itself. *Ego cogito* means that in the original upsurge of appearing, ipseity (selfhood) is implied "comme son essence même et comme sa possibilité la plus intérieure" (p. 73). Descartes states this co-originarity only in the form: "*Nam quod ego sim qui dubitem, qui intelligam, qui velim, tam manifestum est, ut nihil occurrat per quod evidentius explicetur*" (AT VII 29; pp. 73–74) — that it is I who doubt, understand and will is so evident that nothing could explain it more evidently. Henry's note complains that the French version's "il n'est pas ici besoin de rien ajouter" weakens the Latin: Descartes says not that no explanation is needed but that nothing more evident exists by which to explain it — the ego is its own ultimate manifestation. Ipseity is thus placed inside phenomenality, not added to it by an empirical bearer.

How much beginnings matter in philosophy appears in this: because Descartes, having rooted the connection of ipseity and thought in the Beginning, did not elucidate it further, the being of the ego has remained wholly undetermined, and the most gratuitous and contradictory claims have been made about it ever since. Kant, unable to avoid the problem, could assign no foundation at all to the simple proposition "I am." The reference recalls Chapter I's account of the paralogisms.

Heidegger's commentary on the cogito in *Nietzsche II* is interesting because it confronts this question directly, though burdened by a serious confusion to be cleared first. For Heidegger, in each epoch being sends itself to us by withdrawing its ownmost being. With the cogito, which opens modern metaphysics, this obscuring reaches its extreme: man takes himself for the *subiectum*, usurping being's prerogatives. He posits himself as ground of truth and measure of all things. This "libération de l'homme pour la nouvelle liberté" — negatively, rejection of every other truth, "revealed, biblical, Christian"; positively, extension of his "autonomous legislation" to all beings — requires, since this legislation must be sure of itself, that man first make sure of himself. "L'autofondation de l'homme comme auto-assurance (certitude de soi)" of the ground of all truth is what the cogito claims (p. 74). Henry's summary is fair to Heidegger; the confusion he will isolate is the identification of the ego with "man."

Measured by its real aim, the cogito does not set man at the centre; it excludes him radically, in the reduction. What subsists has no eyes, ears, body or belonging to the world: "c'est seulement le premier apparaître selon l'effectivité phénoménologique pure" of its self-appearing (p. 75). The idea of man in constituted Cartesianism appears only later, when the gaze has slid from *cogito* to *cogitatum* and, within the system of representation, one *cogitatum* — the idea of God — shows the system does not support itself. Man enters Cartesianism only when he discovers himself finite, never as a ground. The textual point is exact: in the Meditations "man" appears in the Third, as the finite being who has the idea of the infinite.

And how, by Heidegger's own account, does man aspire to the role of ground? Not as man but as cogito: not an entity but an ontological structure — that of pure phenomenality — can be the ground of truth. In Heidegger's commentary "man" arbitrarily interferes with the Cartesian "subject" or a subjectivity baptized "human." The only question is the essence of phenomenality, the essence of "thought," and whether it reduces to representation. The ego is to be thought from appearing, not from anthropology; Heidegger's slide from structure to entity is exactly the error he imputes to Descartes.

For Heidegger, such a reduction characterizes Cartesianism and modern subjectivity generally. *Cogitare* means *percipere*, "prendre possession de quelque chose [...] au sens de le disposer devers soi," of placing something before oneself, representing it (*Nietzsche* II 122; p. 75). Because *cogitare* and *percipere* mean "to place before oneself," they mean the same as *Vorstellung* in its double sense: the bringing-before-oneself and what has been brought before oneself, the made-visible. In this disposing, the represented is not only given but dis-posed as available, secured as what man can master. Through its link with the *dubitare* it rejects yet comes from, *cogitare* relates to the indubitable: its placing-before "n'admet rien en tant que posé et certain [...] qui ne soit sanctionné devant lui" (p. 76). If representing is "a placing in full security," what makes it possible, what must be posited as secure? The ego answers, not merely joined to representation but as its intrinsic possibility. "Tout *ego cogito* est *cogito me cogitare*": every "I represent something" represents "me" as well, the one who represents. This does not mean that in representing Freiburg cathedral I also represent myself beside it as a vague marginal object. By an essential necessity the representing self projects itself before itself and is implicated in its representation, since every represented is represented *to* the self, before it, toward it. The self is presupposed a priori as belonging to the constitution of the field of discovery, insofar as the field is built as thrown by it, before it — the "retro-reference" to the self being identical with the field's structure. Henry reconstructs Heidegger's argument at its strongest before overturning it: the ego is not a represented content but the pole toward which the representational field is organized.

Because he confuses the self with man, Heidegger can write that in every act of representing it is man who represents to himself, so that he represents himself with what he represents, "non pas après coup mais à l'avance" (*Nietzsche* II 125; p. 77). If the self is implied in representation as the toward-which of all represented and the implicit term of this retro-reference, every consciousness of an object is first consciousness of self, of the self ob-jected at representation's horizon as its ground. For the Self underlies representation, extending beneath it as that from which it unfolds and to which it returns: "Le Soi est *subiectum*" (*Nietzsche* II 126). The hyphenated *ob-jeté* already signals Henry's coming objection: a self "ob-jected" is a represented self.

A theory of the ego and of "I am" takes shape, claiming to explain *ego cogito ergo sum*. It develops a decisive presupposition, of which it is merely the reading-off: *cogito* means "I represent to myself." There is then no inference from *cogito* to *sum*, but recognition in the structure of representation of the "I" that necessarily unfolds there and is ultimately identical with it. Because every object is ob-jected, op-posed to the one representing it, the representer is already there as having disposed the object toward himself and, more fundamentally, as having already disposed himself toward himself — by which "l'homme [...] peut, en tant que celui qui se représente quelque chose, dire 'je'" (*Nietzsche* II 129–130; pp. 77–78). Leaving aside "man," which has no business here, there is no inference, because the *sum* of the representer — his self-disposal in representation — is identical with representation and constitutes it. *Cogito* and *sum* mean the same: I dis-pose toward myself, I represent. The "I" is so certainly disposed to representation that "aucune induction, si concluante fût-elle," could reach that certainty (*Nietzsche* II 130; p. 78). Henry grants the result — no inference — but for the opposite reason: not because *sum* is the self-disposal of representation, but because *sum* is the self-appearing that precedes it.

Nothing in the Cartesian text alludes to a problematic like that of *Nietzsche II*, making ipseity depend on and intelligible from the structure of representation. On the contrary, the brief, enigmatic, flashing irruption of the ego in the Second Meditation occurs at the ultimate moment of the reduction, where doubt is alone, where there is no world and so no representation. What Descartes holds is a purely immanent element reduced to itself, to its formal reality apart from all objective reality, and in it he reads the ipseity of the ego — identical with it, with the supreme essence, beyond which there is nothing in which ipseity could be recognized more evidently: "*tam manifestum est ut nihil occurrat per quod evidentius explicetur*." But this essence is that of manifestation. One must therefore face a double evidence: "l'essence originelle de la phénoménalité exclut de soi la représentéité" — representedness — "et [...] c'est précisément par l'œuvre de cette exclusion qu'elle s'essencifie en soi-même comme un Soi" (pp. 78–79). The two theses to be demonstrated are announced: appearing excludes representation; and this exclusion is what makes appearing a Self.

For the first, recalling *Passions* §26 suffices. Pushing the reduction to the limit, it states that everything represented, far from being secured by representation, is doubtful — what I believe I see or imagine in pure representation, "in a dream" — while one cannot feel sad or moved by any passion "qu'il ne soit très vrai que l'âme a en soi cette passion." The immanence of the affective determination, sadness, or what Descartes calls the formal reality of the idea, alone is the site of absolute certainty and truth, which, as self-certainty and self-legitimating truth, consist in appearing's first self-appearing. Representation has nothing to do with this: sensation — pain, for instance — is wholly what it is in the immanence of its affectivity without first being placed before itself; "pour être certaine de soi elle n'a pas besoin de cette venue à l'ob-stance, sa souffrance lui suffit" (p. 79). Considered in itself, as Descartes invites, apart from its being-represented in the body or object, sensation receives being in the self-affection of its affectivity. In the exteriority of representation, outside thought's immanence, "nous ne concevons en aucune façon quelle chose c'est que cette couleur, cette douleur" (*Principles* I §68). "Ob-stance" is Henry's calque on *Gegenstand*, the condition of standing-against; pain's certainty does not depend on it.

The Cartesian critique of secondary qualities thereby reveals its "abyssal" meaning: an ultimate division between the dead and the living. Sensations are life; they unfold where life does, where there is neither ek-stasis nor world, in the radical interiority Descartes calls the soul. Granted to the phenomenologists, there are transcendent qualities — the sky is blue, the river serene — and it does seem to me that my foot hurts. But the quality extended in the thing — colour on the surface, pain in the foot — is only "la représentation irréelle, l'ob-jection d'une impression réelle vivante," which affects and impresses itself in its affectivity alone (p. 80). Where self-feeling occurs, there is no space, whether of the thing or of the organic body into which the impression is ob-jected. Evidence: the dream, with no real space, where the wall still seems yellow; the amputee's phantom pain, whose being is only its impressional self-impressing; most radically, pure feelings like sadness and joy, which have no objective reality, only formal reality, are wholly in the soul and cannot owe their being to a representation they lack. Henry reverses the usual valuation: secondary qualities are not lesser realities but the only real ones, because they are lived; primary, representable qualities are the "dead" world. The phantom limb, a classic of Merleau-Ponty, is turned against embodiment-as-being-in-the-world.

Heidegger's claim that "le représenter (*percipere, co-agitare, cogitare, repraesentare in uno*) est un trait fondamental de tout comportement humain, même non cognitif" (*Nietzsche* II 346) must be not only contested but reversed. Non-cognitive "behaviours" — feelings, passions, will — are in Descartes wholly foreign to representing, and this heterogeneity defines the original dimension of the cogito. Hence even cognitive behaviours, as belonging to the cogito — the idea in its formal reality, as a mode of the soul — ignore representing. Heidegger's parenthesis is an amalgam: original *perceptio* and *cogitatio* have nothing to do with *repraesentare in uno*, as the definitions of *cogitatio* by immediacy and the uses of *perceptio* referring to the same immanence show: "Celles [nos perceptions] qui ont l'âme pour cause sont les perceptions de nos volontés" (*Passions* §19); the passions may be defined as "des perceptions ou des sentiments ou des émotions de l'âme, qu'on rapporte particulièrement à elle" (*Passions* §27; pp. 80–81). Henry's philological point is that Cartesian *perceptio* frequently means immanent self-givenness, so Heidegger's equation of *perceptio* with *Vor-stellen* falsifies the vocabulary.

If one cannot go higher than this principial immediacy, it must also ground the essence of ipseity, of the ego. Though Descartes did not elucidate it further, he explicitly located in thought's most primitive seeming what makes it not only thought, being, "there is," but "I think, I am." The original phenomenalization of phenomenality is ipseity insofar as appearing appears to itself in an immediate self-affection without distance, independent of ek-stasis and representation — so that what affects it and shows itself to it is itself and not something other, its own reality and not something unreal; affecting itself and constituting the content of its self-affection, it is "comme tel un Soi, le Soi de l'ipséité et de la vie. Car le Soi est l'identité de l'affectant et de l'affecté" (p. 81). Such a being Descartes calls soul, Henry calls life: what experiences itself, and which is affected by anything only on condition that it first affects itself. This is the volume's central definition of the Self: not a pole, not a reflection, but the identity of affecting and affected in self-affection. It is the thesis of *C'est moi la vérité* in compressed form.

Because ipseity resides in thought's immanent essence, the claim to ground the ego on the seeing of representation is a paralogism, all the more deceptive for relying on an "appearance" that must be deconstructed. It is true that every representing as self-representing, as an act of ob-jecting and op-posing, implies that the representer projects itself at the horizon as that toward which everything opposed is opposed. But the representer is not a Self because the opposed is represented to it; rather, "c'est parce qu'il est un Soi et porte déjà le Soi en lui qu'il peut se représenter ce qu'il se représente," project itself beyond the opposed, oppose and dis-pose itself to itself — that all object-consciousness is self-consciousness (p. 82). The Self is implied in representation as its *subiectum* only because presupposed by it, as what it does not produce or explain but presupposes "comme l'autre d'elle-même, comme le fondement qu'elle est impuissante à fonder." The word "paralogism" is chosen to echo Kant: Heidegger commits a paralogism of representation, inferring from the formal "self" of representation to the reality of the Self.

All of Heidegger's propositions linking the ego to representation and deriving it from representation must be reversed. To the claim that in *ego cogito* "le 'je' y est entendu en tant que le soi sur quoi le re-présenter en tant que tel revient par essence se poser" (*Nietzsche* II 133), one replies that it is because the "I" is already understood, already constituted in itself outside representation's structure, that representation "is thus what it is" — what possesses itself as a Self beforehand in order to represent anything. The reversal is a reversal of the order of foundation, not a denial of the structure Heidegger describes.

The same inversion holds for the statement that, since the relation to the one-who-represents belongs to representing and all representedness gathers toward him, he can call himself "I" and is, in an emphatic sense, subject — that toward which everything returns within the underlying ground of representation (*Nietzsche* II 131). The one who represents can call himself "I" only if he already is so in himself, on the ground of ipseity's own essence. Otherwise the "I" of "I represent to myself" would be like the tree said to be reflected in the river, which sends back its image — as if the image's being set before the tree and referring to it made the tree an ego, "comme si un pronom réfléchi suffisait à faire surgir [...] l'ipséité" (pp. 82–83). The image of the tree is the essay's sharpest counterexample: reflexive structure without selfhood. A reflexive pronoun does not make a self.

One will object that the tree does not truly represent its image or transcend toward it. Precisely: representing cannot set before itself what refers back to its self, cannot project itself as the Self toward which all representedness unfolds, unless it is first a Self; protentional and retro-referential relations presuppose ipseity rather than constitute it. The presupposition is double. Ipseity is immanent to representation as its condition, since there is no self-representing without it; and, implied in representation, ipseity is neither explained nor grounded by it. "Ce n'est pas parce qu'il y a un 'devant soi' [...] qu'il y a un Soi," but because there is a Self in which ipseity lives that anything can be determined relative to it (p. 83). The Self exists neither in the "before" nor in the "toward," which are not even possible by themselves. "Le Soi est un phénomène de la vie, surgissant dans l'intériorité radicale de son auto-affection." That "before" and "toward" exist only as "before oneself" and "toward oneself" means that "l'intériorité est la condition de toute extériorité, le Soi est la condition de la représentation." The final formula states Henry's foundational order between the two modes of appearing in terms of selfhood: the "self" of "before itself" borrows its sense from the self of life.

The Heideggerian paralogism is easier to see in its Cartesian context. Descartes did seek an unshakeable foundation of truth, an ultimate certainty, and believed he found it in *ego cogito*. Because this ego must support the edifice of knowledge, its consistency must first be established insofar as it is identified with thought. The question is how this consistency takes shape, what makes the *ego cogito* self-legitimating. Heidegger answers: "La consistance de moi-même en tant que *res cogitans* consiste dans la sûre et stable détermination du re-présenter, dans la certitude conformément à laquelle le soi est porté devant soi-même" (*Nietzsche* II 132; pp. 83–84). The question of certainty is the terrain on which Henry will meet Heidegger next.

Two interlocking theses are advanced. First, the ego is defined as representing: "I am insofar as I represent myself." Second, the ego is certain of itself precisely as representing, because it places itself before itself and holds itself firmly in this act. In the secure structure of this self-placing, all truth and certainty concerning what is gathered there and brought to the condition of the ob-jected become possible. Once the Self is defined as self-representing and secured as such, it secures everything it represents: "mon représenter [...] décide de la présence de toute chose représentée," of its being as an entity (*Nietzsche* II 131). On the prior certainty of the self's self-placing rests the certainty of all that is placed before it. "Ce sur quoi toutes choses se trouvent replacées comme sur un fondement inébranlable est la pleine essence de la représentation même" (p. 84). Henry isolates the structure — certainty of the world grounded in certainty of self-representation — in order to show that Descartes' doubt targets exactly that structure.

But in the Cartesianism of the Beginning, everything represented, valid only by being placed on this *subiectum* of representation, everything appearing in the Self's self-proposal, is swept away by the reduction, cast out of all possible certainty and annulled. For the appearance produced in self-opposition, identical with it, cannot secure itself; the seeing that moves in this appearance and borrows its light is doubtful. Far from an "unshakeable foundation," "la 'pleine essence de la représentation même' se désagrège et part en lambeaux" (pp. 84–85). The radicality of the Cartesian effort is measured by this rejection of representation, which could be illusory, and by the fact that, despite and thanks to it, a path is cleared to a true foundation. Heidegger's own formula is turned against him: what he calls the foundation is exactly what doubt dissolves.

That this foundation is the ego is possible on one condition: that it not be the ego of representation, whose ipseity would find its essence in representation. Such an ego would vanish with it in the reduction — and more: no ego is possible in and through representation, since the structure of opposition is that of otherness, so that whatever shows itself to the Self and affects it in this structure is in principle other than it. Affected by this other, the Self cannot be affected by itself and its own reality; it cannot be a Self, "ce qui s'affecte soi-même et dont tout l'être est constitué par soi" (p. 85). The argument is a priori: hetero-affection cannot yield selfhood, because selfhood is by definition self-affection.

Moreover, for Descartes representation never presents reality — formal reality — but only the objective reality of the idea: an image, a double, a copy, an unreal equivalent that figures reality and refers to it without being it. What occurs in representation is both other and unreal, the contrary of the Cartesian ego, the Self that bears and defines reality. That a self represents itself can be understood only in a restricted sense: the self projected in representation is only a represented self, not the real Self that projects, but an unreal Self "co-ob-jeté à l'horizon de la représentation comme ce qui accompagne tout ob-jeté" (pp. 85–86), insofar as every ob-jected means "thrown before the self." This co-objected Self belongs to that meaning as its implied term, unreal like it. The Cartesian theory of objective reality supplies Henry with the thesis that the represented is unreal, which he applies to the represented self.

This represented Self is possible only from the real Self, as its mere representation or projection. And it is not this unreal self that is affected: "rien d'irréel, rien de représenté ne forme jamais le lieu d'une affection possible," but only what originally affects itself, the real, living Self (p. 86). Everything represented is represented before, to and toward it; that the represented also implies an unreal self in its representedness is only the representation of the real process of representing and of the fact that every possible represented is presented to a real Self. The supposed reading of ipseity in representation confuses the projecting, affected Self with the projected Self and reduces the first to the second, which is only its representation and presupposes it. The distinction between the Self who is affected and the Self that figures in the noematic sense "before me" resolves the paralogism: Heidegger describes the second and takes it for the first.

That representation is never the foundation in the Cartesianism of the Beginning appears also in that it cannot define the essence of certainty and truth any more than of ipseity. The entity becomes true and certain only in representing, placed before it and disposed toward it. But it holds truth and certainty from representation only if representing is already true and certain in itself. For Heidegger, it is because placing-before is firm and secure that what it holds is true. Not for Descartes: holding firmly before oneself, representing, seeing — all this is still doubtful. Henry quotes Heidegger: what is true is what representing brings clearly and distinctly before itself and disposes toward itself, to posit the represented securely; "La sûreté de semblable représenter est la certitude. Le vrai dans le sens de celle-ci est le réel," and this stability excludes the inconstancy of the doubting representing that "posits here and there"; "Le représenter, libre de doute, en est un clair et distinct" (*Nietzsche* II 342; pp. 86–87). Henry's note explains that he replaces Heidegger's bracketed subject "man" with "representing," for intelligibility — itself an application of his thesis that "man" is out of place in the analysis.

But Descartes' doubt strikes not only the inconstant representing that "posits here and there" but all representing as such, including the stable one that holds the represented in clear evidence. Because doubt is not a modality of representing but "loge dans son essence," what is "free of doubt" can no longer be this representing, however clear and distinct. The whole Cartesianism of the Beginning consists in seeking a ground of representation, an absolute certainty which, far from consisting in "the security of representing," which has none by itself, must establish it (p. 87). Henry's decisive objection to Heidegger: the hyperbolic doubt extends to clear and distinct perception itself, so certainty cannot be the security of clear and distinct representing.

This task falls to the ego — not the ego projected in self-representing, a represented ego as doubtful as its representation, whose own possibility (ipseity) must first be recognized. Only a problematic that ceases to rely on representing, its seeing and what it sees, can ground representing: it must first establish the existence of seeing, its self-feeling, such that, however false every representation, "n'en subsiste pas moins, en dehors d'elle et de son voir, le phénomène originel de son auto-affection." In this original form of thought, in which thought reaches itself immediately apart from all representing, "dans l'anti-essence de la représentation, non dans celle-ci," Descartes sought the absolute certainty that must ground representation's (p. 87). Before extending the reign of representation and science infinitely and offering the whole Earth to its ravage, the Cartesianism of the beginning had marked it with "une limite infranchissable." The final clause answers Heidegger's history of technology from Chapter I: the Cartesian origin of modernity contains the limit of the modern project, not merely its programme.

Heidegger's interpretation is not innocent. From its grave failings it draws the benefit that is no doubt its unavowed aim: inserting the ego into "the history of Western metaphysics." What is lost is not only the possibility of grounding any ego but the most initial element of the Beginning, the phenomenality of the soul — more foreign to representation than that from which the history of Western metaphysics proceeds, "que la vérité des Grecs et de Heidegger lui-même" (pp. 87–88). Henry's last word places Heidegger and the Greeks together on the side of ek-static truth, and the Cartesian soul outside the history of being altogether — the reason the cogito could not be read within that history.

## V. Descartes' Cogito and the Idea of an Ideal Phenomenology

Henry proposes a phenomenological reading of the cogito and anticipates the obvious reservation: to understand a thought from a later one, with procedures and conceptual systems that did not exist when the earlier doctrine formed, risks falsifying rather than illuminating. Can a phenomenological reading of the cogito be anything but an "interpretation"? The essay (an ENS lecture of 1991, revised 1996) thus begins with the hermeneutic objection Henry's previous chapters invited.

On one condition only: that the cogito be the birth certificate of phenomenology itself. Then a phenomenological reading is not merely possible but "la seule possible" (p. 89). First thesis: phenomenology is the only access to what is thought and must be thought in the cogito. The anachronism objection is reversed: if phenomenology originates in the cogito, reading it phenomenologically is reading it on its own terms.

This holds only with an essential precision: the phenomenology meant is not the movement inaugurated by Husserl, continued by Heidegger and their heirs, which Henry calls "historical phenomenology." Whether it invokes the cogito or explicitly criticizes it, historical phenomenology has missed what is at stake there. Only an "ideal phenomenology," one "qui serait ce qu'elle doit être," can grasp the cogito radically (p. 90). The title's "ideal" thus means not eidetic but normative: phenomenology as it should be, measured against its own idea.

Which phenomenology, then, if not Husserl's or Heidegger's? The one born in the cogito and identical with it, able to define its object and the means to reach it. But is understanding the cogito by the phenomenology born in it not circular? The cogito would be hard indeed to understand if it could be understood only from itself, in a self-understanding whose nature we do not yet grasp. Henry accepts the circle: the cogito's self-understanding will turn out to be its self-revelation, which is not a circle of reasoning but the structure of life.

Why is the cogito so difficult? Because the term covers two wholly different things. First, a problematic: a movement of thought proceeding by implications and evidences toward a certain reality, whose most general structure is that of thought itself, since it is thought's own process. A footnote adds that this process is expressed in texts, so it is also a linguistic process dependent on language and morphology (French or Latin); as text it tells a story comparable to literary or dramatic ones — some read the cogito as a drama with a plot, or a spiritual exercise of the period — and so becomes a cultural object open to historical, social, psychoanalytic and above all grammatical and logico-mathematical analysis (pp. 90–91). The footnote catalogues the approaches that treat the cogito as text — literary, dramatic, ascetic, logical — in order to set them all aside as concerning only the first sense.

Second, *cogito* means what the problematic aims at, the "reality" the thought-process seeks to grasp. The theme of the Meditations, especially the Second, is the nature of the human mind, which is thought, the essence of thought. The cogito in its double sense is thus the thought-process aiming to grasp the essence of thought — whence the idea of reflexivity commentators often attach to it. Henry names the source of the reflexivity reading in order to undo it.

Second thesis: the essence of thought that the problematic aims at "n'a aucun rapport" with the essence of the thought deployed in the problematic (p. 91). Though both are called thought — *cogito, cogitatio* — an abyss separates the realities named, and it is this designation of two heterogeneous "things" by one term and seemingly one concept that is the main obstacle to understanding. Hence any attempt to found understanding of the cogito on texts, words, language is nonsense; only a prior understanding of the cogito can give sense to the words. The claim is anti-hermeneutic in principle: the meaning of "cogito" is not available in language, because what it names is not of the order of what language shows.

Henry sketches the two realities confused in "cogito": the structure of thought unfolding as implications in the first two Meditations, under the title "*cogitatio–cogitatum*"; and the essence of thought it seeks, under the title "*cogito sans cogitatum*" — presupposing that there is a *cogito* without *cogitatum*, a *cogitatio* without objective reality, and that this is exclusively and essentially what Descartes first has in view (pp. 91–92). The phrase "cogito without cogitatum" is the essay's thesis-formula, flatly opposed to Husserl's principle that all consciousness is consciousness of something.

Comparing "*cogito cogitatum*" with "*cogito* without *cogitatum*," one may doubt they name exclusive realities: do they not share the element *cogito*, the first being richer by adding the *cogitatum*? Unless — reaffirming the thesis — *cogito* in the second phrase means something wholly other than in the first, with no relation to it. The objection treats the cogito as a constant term and the *cogitatum* as an addition; Henry will show that in the first formula the cogito is nothing apart from the *cogitatum*.

What does *cogito* mean in "*cogito cogitatum*"? The simple phrase becomes unintelligible as soon as one takes it as the formal prototype of a two-term structure with many ramifications: I think the tree loses its leaves, I think the leafless tree, I imagine it. The *cogitatum* is the tree, perceived, imagined or conceived; and just as the *cogitatum* is an entity, *cogito* would refer to me standing before the tree, imagining or remembering it. Henry sets up the naive picture of two entities facing each other.

Following Descartes, the last proposition must be struck out at once: the tree, the *cogitatum*, is an entity, surely not the cogito of this *cogitatum*. Descartes' footnoted denial — I am not this assemblage of members called the human body, not a subtle air spread through them (AT VII 27) — removes the ego from the order of entities. What then is the cogito of the tree, unlike it, no entity but its appearing, its "consciousness"? This question alone shows that any access to the cogito "ne peut être que phénoménologique": in what does this appearing, the cogito of the *cogitatum*, consist? (pp. 92–93). The first thesis is thus derived from the text: once the cogito is not an entity, only a science of appearing can approach it.

The *cogitatum* could be the tree, the path, the man on the path, his image, memory or idea, an artwork, beauty. If each, despite its difference, is called the *cogitatum*, then the *cogitatum* is none of them: each is the *cogitatum* only as thought, *qua cogitatum*. "Mais le *cogitatum qua cogitatum*, c'est le cogito purement et simplement, le cogito du *cogitatum*" (p. 93). Henry's argument is a variation: what is invariant across all *cogitata* is their being-thought, and that is the cogito.

The first, pre-philosophical approach must be corrected. We thought, following the text word by word, that the structure involves two terms, cogito on one side, *cogitatum* on the other — I who represent, what I represent. Not so: the cogito does not oppose the *cogitatum*, does not differ from it once we think the *cogitatum* as such, the tree's "insofar as it is thought." *Cogito* in "*cogito cogitatum*" is the tree's being-thought, "la pensée en tant que telle et rien d'autre. Le cogito est donc identique au *cogitatum* saisi en lui-même — *qua cogitatum*" (p. 93). The cogito of intentionality is thus noematic through and through — the thesis Chapter II drew from Husserl's "the noesis is the noema of this experience."

In the language of classical philosophy, the opposition of cogito and *cogitatum* becomes that of subject and object: the thinking subject on one side, the tree it thinks on the other. Is it not strange that thought is on the side of the tree, around it, in its being-thought, as much as "in" the subject? The enigma vanishes once we see that the subject is nothing but the tree's being-thought. But being-thought is being an ob-ject, standing there in front, in "ce premier dehors qu'est l'objectivité." "Le sujet ne s'oppose pas à l'objet mais à l'étant," making it what stands before. "Le sujet, c'est l'objet comme tel," the *cogitatum qua cogitatum*. And the standing-before of the object is what lets it show itself, its appearing — the tree's cogito (pp. 93–94). Chapter I's formula (the subject is opposed not to the object but to the entity) is derived here from the internal analysis of *cogitatum qua cogitatum*.

Henry turns to the cogito's historical destiny, especially Kant, who weighs so heavily on historical phenomenology, on Heidegger more than Husserl. For Kant, *cogito* is the subject, considered remarkably apart from its being, every passage from *cogito* to *sum* being declared a paralogism. This pure subject, reduced to "I think," is nothing closed on itself: its structures are those of objectivity, standing-before, deployed through the pure intuitions of space and time and the categories, so that forms and categories are conditions of phenomena only insofar as they make visible — and they do so because standing-before inhabits them all (p. 94). Kant is the transition from the Cartesian cogito to Heidegger: a subject without being that is the form of objectivity.

To be placed before is to be represented. To say the "I think" is the subject of representation is to say representation makes visible — it is cogito, *cogitatio*. As making visible, representation also founds being: "Être, c'est être représenté." Heidegger suggests this all comes from Aristotle when, discussing the rest characteristic of Aristotle's *Physics*, he writes that the house standing there *is* by standing out in its aspect, exposed in the unconcealed, resting "in this outside of its look" (*in diesem Aus des Aus-sehens*) (p. 94). The citation lets Henry tie Heidegger's own Greek ontology to the Kantian representation it supposedly opposes: the "out" of the look is the same outside.

Projecting this reading of Aristotle back onto Kant suffices to understand the essence of Descartes' cogito as Heidegger reads it. *Cogito*, by Heidegger's repeated statements, means "I represent to myself" and only that. Its decisive trait is the link to certainty: the cogito emerges from doubt, but doubt aims at the indubitable. What is certain? Two things. The tree — not in itself but as represented, placed before, dis-posed, exposed in the openness of a fundamental Outside. And this ecstatic dimension itself, so that whatever is gathered in it is certain. "Le disposé ne souffre plus du doute quant à ce qu'il est et à la manière dont il est" (*das Zugestellte [...] keinen Zweifel mehr zulässt*); "Le re-présenter/poser devant est un poser en toute sûreté" (*das Vor-stellen ist ein Sicher-stellen*) (*Nietzsche* II; pp. 94–95). The German wordplay *Vor-stellen / Sicher-stellen* encapsulates Heidegger's thesis that certainty is a mode of positing; Henry will deny that Descartes' certainty is any kind of positing.

But what Descartes would establish securely is not only the *cogitatum* but the cogito and its ego, man securing himself and grounding his mastery over all he represents, preparing the will to power and unleashed technology. Heidegger's extraordinary concern to ground the ego Descartes passed over so quickly — which should have led him deeper than any other commentator — is deceptive. His account of the ego "n'ajoute en effet rien à la structure de la représentation," to which it leads back as what the *ego cogito* is identical with (p. 95). Chapter IV's argument is now summarized.

Henry restates Heidegger's derivation: every "I represent something" represents "me" too; every human representing is "a self-representing." Not that I represent myself beside the tree as a marginal object, but that every represented is represented to me, before me, toward me. The self is presupposed a priori as the representer before whom everything represented will be. This retro-reference implies that the self dis-poses itself to itself in all representation. Because this self-disposal structures all representation, there is, Heidegger adds "magistrally," no passage from cogito to *sum*; the "I am" is implied in the cogito as "I represent myself" and identical with it (pp. 95–96). Henry's "magistrally" is not ironic: he accepts that, for the cogito–*cogitatum*, Heidegger's analysis is correct.

Henry had said Heidegger did not understand a word of the cogito. Should this be reversed — was he the only one to understand it? Yes, regarding the cogito–*cogitatum*. And this explains why Heidegger always expounded so profoundly what he criticized: "parce qu'il pense la même chose que ce qu'il critique" (p. 96). His critique of representation has only limited significance: it denies man, as the "I" of "I represent," the right to become master of nature, of what is only as represented — unlike the Greek listening to what comes about. But if, faced with the opposition between the moderns' *Gegenstand* and the ancients' listening *Gegenüber*, we ask the fundamental phenomenological question of pure phenomenality, the opposition dissolves: the same appearing founds the Greek *phainomenon* and the modern phenomenon, the one that lights up in Ek-stasis, from which "I represent" borrows its power to manifest by placing-before. This is Henry's most compact statement of his relation to Heidegger: depth of repetition is the sign of sameness, and the epochal differences of the history of being are variations within one mode of appearing.

The proof of this abolition of any fundamental phenomenological difference between Greek and modern phenomenology is Heidegger's own history of metaphysics, which establishes the continuity of the transformations of "ce seul et unique apparaître" of which the cogito as cogito–*cogitatum* is one avatar (pp. 96–97). Heidegger's history of being, which insists on epochal breaks, is cited as evidence of a deeper unity.

Back to the analysis. *Cogito cogitatum* is the structure of thought unfolding in the thought-process that aims at thought's essence. Is it not also the essence of thought in general? So it has been understood: every cogito is *cogito cogitatum*, consciousness of something. All modern phenomenology and philosophy have adopted this self-evident interpretation. Heidegger, who criticizes the cogito, and Husserl, who invokes it, say exactly the same: Heidegger, that all willing, all stances, all "affects," "feelings" and "sensations" relate to something willed, felt, experienced; Husserl (*Crisis* §20), that another term for intentionality is *cogitatio*, "avoir conscience de quelque chose," something I experience, think, feel or will (p. 97). The structure of the thought-process extends indefinitely to define thought in general and the essence of all that is, on the basis of a phenomenological presupposition governing the whole history of Western philosophy. What, then, does the opposed statement "cogito without *cogitatum*" mean? Henry's pairing of the two quotations shows the agreement extends to affects: both thinkers make feeling intentional, which is exactly the point Henry denies.

The statement clearly expresses a reduction, bracketing the *cogitatum* to consider only the cogito. Is this not Husserl's founding reduction? Whatever its modifications — whether it keeps to the certain "immanent" data of consciousness from which the object is constituted or concerns itself increasingly with the transcendental modalities of constitution — it has always divided phenomena of consciousness reputed certain from those constituted in it and not certain. These "phenomena of consciousness" relative to constituted objectities are the cogito opposed to the *cogitatum*. Since the former constitutes the latter, it allows the latter to be recovered, grounded and legitimated, so that the reduction loses nothing (pp. 97–98). Henry concedes the resemblance in order to mark the difference: Husserl's reduction keeps the *cogitatum* as constituted; Descartes' strikes it.

But bracketing the *cogitatum* to keep the cogito has become impossible after the preceding analysis. So long as one naively takes the *cogitatum* as something and the cogito facing it as something else, one can strike the one and keep the other. But once one sees that the *cogitatum qua cogitatum* is the cogito, appearing as such, striking the *cogitatum* suppresses appearing itself, plunges into "un Abîme inconcevable, celui d'un néant absolu" (p. 98). This is the Abyss before which Descartes' cogito — doubt, the radical reduction — places us for an instant. Expressed as "cogito without *cogitatum*," this reduction strikes not only the *cogitatum* but the cogito itself, the whole statement "*cogito cogitatum*." What can remain? This is Descartes' question. The reduction, rightly understood, is more radical than Husserl's because it suspends not the world but the appearing of the world — and so seems to leave nothing.

The radical reduction, Cartesian doubt, unfolds in three essential moments that are not stages of a problematic but point secretly to what it aims at. First, it puts out of play the entity, every conceivable entity, the material body of nature, my body, myself as entity. No longer can one say with Heidegger that in Descartes man "becomes certain of himself as the entity that posits itself on itself," that the Meditations remain within the question of *ens qua ens*, that in the history opened by the cogito truth about the entity becomes certainty, that "the ego is the truest entity" (*Nietzsche* II). With respect to the cogito without *cogitatum*, henceforth "the original cogito," such propositions have lost all sense (pp. 98–99). The first moment refutes the ontic reading of the cogito that Heidegger's history requires.

If the Cartesian reduction strikes all entities globally without considering them individually, it can only be because it first puts out of play their common condition, the appearing in which each shows itself as phenomenon and only so "is." This appearing is held doubtful, and only so is everything that shows itself in it disqualified, a priori. "La réduction ontique est donc en réalité une réduction phénoménologique." This is the second moment, the unheard-of moment in which appearing itself is set aside (p. 99). Henry explains the formal feature of doubt — its globality — by its object: one can doubt everything at once only by doubting the medium.

Two remarks. First: the phenomenological, not ontic, character of the reduction explains its sudden extension. Limited at first to what there is some reason to doubt, the sensible, it suddenly seizes everything, including the indubitable rational truths. This is not madness but "simple nécessité eidétique": doubt now considers in appearance not what appears (that two and two make four) but the appearing of the appearance. Once this appearing is doubtful — once the seeing that shows me 2+2=4 is deceptive as seeing, its making-visible a distortion — what it shows is surely not as seen and perhaps not at all. The same holds of the seeing that shows me the world or my dreams. Because a single seeing, deceptive in itself, is at work, everything it shows — world, dreams, rational truths — is delirious phantasmagoria. The evil genius hypothesis implies no rupture, no sudden appeal to an infinite will of unknown nature: "L'hypothèse du Malin Génie est phénoménologique, c'est la réduction elle-même, hypothèse de la fausseté de l'apparaître" (pp. 99–100). The reading defuses the standard complaint that the evil genius is an arbitrary metaphysical fiction: it is simply the hypothesis that appearing deceives.

Second remark: the presupposition that appearing is false, even if not that of absolute Nothing, ruins all possible knowledge and phenomenology especially, since phenomenology always sought to ground itself on appearing. If one keeps strictly to this aim, how could appearing be false? Keeping strictly to appearing here means not keeping to what appears as it appears, but to pure appearing as appearing — to light as it shines. Who could doubt appearing in the actuality of its appearing, the brightness of light in its shining? Yet Descartes does — the third moment of his reduction, intelligible only at a point of radicality that seems to make it impossible (p. 100). Henry here pits Descartes against the principle of all principles: what gives itself in intuition, insofar as it gives itself, could still be false if the giving is a seeing.

Which appearing does Descartes contest, against the force inherent in any appearing that actually appears? Seeing. "Je suppose donc que toutes les choses que je vois sont fausses" (AT VII 24) — sensible seeing being the first example of what functions everywhere as a seeing, as sense, imagination, memory, conception, on the ground of a transcendental seeing of which the *intueri* of the intellect is the refined form. That what is in question is the essence of seeing, its appearing, the *cogito cogitatum*, the placing-before — "in full security," said Heidegger — the Outside in which this placing occurs, ek-static truth, the "hors de moi" where there is "peut-être rien du tout" (AT VII 34), is announced at the end of the reduction by the abrupt return to sensible seeing, which everyone understands and no one contests insofar as he sees — because, as Husserl says, seeing is an absolute that "ne se laisse pas démontrer ni déduire" (*Idea of Phenomenology*; pp. 100–101). With the bar placed on this "outside me," are we not before the Abyss of Nothing? What remains? Henry's choice of Husserl's phrase is pointed: the absoluteness of seeing that Husserl takes as foundation is exactly what Descartes suspends.

Fortunately Descartes answered precisely. "*At certe videre videor*" (AT VII 29). At the height of the reduction, when the bar is on seeing, seeing itself remains indubitable. More exactly: seeing as such is doubtful (and so everything seen), but "it seems to me that I see" is absolutely true. This is possible only if the appearing in which seeing sees what it sees — *cogito cogitatum* — differs in essence from the appearing in which seeing is given to itself, in which "it seems to me that I see." Only so can the first be false and the second true (p. 101). The logical structure is a reductio: if both appearings were the same, both would be equally doubtful.

What does it mean for these two appearings to differ in essence? To differ in their very appearing, in the pure phenomenological matter of which appearing is made. Here, appearing is ecstatic phenomenality, ecstatic truth, and it is false; there, it is feeling, self-feeling — cogito without *cogitatum*, "apparaître écrasé contre soi, immergé en soi, submergé par soi," whose power of revelation is accomplished in pure suffering and enjoying of oneself before a world rises, before any Outside, before the space of light in which the *cogitatum* unfolds (p. 101). Material phenomenology (Chapter III) supplies the criterion: two appearings differ in what they are made of — light versus pathos.

The radical reduction is thus phenomenological in an ultimate sense: with unequalled violence, seemingly close to madness, it strikes appearing itself and contests it in its very appearing, splitting it "tel un bloc de cristal brisé à coups de marteau." On one side it finds an appearing that no longer carries its self-legitimation and self-attestation: seeing, held doubtful in the light of its vision and the very incontestability of that light. On the other, the appearing in which seeing is given to itself without *cogitatum* and without its light, without seeing itself or being able to, "dans la Nuit d'un non-voir absolu," which is absolutely certain (pp. 101–102). Why can the reduction reduce the first and not the second? Stranger still, the first is the light everyone sees, in which Being is immediately shown, which each bears as his "consciousness" or Dasein, while no one has ever seen the second or ever will. The paradox — the visible is doubtful, the invisible certain — is Henry's definition of the reversal of classical phenomenology. "Consciousness" and "Dasein" are equated as names for the first appearing.

We reach the heart of Descartes' meditation: "je ne me puis empêcher de croire que les choses corporelles [...] ne soient plus distinctement connues que cette je ne sais quelle partie de moi-même qui ne tombe point sous l'imagination," though it is strange that things doubtful and remote be known more clearly than those true and certain, belonging to my own nature (AT VII 29; p. 102). Descartes' "strange" registers exactly the paradox Henry just formulated, which the wax analysis will try to dissolve.

If what stands before me in incontestable clarity is struck by the radical reduction while what withdraws into my night alone remains certain, there can be only three reasons: there is a duality of appearing, differing each time in the matter of its pure phenomenality; the first is doubtful and the second not, the second founding the first, installing it in its being and its indubitability; the second alone is autonomous and absolute, the first merely founded. "Ces trois thèses composent ensemble le cartésianisme fondamental délimité par la réduction" (p. 102). The three theses — duality, foundation, autonomy — are the axioms of Henry's own phenomenology since *L'essence de la manifestation*, here ascribed to "fundamental Cartesianism."

A single text contains three of these theses and affirms the duality of appearing according to whether its matter is the ecstatic light of seeing (perceiving) or pathos. Seeing's doubtfulness lies in its inability to decide whether what it shows exists or belongs to dream. What is in question is not the perceived, imaginary or dreamt entity but, different from the entity and from its being, "ce je ne sais quoi qui se dérobe à l'Ek-stase, une Archi-révélation sise dans la chair impressionnelle du pathos." This Arch-revelation can occur even if the "world" were not, and remains certain even if all ecstatic truth were deceptive. Henry quotes *Passions* §26 once more (p. 103). "Impressional flesh" is a term from Henry's late vocabulary (*Incarnation*, 2000): the 1996 revision places the Cartesian passion in the flesh, not in the body.

The last thesis remains: that pathos alone installs seeing in seeing and gives it certainty, a certainty seeing never holds from its seeing but from its pathetic character. This character not only constantly determines seeing relative to what it sees — as desire, anxiety, love — but first makes it possible "en tant qu'un voir s'auto-affectant, s'auto-éprouvant, en tant que voir vivant et de cette façon seulement : en tant que non-voir" (p. 103). Affect determines seeing twice: empirically, by colouring what is seen; transcendentally, by giving seeing to itself.

Only this non-seeing, immanent to all seeing as pathetic self-affection, reveals seeing originally to itself and installs it in certainty, outside all evidence. And only because this ultimate ground escapes all evidence does doubt against evidence, instead of reaching it, uncover it as the sole ground. From the pathetic self-affection of every ek-stasis follow the massive theses that end the Second Meditation: the givenness of every conceivable objectity presupposes the self-givenness of this givenness, which differs structurally from ecstatic givenness in that, "ignorant tout horizon, tout aspect, tout déroulement temporel, elle est absolue," while the latter is finite like the place of light it opens (pp. 103–104). Descartes expressed the absoluteness of this Arch-givenness by one of the most powerful litotes in the language: the nature of the mind "est plus aisée à connaître que le corps." Henry reads the title of the Second Meditation as an understatement: the soul is not merely easier to know but known in an absolute mode that knowledge of the body can never reach.

What is the soul? Can the problem of *sum*, passed over so far, be avoided? Descartes says *cogito ergo sum*, or better *cogito, sum*. Is this not where Heidegger's final objection is legitimate — that the cogito is not original, resting on an unclarified presupposition, being, since I could never say "I am" without an implicit pre-understanding of the meaning of being in general? Henry returns to the objection already met in Chapter III, now to answer it from the analysis of reduction.

Thinking the meaning of being in general always proceeds from the entity: thinking the entity as entity — *ens qua ens*, as Heidegger said of the Meditations — and so, at least confusedly, being as such. But from its first step the radical reduction put the entity, and with it being, out of play, as the reply to Gassendi attests: I could not say "I am a thinking thing," Gassendi objected, unless I knew what an animal or plant is and so "ce qu'on nomme une chose" (AT VII 362; p. 104). Henry turns Heidegger's argument into Gassendi's: both require prior understanding of being as such, and Descartes' reply to Gassendi answers Heidegger in advance.

Where then does Descartes get *sum*, if not from what is or from being itself? From the cogito. *Sum* in no way means a being like that of the tree or animals, or "being itself"; all that is ontic and ontological has been suspended. And what is this being of the tree that would also be mine? It is, in Fichte's words that hold the destiny of Western thought, "son être en dehors de son être" (*Way to the Blessed Life*): the tree in its exteriority to itself, *cogitatum*, ob-ject (pp. 104–105). The being of the tree is its appearing as exteriority of the Outside. Being is always appearing, but here appearing has thrown what appears outside itself and discovers it only as other, foreign, external, unreal, "étant mort dont l'intériorité vivante échappe à jamais." Fichte's formula (being as existence, *Dasein* as being outside being) serves to define world-being as self-exteriority, the contrary of life.

The cogito from which Descartes draws *sum* proceeds from the radical reduction that suspended the entity, its being and being itself. Phenomenologically, the reduction suspended the appearing that throws outside itself, the cogito whose *cogitatio* is the *cogitatum qua cogitatum*. From it proceeds the original cogito — not the tree's appearing in naive perception and the worldhood of a world but "l'Archi-révélation, l'Archi-affection en laquelle s'auto-affecte toute affection possible," including every ecstatic affection by an entity. This Arch-affection is autonomous — "ni étant ni être mais une Archi-facticité" generated in the Arch-revelation of this Arch-affection and identical with it. "*Sum* désigne cette Archi-facticité" (p. 105). The prefix *Archi-* marks what precedes the distinction between entity and being. *Sum* is not an ontological claim but names the fact of self-revelation.

What fact is this? "Le pur fait d'apparaître considéré en lui-même," appearing's appearing insofar as it constitutes the being of being, what makes it be — insofar as one can still speak of being, since it is neither what is nor its being (p. 105). Henry's caution on "being" marks the distance from ontology: life is prior to the ontological difference.

In the radicalized reduction, the pure fact of appearing was reduced, the bar placed on the appearing that appears as the ecstatic Truth of Being. Why? Because such appearing does not bring itself into appearing, does not produce its own appearing; left to itself it would not appear, would not be the pure fact of appearing, "l'Archi-fait qui doit porter tous les autres." Only its pathetic self-affection can bring the ecstatic appearing of Being into itself; only this Arch-affection as Arch-revelation makes appearing a "fact" — the Arch-facticity Descartes names *sum*, the *sum* of the cogito whose phenomenological matter is a pure affectivity, revealing in its own affective flesh, outside and independently of ecstatic Being (pp. 105–106). A long footnote adds that neither Husserl nor Heidegger saw this, despite their seemingly divergent readings: in both, the appearing of the cogito, reduced to that of the *cogitatum qua cogitatum*, can appear to itself only through a doubling that underlines rather than removes its initial incapacity. Husserl: Descartes, having established the evidence of *cogitatio*, asks what assures him of these data — "*la clara et distincta perceptio*" (*Idea of Phenomenology*). Heidegger: Descartes pronounces *ego cogito sum* "en tant qu'une connaissance claire, précise et indubitable" (*Nietzsche* II 120). On the illusion of entrusting to a second *cogitatio* a power of self-revelation the first lacks, Henry refers to "La méthode phénoménologique" in *Phénoménologie matérielle*. The footnote shows the two readers converging in clear and distinct perception — the doubling that Henry's self-affection makes unnecessary.

When Descartes tried to say, at the height of the reduction in the passage on the evil genius, the Arch-connection of Arch-revelation and Arch-facticity, he wrote: "la pensée est un attribut qui m'appartient : elle seule ne peut être détachée de moi" — *haec sola a me divelli nequit* (AT VII 27) — "comme si c'était en effet dans l'immanence radicale qui rejette toute Ek-stase que se reproduisent identiquement la révélation la plus originelle et ce que, comme effet de cette auto-affection [...] nous appelons 'moi'" (p. 106). A footnote adds the companion question — is there any of these attributes (doubting, conceiving, willing, imagining, sensing) that can be distinguished from my thought or separated from me (AT VII 29) — and refers to *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*, chapters I–III, for the immanence of this self-affection. "Cannot be torn from me" is read literally: inseparability is the absence of the distance that ek-stasis would introduce, and the "me" is the effect of self-affection — anticipating the thesis that the ego is generated in life.

In a recent work Jean-Luc Marion stated what Henry calls the fourth principle of phenomenology: "d'autant plus de réduction, d'autant plus de donation" (*Réduction et donation*, p. 303 ff.; cf. *Phénoménologie de la vie* I). Unlike ontology or metaphysics, its avatar, this principle can lead us back to Descartes' abyssal intuition. At the height of the reduction, with the entity and the givenness that gives it struck out, there is given "hors d'être," outside horizon and finitude, apart from any aspect or profile, the Arch-givenness: self-givenness in which life gives itself immediately and wholly to itself — "omni-donation" that gives everything insofar as everything is given in life, not only life but every ecstatic affection too (pp. 106–107). Henry appropriates Marion's principle (discussed at length in *De la phénoménologie*) but bends it: maximal reduction yields not the call or the saturated phenomenon but life's self-givenness. "Hors d'être" echoes Marion's *Dieu sans l'être*.

What arises in life's self-affection, generated in it — what we call our soul — has nothing to do with what we see and perceive as entity. "La vie a d'autres façons." The radical reduction necessarily opens onto them, and elucidating them is the task of a radical phenomenology. That Descartes then took other paths does not matter here. "Point de départ de cette phénoménologie radicale, le cogito peut en être aujourd'hui le recommencement" (p. 107). The close repeats the conclusion of Chapter II — phenomenology must begin again — and identifies the beginning with the cogito, now read as the first statement of a phenomenology of life.

## VI. Schopenhauer: A First Philosophy

By "first philosophy" Henry means a philosophy in the style of the radical problematic of Descartes' Meditations: one that returns to the root of things, their ultimate ground, and, possessing it as the ground of all conceivable reality, can understand everything that is. The ambition seems colossal, beyond human strength, yet it was Schopenhauer's even more than Descartes', who was keenly aware of the certainty but also the finitude of our knowledge. Understanding everything from its ground presupposes that the ground is unique, the same for all that is, and that it can first ground itself, epistemologically and ontologically. Schopenhauer fascinated philosophers, a wider public and most great creators of the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries because his thought offered itself as a gaze that pierces multiform, changing, disconcerting appearance to grasp its one, ever-identical principle, "un principe d'intelligibilité vraiment radical [...] auquel rien [...] n'échapperait plus" (pp. 109–110). The two conditions — a unique ground that grounds itself — are the criteria by which Schopenhauer will be judged and found wanting.

A further trait of first philosophy: the principle is not something one rises to gradually, nor does it come into possession of itself only at the end of a required route. Immediacy — "la fulguration d'une révélation initiale" and the identity of being with it — is contained in the idea of first philosophy, which satisfies its concept only when it holds the primitive Phenomenon in which being brings itself into appearing from the start, unveiling its own inner essence and that of everything. But if the principle is immediately in itself and we immediately in it, then it is in us, in our inner being identical with the inner being of being, that the Absolute flashes; in what we are we read the secret of the universe. Descartes speaks of a "sorte de connaissance intérieure qui précède l'acquise" (AT VII 422). Because this knowledge is not of an external reality but self-knowledge — reality knowing itself as it is — a radical thought is possible that, returning to this place of all origins, discovers at once the reality of things, ours and the universe's. Schopenhauer believed he had found this place, which gives his thought the tone and illuminating power of a first philosophy (p. 110). Henry thus defines first philosophy by self-revelation, aligning Schopenhauer's "inner" access to the thing in itself with the Cartesian *videor* of the previous chapters.

To find in oneself the principle of the whole universe means first not having to decipher it on things. The critique of exteriority, and so of every objective form of knowledge in its claim to instruct us about ourselves and the depths of being, is the fundamental thesis and distinctive trait of all first philosophy. Hence an enigma: how can such a philosophy speak to a culture living in the world of technology and Galilean science, marked by thoroughgoing objectivism? Perhaps objectivism itself holds the key: precisely because the inner principle of all things — "la vie invisible et irreprésentable" — is today more than ever repressed, cast out of a universe given over to instrumental devices and self-generating objective processes, the need for what has been lost is felt more cruelly. The return, in the positivist age, of a vitalist philosophy in various, mostly confused forms, only circumscribes "l'immense vide que la pensée de Schopenhauer et, à sa suite, la psychanalyse s'efforcent de combler" (p. 111). The essay's verdict is foreshadowed: Schopenhauer and psychoanalysis answer a real need, but in a way that, by its own failure, deepens the void.

For Schopenhauer the critique of exteriority is the critique of representation. He does not ignore its power or the extent of the field it opens, coextensive with the world: "Le monde est ma représentation" (MVR — Burdeau's French translation of *The World as Will and Representation* — p. 25). This definition has a philosophical, phenomenological and ontological meaning. Naively, the world is the sum of entities. To say it is my representation is to stop taking this totality for granted and to raise to a problem what lets it be — each entity's being-represented, considered as such. Because the entity "is" only as represented, it offers itself as ob-ject. Its being-object, its being-represented, is what Kant thematized in the transcendental philosophy of the subject. The subject's being is the object's being-object, what lets the entity be by entering the condition of phenomenon. Schopenhauer, steeped in Kant, understands this transcendental meaning to the end: subject and object are opposed only in naive pedagogical presentations of idealism; they belong to one structure, representation. "Le sujet ne s'oppose pas à l'objet en vérité, il s'oppose à l'étant" — it is the objective condition as such. Henry quotes: the split into object and subject "est la forme primitive essentielle et commune à toute représentation" (MVR, p. 52); "Le sujet est posé en même temps que l'objet et réciproquement" (p. 61) (pp. 111–112). Henry's formula from Chapters I, II and V is here found in Schopenhauer himself, who therefore cannot place the essence of reality on the side of the subject of representation.

A last trait: the subject so understood is exhausted in representation, its phenomenological reality being nothing but that of the entity, objectivity. What shows itself, what is known, is the object — the objective condition — not the subject as other than this exteriority. "Le sujet connaissant comme tel ne saurait être connu" (MVR, p. 898); it "connaît tout [...] sans être connu lui-même" (p. 27). This "zone d'ombre," this focus of darkness at the centre of the luminous sphere of the world, begins to be understood when one turns to the fundamental theses of the major work (p. 112). The unknowable knowing subject is Schopenhauer's version of the anonymity Henry found in Husserl (Chapter II), and the "darkness at the centre" will turn out to be the will.

Fundamental thesis: nothing given in the representation that is the world is real; nothing there is will, which concentrates the whole of reality. But if the will contains and exhausts all actual reality, what does representation represent? Schopenhauer's answer is unequivocal. Because the will is the principle of all reality — not a cause producing it, of which reality would be the product, but its inner essence, so that wherever reality unfolds, the will has already unfolded wholly — the reality represented in the world is that of the will, nothing else (p. 113). Henry stresses that "principle" means essence, not cause, to block the reading of the will as a hidden producer behind the world.

How can representation represent the will — show it and seemingly give it — while not showing or giving it? Representation must be a mere representation, not giving reality in itself but an equivalent — an image, a double, an ersatz that stands for it without being it, "comme la photo de Pierre représente Pierre quand il n'est pas là." That representation does not and cannot give reality in itself marks it as a power of principled derealization, ultimately a kind of imagination, producing only images of things, never things as they are (p. 113). A footnote adds that this is why Schopenhauer could identify the world of representation with a dream forming "the leaves of the same book," interpret Kantian phenomenalism, to the purists' scandal, as a kind of illusionism, and invoke Plato and India — the shadow, the veil of Maya. Henry endorses the derealizing character of representation — it is his own thesis about ek-static appearing — and uses Schopenhauer's "scandalous" illusionism as evidence.

Why is representation only a mere representation, a faculty of the unreal? Because it gives in exteriority, as ob-ject, and reality — the reality defined by the will, all possible reality — is never given so (pp. 113–114). The unreality of the world is not an empirical deficiency but follows from its mode of givenness.

This teaches more about the will as reality. If unreality belongs to a mode of givenness — placing the entity outside itself in the exteriority of the world, offering only an image, an aspect, the face it turns toward us while hiding its inner reality — then the only reality, the will's, also refers to a mode of manifestation: Henry italicizes, "elle n'est réelle que sous la condition d'un pouvoir originel de révélation qui la révèle non plus comme une apparence extérieure, comme une image muette, sans épaisseur et sans vie, mais en elle-même et telle qu'elle est" (p. 114). This is the decisive step of Henry's reading: if reality is defined by contrast with a mode of appearing, it must itself be a mode of appearing, not a thing behind appearing.

Like the world of representation, the will cannot have a merely ontic meaning, designating a type of entity — one that always wills, inhabited by an unsatisfied and unsatisfiable desire because no object can end its quest. It is true that in Schopenhauer there is no *bonum supremum*, no absolute uniting with which the being of willing, laminated by lack of being and constituted by it, could quench its thirst. But such a being we can represent, and so it appears in the world, as "toutes ces bouches, de tous ces sexes, de ces griffes et de ces dents," hurled at a prey they pursue without rest and never obtain: their satisfaction (p. 114). The rhetoric of mouths and teeth is Schopenhauer's; Henry's point is that this vivid picture is a picture, belonging to representation and therefore not the will itself.

So long as the will floats before our gaze as this protean, ever-recommencing appearance, it is not real, reduced to a chaotic and menacing parade of images, like a dream or nightmare. More: and this is the cardinal thesis that makes it a first philosophy, the representation of willing not only casts it into unreality, but the play of appearances would be unable by itself to tell us that they are appearances of willing unless willing were revealed otherwise, in itself and as it is. Only a specific mode of appearing, other than representation, can give access both to the reality of willing and to the knowledge of what willing in general is — so that the reality of the will presupposes this mode of revelation and coincides with it (pp. 114–115). The argument is Henry's against all naturalism: the world cannot be read as will unless will is known otherwise than through the world.

This clarifies the enigmatic proposition of the title. The apparent asymmetry — representation designating a mode of appearing with ontological meaning, will seeming to designate something (drive, desire, *conatus*) with only ontic import — vanishes once one sees that will, opposed to representation, is not only the reality of which representation offers a hollow double but first "un mode de révélation," the one in which reality is accessible and in which one must stand to stand in reality. Texts confirm the ontological meaning: "ma volonté en tant que j'en ai conscience d'une manière toute différente [de la représentation intuitive] et qui ne souffre de comparaison avec aucune autre" (MVR, p. 152); "une autre manière [d'être connu] absolument différente et que désigne le mot volonté" (p. 152). "The world as will and representation" thus names two modes of appearing — Henry's duality of manifestation read into Schopenhauer's title.

In what does this mode consist? Negatively, rejecting representation is rejecting the subject/object pair that co-constitutes it, or rather identifies in it, the subject being what makes the entity an object, its objectivity, the transcendental exteriority where every world originates. That this exteriority is excluded from the will's being determines it positively as "une intériorité radicale" (pp. 115–116). Interiority designates a being no distance ever separates from itself, which embraces itself at every point, wholly itself at every instant. Such a being, coinciding with itself, which no one — not even itself — can separate or tear from itself, which cannot rid itself of itself, is not the dead being of tautology, A = A. "Bien plutôt est-il la vie, ce qui s'éprouve soi-même immédiatement, la subjectivité absolue" (p. 116). The contrast with A = A guards against reading self-coincidence as logical identity: life's identity is lived, an undergoing, not a formal relation.

That the immediacy in the will's interiority is an experience, appearing in its most original form, gives the concept of will its ultimate ontological meaning: designating a mode of revelation, it designates reality, since in this mode the will is not thrown outside itself into imaginary appearance but is in itself, where it feels and experiences itself immediately as it is — "un être tout entier connaissant et tout entier connu, indissolublement." Schopenhauer rises to a rapid but decisive elucidation: the concept of will is the only one that does not originate in phenomenon, in mere intuitive representation, "mais vienne du fond même de la conscience immédiate de l'individu, dans lequel il se reconnaisse lui-même, dans son essence, sans aucune forme, ni celle du sujet et de l'objet, attendu qu'ici le connaissant et le connu coïncident" (MVR, p. 151; p. 116). The coincidence of knower and known is the textual centre of Henry's affirmative reading; the rest of the essay shows Schopenhauer retracting it.

The split of reality into representation and will is not real; it affects not reality but its appearing. Because there are two heterogeneous modes of appearing, the one reality appears in two ways: outside us as unreal intuitive appearance; in us, in its reality, as what we are. The will is real insofar as we coincide with it where it coincides with itself, unreal in the world. Schopenhauer's extraordinary philosophy of the body, indebted to the French *Idéologues* and in some respects close to Maine de Biran's of fifteen years earlier, illustrates this. The body is the appearing of the will, opening us to the ultimate reality, the thing in itself, which Kant had relegated to the unknowable. "Le corps lève l'interdit." Escaping the discredit Western thought has laid on it since the origin, which consigned it to knowledge of appearance, the body lets us pierce appearance and places us at the heart of the will: its effort, willing and desire are the will's own willing in us (pp. 116–117). A footnote marks the difference with Biran: Biran gave his discovery of the subjective body a radical phenomenological foundation (Henry refers to his *Philosophie et phénoménologie du corps*). The comparison sets the standard Schopenhauer fails to meet.

If the body is the appearing of willing, it must, given the division of appearing, divide itself, offering a double appearance by the duplicity of its mode of manifestation. Schopenhauer's "abyssal" thesis: this body is given "de deux façons toutes différentes : d'une part comme représentation dans la connaissance phénoménale [...] ; et, d'autre part, en même temps comme ce principe immédiatement connu de chacun que désigne le mot Volonté" (MVR, p. 141; p. 117). In reality there is one body, one willing, one desire. It is in appearing, as not only the will's interiority but also representation, that this one body, mine, takes a double aspect: one aspect properly so called — the side of exteriority in which my objective body resembles others, a representation among those composing the world — and a "second side" that is no longer a side, presents no face to any gaze, "qui n'a plus de visage," given only in itself and to itself, where, coinciding with the force traversing it, it is one with it. "Faceless" echoes the conclusion of Chapter II: life "n'a pas de visage." The second side is not a hidden face but no face at all.

The dichotomy of appearing — the body as representation and as inwardly lived will — solves the problem of the action of soul on body, the cross of classical philosophy since Descartes. The original, absolutely immanent, absolutely subjective body that I am is nothing but the soul; and how it could act on the objective, represented body, its image in the world, need not be asked, since the external change in the intuitive body is not an effect of the soul's willing. There is one body, so any action of one on the other is impossible and expresses only a tautology. On the basis of the duplicity of appearing: every determination of the immanent force constituting our original subjective body and the reality of will in us is also given, as it is accomplished in us, in representation, as an objective displacement in space. Following the French *Idéologues*, Schopenhauer recognized this: "L'acte volontaire et l'action du corps ne sont pas deux phénomènes objectifs différents, reliés par la causalité [...] ils ne sont qu'un seul et même fait : seulement ce fait nous est donné de deux façons différentes [...] L'action du corps n'est que l'acte de la volonté objectivé" (MVR, p. 141; p. 118). The mind–body problem is dissolved not by a metaphysical identity theory but by the duality of appearing of one reality — the solution Henry credits to Biran and develops in *Incarnation*.

If there is a double appearance of the body, of the will, of the essence of things, which is decisive? Representation, which places in the front of a world as ob-ject, or the will that embraces itself in the night of abyssal subjectivity without light or world — which gives reality as it is and lets us know it is will? Schopenhauer: our body gives access to the thing in itself — not the objective body, a representation like others, unable to surpass the appearance it offers toward the world in itself. The meaning of representation, the "passage [...] à ce qu'il peut être en dehors de la représentation" (MVR, p. 141), only our original body gives, as no longer a mysterious representation but the immediate inner experience of the will it is identical with (pp. 118–119). The question of priority is the one Henry put to Descartes (Chapter V): duality, foundation, autonomy.

Because my body is given to me not only outside me in representation but first in me as what I am, as will — "mon corps est l'unique objet dont je ne connaisse pas uniquement l'un des côtés, celui de la représentation ; j'en connais aussi le second, qui est celui de la volonté" (MVR, p. 170) — I know the inner essence of things, and the veil lifts at once from all the hieroglyphs of the universe. Just as, my body being given twice, I know that the tireless willing I feel in me is the same that represents itself in my objective body and its spatial movements, so I divine that all the movements I see in the world — hands that grasp, eyes that see, voracious sexes and mouths so like mine — are traversed by the same desire that nothing fills and that ceaselessly begins again (p. 119). The analogical extension from my body to the world is the core of Schopenhauer's metaphysics; Henry accepts its form, but its validity depends entirely on the first-person knowledge being genuinely immediate.

The fundamental theses of a first philosophy — whose prototype is Descartes — impose themselves in their massive coherence. They are the theses of immediacy: there is no being or knowledge except by virtue of a parousia in which being embraces itself immediately. It follows that its ontological and phenomenological substantiality are the same, and that being and knowledge, as identical, are at the beginning. Because the self-experiencing of this embrace bears the essence of ipseity, this primitive "knowledge" is "connaissance de soi." The primacy of self-knowledge over all other, due to immediacy, gives first philosophy its ontological weight and power of intelligibility. "Chacun se connaît immédiatement soi-même [...] et n'a de tout le reste qu'une connaissance médiate" (MVR, p. 886). By this immediacy self-knowledge is the principle for understanding all nature (pp. 119–120). Ipseity follows from self-affection, as in Chapter IV: Henry generalizes his Cartesian result into a criterion of first philosophy.

The most general presuppositions of modern thought are thus overturned — those that feed its claim to scientificity but also its disarray, its loss of confidence in man, the destruction of true certainty in favour of the exhausted belief in the sole value of objectivist knowledge. Schopenhauer, taking up the torch of philosophical truth, says the contrary of the positivism that flattens modernity: far from being determined from the world, from scientific knowledge projected on it as on one object among others, the self is the starting point and condition of all inquiry: "C'est en partant de nous-même qu'il faut chercher à comprendre la nature, et non pas inversement chercher la connaissance de nous-même dans celle de la nature" (MVR, p. 891; p. 120). The citation supplies the slogan of Henry's anti-naturalism.

This immediate self-knowledge is knowledge of the will; hence "notre volonté nous fournit l'unique occasion d'arriver à l'intelligence intime d'un processus qui se présente à nous d'une manière objective," giving something immediately known and "pas, comme tout le reste, uniquement donné dans la représentation" (MVR, p. 891). Since the mediation of "all the rest" is representation, immediacy demands its radical exclusion: the will as thing in itself, revealing itself apart from representation, can do so only in and through itself, as constituting its own mode of revelation: the thing in itself can enter consciousness only immediately, "à savoir en ce sens qu'elle-même prendra conscience d'elle-même (*es selbst sich seiner bewusst wird*)" (MVR, p. 891; pp. 120–121). The German reflexive is the closest Schopenhauer comes to self-affection — the will becoming conscious of itself — and it is the formula he will immediately qualify.

One must take the measure of what is at stake to understand Schopenhauer's failure, his inability to meet the conditions he set: the ultimate phenomenological footing of the whole analysis. The possibility of self-knowledge grounding knowledge of the universe rests on the prior possibility of self-knowledge, on a clear apprehension of its essence. But this was worked out negatively. The ontological devaluation of representation, stripped of the power to exhibit reality, is decisive: if Western philosophy is a metaphysics of representation and the "Greek origin" its true presupposition, the whole conceptual development called "philosophy" is shaken — rightly, if in the exteriority of representation and the ek-stasis of being that supports it reality never reveals itself in itself and is recognizable in the world only if first experienced elsewhere, under an a priori knowledge Schopenhauer calls will (p. 121). Henry's praise of Schopenhauer's demolition of representation is real; the failure lies in the positive determination of the alternative.

This a priori knowledge of the will must not merely be asserted — "nous savons et nous comprenons mieux ce qu'est la volonté que tout ce qu'on voudra" (MVR, p. 154) — but positively determined. Faced with the immense task of promoting a thought other than the West's — "qui ne soit plus la pensée mais la vie" — Schopenhauer is helpless. So powerful are the presuppositions that have directed philosophy since Greece, inscribing phenomenality in the ek-stasis of being, that the deliberate attempt to escape their horizon remains caught in it. When the time comes to define the other of representation in its phenomenological positivity, Schopenhauer loses his bearings, abandons his postulates, falls into contradiction and absurdity — Henry italicizes — "faute de disposer d'un pouvoir de révélation autre que celui de la représentation extatique, c'est finalement à celle-ci qu'elle confie le soin de rendre manifeste ce qui lui échappe dans le principe" (pp. 121–122). The diagnosis is the same as for Descartes (Chapter III) and Freud (Chapter I): lacking a concept of non-ecstatic manifestation, the thinker reassigns the invisible to the visible.

The catastrophic reversal occurs when the will's phenomenological status, which cannot remain in suspense, is re-examined in chapter 18 (Henry's text says 17) of the Supplements to Book II. Barely are immediacy, the identity of the will's phenomenon with the will, and the immediate entry of the thing in itself into consciousness reaffirmed than the necessary precisions restrict and finally destroy them. Inner experience of willing was to coincide with the will's self-experience as thing in itself. Not quite: "cette perception intime [...] de notre propre volonté" cannot coincide with "la connaissance complète et adéquate de la chose en soi," because it is not "tout à fait immédiate." What does "not quite immediate" mean? That all knowledge implies a mediation with which it coincides, the distance that always separates us from the known. For to know is to see, to see is to see what is placed before, so that — Henry italicizes — "la position devant, la mise à distance qu'elle institue est la condition de la vision, la visibilité elle-même comme telle" (p. 122). Because representation achieves this placing-before, Kant takes it as the condition of all possible experience. "Not quite immediate" is the hinge of Henry's critique: Schopenhauer cannot conceive an immediate *knowledge* because knowledge for him means seeing.

Schopenhauer does not escape this ultimate presupposition, identifying phenomenality with ek-stasis, born in Greece, radicalized in Kant's metaphysics of representation and transmitted to him, even when he wants to withdraw reality from it. Hence mediation perverts the immediacy of knowing the will: if the will is to show itself, be seen and known, it must stand in this "before," this distance, however small, in which all knowledge and phenomenality consist (pp. 122–123). "However small" is pointed: the issue is not how close inner experience comes, but whether it involves any distance at all.

The theory of this "not quite immediate" immediate knowledge is then built (and unbuilt). Knowledge of the world is representation in Kant's full sense. Schopenhauer retains the a priori conditions — the forms of intuition and the categories — as the *principium individuationis*, composed of time, space and causality. The experience it opens, "the world as representation," is external experience; the inner experience of willing, "the intimate perception of the will," which is to give us the reality of things, is internal experience. How do they differ? The question prepares the reduction of inner experience to Kant's inner sense.

Internal experience excludes the forms of space and causality — but not of time. Schopenhauer's inner experience of the will is Kant's inner sense, an experience whose form is temporality itself. As experience of the will in inner sense, it gives, in place of what the will is in itself, its temporal appearances, the succession of acts in which it manifests itself, so that its pure essence, the phenomenality it is made of, is "cette première mise à distance en laquelle consiste le temps conformément à sa triple structuration ek-statique": the ek-stasis of the future, of the present as what stands before now, of the past as what I represent as "just past" and "further past" (p. 123). Henry's argument against Husserl in Chapter II returns: time is the first ek-stasis, so an experience in time cannot be immanent.

This is the upshot of Schopenhauer's elucidation of inner experience: retaining temporal representation, consisting indeed in this form, it does not exclude the oppositional structure of representation, the opposition of subject and object, but reproduces it. The experience of the will is then no longer the other of representation but its essence and most general ground, opposition as such being its unavoidable condition, "sa phénoménalité même." Book I's statement — the concept of will alone comes from immediate consciousness, "sans aucune forme, même celle du sujet et de l'objet, attendu qu'ici le connaissant et le connu coïncident" (MVR, p. 154) — is simply reversed when temporal representation reintroduces opposition into inner experience: "dans la connaissance de notre être interne aussi il y a une différence entre l'être en soi de l'objet de cette connaissance et la perception de cet être dans le sujet qui connaît" (p. 892; pp. 123–124). Henry sets Schopenhauer against himself, Book I against the Supplements, as he set Husserl against himself in Chapter II.

All the difficulties of classical thought, of the Kantian critique especially, are repeated at a stroke. The deep reality of the universe, identical with ours, is not only torn between subject and object of knowledge; knowledge immediately shows itself illusory, since the will that has become its object is not known in itself but only in the unreal appearance of a world, and the subject does not know itself either. So one must read the passage in which the philosophy of will succumbs under the weight of representation: in consciousness too "le moi n'est pas absolument simple mais il se compose d'une partie connaissante, l'intellect, et d'une partie connue, la volonté : le premier n'est pas connu, celle-ci ne connaît pas" (MVR, p. 892; p. 124). The split self — intellect unknown, will unknowing — is the exact contrary of the self as identity of affecting and affected (Chapter IV).

Vain, then, is the attempt to revalue inner experience, doomed by subsumption under representation to exteriority. One may say that despite its imperfections the perception of our own willing "est de beaucoup plus immédiate que toute autre perception," "le point où la chose en soi entre le plus immédiatement dans le phénomène" (MVR, p. 893); still, this phenomenon is not that of willing but of the subject in its difference from what it knows — "l'apparaître de la différence et de l'ek-stase." And there is no other: "mon intellect seul susceptible de connaître, est toujours distinct de moi comme volonté" (p. 893). The will is forever deprived of this sole mode of phenomenality; it has become again the unknown, unknowable thing in itself. To the question what the will is apart from its representation: "Cette question ne recevra jamais de réponse" (pp. 124–125). Schopenhauer could challenge the metaphysics of representation and its Greek concept of phenomenality only "pour sombrer dans une philosophie de la nuit." Instead of opening Western thought to a meditation on life, he set before it the crushing dilemma that would govern modernity: "ou la représentation ou l'inconscient." The dilemma connects this chapter with *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*, whose chapters on Schopenhauer it summarizes: the Freudian unconscious inherits the will's exile from phenomenality.

Two of the dilemma's many consequences are retained: the ruin of first philosophy in Schopenhauer and the perversion of the concept of life. The ruin of first philosophy is that of the philosophy of the body and can be read in it. For Schopenhauer the body is not merely one representation among others; the objective body is secondary, showing itself as what it is — traversed by insatiable appetite — only as representation of the will, its manifestation in the world: "Ce corps entier n'est que la volonté objectivée c'est-à-dire devenue perceptible" (MVR, p. 141). But according to Book I, representation is blind, unable to deliver its meaning or surpass itself toward the reality it represents. As one representation among others, the objective body can be given as objectified will only on condition of a prior experience of the will in itself. Only if we know what will is can the objective body appear as its objectification: "La volonté est la connaissance *a priori* du corps ; le corps est la connaissance *a posteriori* de la volonté" (MVR, p. 141; pp. 125–126). The formula states the correct order of foundation; the next unit shows Schopenhauer inverting it.

*A posteriori* means: insofar as we experience the will in us and coincide with it in that coincidence which is our absolutely immanent, subjective body, we can know that the objective body is its representation. But once this a priori knowledge of the will's being in itself is denied, the will manifests itself only through its temporal phenomenal forms, which are those of our representational body and its acts as objective displacements. The represented body becomes the will's only possible manifestation and the condition of knowing it. Schopenhauer's thought closes in a circle: a priori condition of knowledge of the represented body, the will now finds in it the condition of its own knowledge — "la connaissance que j'ai de ma volonté, bien qu'immédiate, est inséparable de celle que j'ai de mon corps [...] aussi mon corps est-il la condition de la connaissance de ma volonté" (MVR, p. 143, Henry's italics). This inversion of "inner" and "acquired" knowledge — Descartes' terms — carries away the claims of first philosophy. When the immediate experience of the will in the original body is discredited, the whole construction collapses: having asked the will for the world's secret, one now finds the world proposed as the only testimony we can have about the will: "la seule conscience générale d'elle-même qu'ait la volonté est la représentation totale, l'ensemble du monde qu'elle aperçoit" (MVR, p. 216; pp. 126–127). The use of Descartes' vocabulary ties the failure to the Third Meditation's reversal in Chapter III.

Graver than the dismissal of first philosophy is the alteration of the concept of life, which Schopenhauer nonetheless brilliantly introduced into a tradition that defined man by thought as object-consciousness, knowledge, reason. The rejection of the metaphysics of representation meant replacing an external relation to being, which empties it of itself, with "l'étreinte pathétique en laquelle il s'éprouve lui-même," a self-affection that is life. Either life is taken for what it is, absolute subjectivity, so that everything that befalls it in this self-affection without distance, light or world — all primitive forms of experience — bears the seal of an original phenomenality consisting in life's pathetic embrace of itself, in a "transcendental affectivity" that makes them forms of experience; or, if the phenomenological status of the will, its immediate experience in the immanent body, has not been recognized, as in Schopenhauer, then all life and its fundamental modalities — force, desire, affect — are lost in the obscurity of a noumenal back-world: "dans l'inconscient!" Unrepresentable life is torn from the oblivion of the metaphysics of representation only to suffer a more systematic, because deliberate, repression, casting it outside all conceivable phenomenality (p. 127). A footnote notes that Schopenhauer spoke at length of affectivity, which, as suffering especially, fills his philosophy, but never interpreted it as the mode of revelation of the will's being in itself, its very phenomenality (cf. *Généalogie*, chs. 5–6). This is the precise point of failure: Schopenhauer had the affective material in hand but treated it as content rather than as the mode of self-revelation.

But the principle cannot be left wholly indeterminate. If it is the naturing of all reality and must explain it, something must be said about it. Schopenhauer's discourse is then purely negative. Since the will is the other of representation, its essence can be reconstructed antithetically by negating all features of the phenomenal world. If that world is structured by the *principium individuationis*, the a priori forms that everywhere produce diversity — "c'est par l'intermédiaire de l'espace et du temps que ce qui est un et semblable dans son essence [...] nous apparaît comme différent, comme plusieurs" (MVR, p. 155) — the will, escaping it, must be "what is one and the same in its essence," grounding the inference that all natural phenomena manifest one willing, and that the force I feel in me is identical with all the forces that tear the universe. If the phenomenal world obeys the principle of sufficient reason, the will is groundless, without reason, absurd. If the phenomenal world is that of knowledge, all knowledge being the making-visible of a placing-before, the will escaping the world is foreign to knowledge: unknown, unknowing, unknowable, its mode of being blindness (pp. 127–128). Henry shows that Schopenhauer's famous attributes of the will are derived by negation from Kant's forms of experience, not from any experience of will.

Here is the decisive event on which Schopenhauer's pessimism is built "de façon totalement illégitime": *Vielheitlosigkeit* (absence of plurality), *Grundlosigkeit* (groundlessness), *Erkenntnislosigkeit* (absence of knowledge), *Ziellosigkeit* (aimlessness) — purely negative determinations whose theoretical function is to forbid applying world-discourse to the will — surreptitiously receive positive meaning and claim to tell us the essence of things. Deprived of reason, the will becomes an unleashed force ravaging the universe; deprived of aim, an endless effort recommencing what it does, as in gravity — "effort continuel joint à l'impossibilité d'atteindre le but" (MVR, p. 215) — plant growth and animal procreation; one in itself yet appearing diverse, it fills the universe with intersecting processes that clash everywhere, conflicting with itself millions of times, pushing to the extreme the absurdity of a world given over to suffering. Thus negative determinations resulting from the system's failure to assign the will a phenomenological status turn into a seemingly positive analysis, "une fresque grandiose et terrible" that gave Schopenhauerianism its image and its late-century success (pp. 128–129). The critique of pessimism is logical: four "-lessnesses" are converted into four positive horrors. Pessimism is the result of a phenomenological deficit, not of an insight into life.

This success governed — or rather corrupted — all modernity, the spirit of its greatest creators and of society as a whole. A philosophical meditation on Schopenhauerianism today can be summed up in two remarks. First, a theoretical failure — Schopenhauer's inability to build a radical phenomenology equal to the new vision of man he introduced, precisely a phenomenology of life, of a mode of revelation other than intentional consciousness — had immense practical consequences, modifying even in its content and deep pathos the life he had recognized as the essence of reality (p. 129). The claim that a theory of manifestation alters life's own pathos anticipates the cultural diagnosis of *La Barbarie*: how life understands itself affects how it feels itself.

Second, the entry of life into philosophy and, through it, into our culture is itself an event of great import. But because it occurred catastrophically with Schopenhauer — so that, for example, Nietzsche's attempted revaluation of life remained in truth without effect — it powerfully contributed to the face of our age, "celui d'une tristesse sans limite, d'une vie justement qui ne croit à rien" because, not having understood itself as "l'irruption triomphante de la révélation et ainsi comme sa première victoire sur le néant," it does not first believe in itself. Unable to draw from its own pathos of self-certainty the motivation of its undertakings and beliefs, it can abandon itself to the dead objectivity of science, take its external knowledge for the only sure knowledge and entrust its destiny to it (pp. 129–130). The essay ends where Chapter I began — the self-misunderstanding of life as the root of objectivism — and announces Chapter VIII's reading of Nietzsche's attempt to revalue life.

## VII. The Question of Repression in Schopenhauer

Henry starts from a summary definition: repression is a psychic process by which certain psychic facts push others back, denying them, if not existence, at least access to consciousness. The definition opens abyssal problems: what is the psyche, what are psychic processes and "facts," what is "consciousness"? It also implies the dissociation of the psychic from the conscious, Freud's great claim. Henry declines a frontal approach and follows history. The detour is methodological: the aporia of repression will be read off its first formulation.

Schopenhauer first introduced the concept of repression thematically into Western thought, in connection with three problems to which he gave original solutions: memory, perception, and reason or madness (p. 131). Henry's thesis, developed in *Généalogie*, is that Freud's metapsychology is prefigured in *The World as Will and Representation*.

The problem of memory concerns the recall of some memories and the forgetting of many others. A memory is a representation, an image floating before the mind — a landscape, a happy moment of the last holidays. The faculty of forming it, of "pro-ducing" it, leading it before the mind, is consciousness in the classical sense, the faculty of representation (pp. 131–132). The hyphenated "pro-duire" fixes the representational sense of production: to lead before.

This is a tautology of decisive but unthought significance. "Consciousness" means representation in the strict sense, presenting before, *vorstellen*, so that the placing-before as such creates the phenomenality of what is placed. Every representation has a content, but being-placed-before makes it conscious; the conscious quality, Freud's *Bewusstheit*, is being-represented as such. Freud adopts the identity of representation and consciousness explicitly: "Appelons donc 'consciente' la représentation qui est présente à notre conscience et que nous percevons comme telle et posons que c'est là le seul sens du terme 'conscient'" (*Metapsychology*; p. 132). Henry reuses the citation of Chapter I: Freud's definition of consciousness is the classical one, which is why his unconscious is its shadow.

A remembered memory is a "representation present to consciousness," placed before it, seen, and so conscious. But the problem of memory is not exhausted by its phenomenological status: one must also understand why this memory presents itself rather than another that does not, and perhaps never will (p. 132). The question of selection is what calls for a theory of repression.

Here Schopenhauer, against all classical philosophy, makes an astonishing claim, which Henry formulates paradoxically: the power to form a memory — to place it before consciousness and make it "conscious" — is not the power of placing-before, of representing, not the power of consciousness. Memory, though it consists in forming representations, is not a representative faculty (p. 133). The paradox locates the cause of representation outside representation.

What power forms these representations, if not the power of representation? The Will, says Schopenhauer — wholly unlike our will, the power to say yes or no, freedom. Schopenhauer's will is not free: it wills eternally, insurmountably, without having chosen to will. Like the desires that wear down our body, or identical with them, it is the ever-recommencing activity that traverses us, that we are and cannot escape. Schopenhauer's will, Freud says, "équivaut aux instincts de la psychanalyse" (GW XII 12; p. 133). Freud's acknowledgment of the equivalence (in "A Difficulty in the Path of Psycho-Analysis") authorizes Henry's genealogical reading.

Classical thought thinks the will from representation, as a faculty of representing ends and means and their adequacy; action is an objective, represented process conforming to an ideal, represented archetype. Schopenhauer thinks the will against representation. Representation opens the domain of consciousness, light, seeing. The will is blind, sees and represents nothing, wholly foreign to representation. Yet it is not nothing: its domain is force. It is efficient cause, power, the only force and power in the world, action in the actuality of its doing — and, for Schopenhauer, the only reality (pp. 133–134). The opposition of force and light, will and representation, sets up the aporia: a blind force must govern what only light can show.

Representation, the world — "the world is my representation" — is only the representation of this force, its unreal double, an image without efficacy or power. To the all-powerful will is opposed a wholly passive representation. Rigorously: the will, force, is in itself deprived of representation and its light; representation, light, consciousness, is in itself deprived of will, of all force. The psyche contains nothing but these two instances defined by mutual exclusion: will plus representation, "de telle façon que la volonté est sans représentation et la représentation sans volonté" (p. 134). The symmetry of the two privations is the structure Henry will attack: it leaves no place for a knowing that is also a power.

This analysis of the psyche is also a theory of repression. If will and representation together constitute the psyche, their relation results from their mutual exclusion: "la volonté par elle-même ne connaît pas et [...] l'entendement qui lui est associé est par lui-même incapable de vouloir" (MVR, Burdeau, III 20). When they meet, representation brings willing the light it lacks but, powerless, submits entirely to it. Their relation is one of force between an instance that is force itself — concentrating, in each action, all the force in the universe — and one wholly deprived of it. If representation defines thought and consciousness, conscious life as a whole is determined by an element deprived of light, the blind power of instincts and drives. The classical conception of man's *humanitas* totters: "Nous ne voulons pas une chose parce que nous nous représentons qu'elle est bonne [...] mais nous nous la représentons et la jugeons bonne parce que nous la voulons" (pp. 134–135). The inversion echoes Spinoza (*Ethics* III, 9, scholium); Henry treats it as the anthropological consequence of the will/representation dualism.

How far the subversion of man's humanity goes when thought and reason are only the "valets" of the instincts cannot be hidden. If representative consciousness is a making-visible, what sense can it keep when, manipulated by the will to live, it is only constant distortion and falsification? One might even ask how all this can be said — how consciousness can criticize itself and see what it really is, if its gaze is constantly distorted by an all-powerful power of error (p. 135). The reflexive objection is the classic one against naturalisms of suspicion: the theory saws off the branch it sits on.

This puzzling economy of the psyche defines the new theory of memory and repression. Recall of a memory and its rejection are explained by the will: it wants or does not want this representation and, in the latter case, "la tient en quelque sorte couverte de la main" (MVR III 29). Freud describes exactly this in the *Studies on Hysteria* (1893): "Il s'agissait de choses que le malade voulait oublier et qu'intentionnellement il maintenait, repoussait, refoulait hors de sa pensée consciente." And repression is defined by this exclusion of a representation from consciousness by the will, if its essence, per Freud, "ne consiste que dans le fait d'écarter et de maintenir à distance du conscient" (GW X 250; p. 135). The parallel citations establish that Freud's concept of repression is Schopenhauer's in content and form; the hand that covers is the image Henry will later turn against the theory.

The theory extends to perception: the will wants or does not want to see certain parts of the perceptual field, turning or averting the gaze, casting an object or part of it out of representation and so out of consciousness, keeping it "at a distance from the conscious" (p. 136). Perceptual selection becomes motivated, a doctrine Henry treats as a corollary.

The theory of madness rests on the same split between drive and representation and strikingly prefigures Freud. Trouble originates in the will's refusal to let a contrary representation into consciousness. For Schopenhauer, faithful heir of Kant, the world is a coherent whole of representations, present, past and future, subject to rules. A repressed representation leaves a gap in the fabric; since a world with holes is impossible, the mind fills it with an invented or displaced representation incompatible with the order of the world — and this negation of the real and its replacement by fabulated material is madness. If the will's resistance to admitting a truth reaches a degree where certain events are "entièrement soustraits à l'intellect" and the gap is filled artificially, "alors la folie est là" (MVR I 211–212; p. 136). The structure prefigures Freud's account of delusion as a patch over a rent in reality ("Neurosis and Psychosis").

Three decisive theories — memory and forgetting, perception, reason and madness — thus rest on the dichotomy of representation and will to live, distributed as conscious and unconscious, the second wholly determining the first. Because this dichotomy constitutes the psyche, the determination of representative consciousness by blind instinct — repression, positive or negative — is not a particular trait but its general law. All imaginary life attests the drive's manipulation of representation; hence psychoanalysis uses association of ideas and symbolic systems to induce the unconscious from conscious phenomena regarded as its products (pp. 136–137). Henry generalizes repression from an episodic mechanism to the constitutive law of a dualist psyche.

At the very moment conscious life becomes intelligible from a naturing power that produces it at every instant, the psychic system as a whole — product and unconscious producer — becomes unintelligible. If the union of will and representation explains how the former welcomes or rejects the latter according to whether it suits it, it is this union that is the problem. To allow or forbid a representation, must one not first know what it is, if only to judge its fit with desire? But how could the will have such knowledge, it that knows nothing, defined by *Erkenntnislosigkeit*? Knowledge consists in representation; the will represents nothing. How could it represent the contested representation? (p. 137). The aporia is the core of the essay and of Henry's general critique of the unconscious: the censor must know what it censors.

Chapter XXXII of the Supplements to Book III involuntarily exposes the aporia: certain events are withdrawn from the intellect "parce que la volonté n'en peut supporter l'aspect [*weil der Wille ihren Anblick nicht ertragen kann*]." But nothing ever presents any face of its being to the will's view, since the will never places anything before itself to see it. Chapter XIX ran into the same aporia: the will forbids the intellect certain representations "parce qu'elle (la volonté) sait, ou plutôt parce que l'intellect lui a appris que ces représentations feraient naître en elle des mouvements [...] [*weil er (der Wille) weiss, d.h. von ebendemselben Intellekt erfährt, dass...*]." The extraordinary slide from *the will knows* to *the intellect has taught it* vainly tries to hide the difficulty, since how the will can know anything is exactly the same problem as how the intellect can teach it (pp. 137–138). The German *Anblick* ("sight," "aspect") and the self-correcting *d.h.* are Henry's textual proofs: Schopenhauer's own sentences betray the impossibility.

The aporia strikes not only Schopenhauer but every conception explaining how a power without consciousness can reject a representation it does not know — every philosophy making the unconscious the organizing principle of the psyche. For this principle, which replaces one representation with another, awakens a memory to conceal a less suitable one, is "un stratège infiniment intelligent qui sait et qui connaît tout," which first sees from the corner of its eye the content it will, as Schopenhauer nicely says, "cover with its hand" so as not to see it (p. 138). The image of the strategist is Henry's reductio of the dynamic unconscious, close to Sartre's critique of the censor in *Being and Nothingness*, though Henry draws an opposite conclusion: not bad faith but a non-representational knowing.

In the light of this aporia, repression becomes a philosophical, transcendental question concerning its very possibility. If repression exists and is the law of the psyche — of our transcendental life, of which the psyche is only the objectification — we must see how it is possible from this life, so we must first understand this life, the essence of absolute subjectivity. And this philosophical question is phenomenological: since the power that rejects the representation must know it in order to want to reject it, and since it is in itself deprived of representation, the question is whether there exists "une forme de connaissance étrangère à la conscience représentative," to placing-before and the seeing that moves in it, ultimately to the ek-stasis of exteriority (p. 138). The question is reformulated in the volume's own terms: is there a non-ecstatic knowing?

Two imprescriptible requirements are implied. First, this knowledge that will know the representation must also be a power, a force dominating representation as the principle that directs and organizes it, prescribing or forbidding its unfolding, making it "secondary," dependent (p. 139). The requirement unites what Schopenhauer separated: knowing and power.

The second is the most troubling, at the root of the aporia on which the philosophy of the unconscious breaks: this knowledge, lacking the oppositional structure of representation, must give access to representation; it defines the knowledge prior to every representation which, already knowing it before it forms, alone can decide its formation, effecting or excluding it (p. 139). The paradox: a knowing that does not represent must know representations.

Radical phenomenology, as Henry has tried to constitute it, answers these questions that led classical thought to the aporia of the psychic unconscious, and finds in the aporia a privileged test of its fundamental theses. The claim is that a phenomenology of life resolves what metapsychology could not.

First demand: is there a mode of knowledge other than representation? Answer: the mode in which life knows itself — transcendental life, our true being, absolute subjectivity. The word "knowledge" must be stripped of its Western sense, knowledge = seeing = placing-before, whose origin is Greek, in the concept of *phainomenon*, what shines in light so as to be seen. Western knowledge, science and modern technology are consequences and forms of Greek truth (p. 139). Henry's genealogy of knowledge again runs from Greek light to technology, as in Chapter I.

In life, by contrast, there is no light and nothing that shines, nothing to be seen, because there is in it no Gap, no Difference from which light could arise and the gaze slip in. Life is the invisible — "non pas un rien phénoménologique, un 'inconscient'" — but the first appearing, the first manifestation and accomplishment of any conceivable experience: it experiences itself immediately and without distance, so that the phenomenality of this immediate experience — its self-feeling, its "suffering itself," the primitive Suffering that gives Being to itself as Life, its Arch-Revelation as self-affection — is Affectivity. Affectivity is original givenness, self-givenness in its actuality, the phenomenological matter and substance of this effectuation. The answer to the first demand is not a speculative proposition but the recognition of the original Phenomenon, the inner promotion of Being in its immediate self-appearing, in the irrefutable pathetic experience it constantly makes of itself as life (pp. 139–140). The invisible/unconscious distinction is the key: invisibility is a positive mode of phenomenality, the unconscious its absence.

Of this non-Greek, non-ecstatic, lightless knowledge to which nothing ever presents a face — this pure suffering enclosed in its subjectivity, which one will call "somnambulistic" so long as relation to the world is taken as model of all knowledge — two things remain to be shown: that it makes every power possible, notably that of forming or rejecting a representation; and that, irreducible to representation, it nonetheless has access to it, letting us know it and so have a motive for producing or repressing it (p. 140). "Somnambulistic" anticipates the hypnosis theme: life's knowing looks like sleep only to a philosophy of the waking gaze.

First, Affectivity as the essence of all power. Any power — moving my arm — as radically subjective, as the "I Can" I am in my original Body, can be exercised only by coinciding with itself in an absolute coincidence without light, world, object, relation to object, knowledge or knowing subject, "mais seulement la pure épreuve de cette immédiation en la chair impressionnelle de son affectivité." Everyone — not only sleepwalkers — walks in a state of hypnosis, without representative consciousness, object-knowledge, intentional aim, thought. Everything we do and know how to do we do so, in the Night of this primordial knowledge. Only so do we move our body inwardly, without seeing or looking, in the body's primitive union with itself, in its life crushed against itself, where every power embraces itself in order to act. Were the gap of the first dehiscence ever to open between this power and us, between it and itself, we could no longer join or exercise it; the simplest act, moving the arm or walking, would be out of reach. "Il est faux de dire avec Merleau-Ponty que d'entrée de jeu notre corps se lève vers le monde," that some intention is always sketched in it, for this never occurs in original corporeity. Hence all power is first pure self-experience, a feeling of power, and, as Nietzsche said more rightly, will to power is a pathos (pp. 140–141). The rejection of Merleau-Ponty's motor intentionality is explicit and central: for Henry the "I can" is not an intentionality toward the world but a self-possessed power — the Biranian thesis of *Philosophie et phénoménologie du corps*. The Nietzsche remark announces Chapter VIII.

The hyperpower of Affectivity that throws life into itself and lets it experience and enjoy itself, be what it is and do what it does, is also the power of representation, the power that forms or does not form it. Second demand: to show that life's acosmic, pathetic self-experience is precisely the knowledge that lets us know representation before it forms and assess it according to our desire, which also dwells in this night before the world (p. 141). The two demands are joined: the power to represent is itself affectively self-given.

In the famous passage of the letter to Plempius (3 October 1637), Descartes opposed to the vision of animals, which see nothing, a vision like ours, which truly sees because it belongs not to the "body" or "eyes," a thing incapable in principle of perceiving, but to the soul. This opposition is not simply between not seeing and seeing: against the animals' blindness the text sets not "we see" but "*sentimus nos videre*" (AT I 413). There is effective vision only insofar as it experiences itself inwardly in a primitive feeling that is a self-feeling. The phenomenality of this self-feeling has nothing to do with the light in which vision sees, with the Outside where objects stand before it. The first two Meditations demonstrate this, since doubt eliminates seeing and all it sees, every possible world, leaving intact only the feeling of vision, vision reduced to its invisible inner experience. In the categories of this analysis: life's knowledge, the primitive Suffering in which it embraces itself, remains, while Greek knowledge, the ek-stasis of Being and the representative consciousness that derives from it, are eliminated (pp. 141–142). The Cartesian demonstration of Chapters III and V is brought to bear on the theory of repression.

But — as the text reveals — life's non-ecstatic, acosmic self-experience is what gives us access to the seeing that places what it sees before itself in light. Only through this Suffering, as seeing's suffering of itself, are we placed in seeing, or is seeing placed in itself, so as to see. "Je vois et je suis certain que je vois," but it is not seeing as seeing-before-itself (as being-in-the-world) that gives me seeing in its certainty — how could it, being doubtful, and evidence itself excluded by radical doubt? What gives seeing its certainty of seeing is only life's knowledge, the primitive Feeling in which it experiences itself: *sentimus nos videre* (p. 142). "Being-in-the-world" is explicitly placed among the doubtful modes: Heidegger's existential is the seeing that cannot certify itself.

"Seeing" is only a name for representation. The last question — how life's knowledge, irreducible to representation, can let us know representation — is answered: life's knowledge knows representation, which places before itself in light, "parce qu'elle n'est rien d'autre que l'autoconnaissance de l'acte qui pose devant." This self-knowledge must be understood in its own phenomenality, as affectivity, as the self-impression of seeing and so its affect. It is an affect — the very one that gives the gaze to itself in its inner self-experience — that decides whether the gaze opens its eyes on what lies before it or closes them so as not to see it, repressing the representation (pp. 142–143). The solution is that what life knows is not the represented but the act of representing; repression is decided at the level of the act's self-affection.

But is the aporia removed? How does the affect know the representation without representing it? By the strict definition, nothing in life's phenomenological substance is ever placed before; considered in its radical interiority, life sees and represents nothing. Are we not back in the situation of Schopenhauer's will, which, deprived of representation, could not want to reject what it had no access to? The transcendental affectivity of life is certainly not Schopenhauer's unconscious will or Freud's drive, "elle n'est pas le non-phénomène mais l'Archi-phénomène." Yet insofar as it excludes all representation, how can it relate to it, welcome or reject it? (p. 143). Henry raises the objection against himself, sharpening the requirement the final answer must meet.

The ultimate elucidation: in representation one must distinguish the act that places before — representing as such, intentionality, transcendence, Ek-stasis as naturing — from what is placed before, the represented as such, "the representational content." Every representational content is excluded from life's inner essence, since it is excluded from the transcendental Affectivity that defines it. So the affect that will repress the representation knows nothing of the content it does not and never will see — before, during or after repression. All the less since, contrary to Freud, Bergson and all the psychology of the time, "ce contenu n'existe pas en soi, indépendamment d'un acte de représenter effectif" (pp. 143–144). No representational content subsists by itself as pure represented; there are no unconscious representations as autonomous formations, nor an unconscious made of them. This denial is Henry's most radical revision of Freud: there is no reservoir of stored representations (and no Bergsonian survival of pure memory); content exists only in the actual act of representing.

But though the affect ignores the representational content, which does not yet exist, it has an essential relation to the act of representing that will form it, being nothing but the experience in which this act is given immediately to itself, its self-affection, which makes the act of representing an affect and so what knows itself by the original knowing that the affect is. Representation is affective at its ground, in its act; the affectivity of this act is the primordial knowledge the act has of itself, which lets it unfold, accompanies it, encourages it to continue or prescribes its interruption (p. 144). Representation is thus governed from within by its own affective self-givenness, not from outside by a blind will.

But when the act stops and the content is not formed or remains incomplete, who decides the interruption? Is it not the content whose formation is interrupted? The objection would reintroduce content as cause.

A crucial question gives the theory its final validation: why is a representation repressed? Because of its content? But the content is not formed, and repression consists precisely in its not being formed. If the representation does not unfold its content before the mind, it can only be because of its affectivity: displeasure, suffering, ultimately terror tied to this representation, "non ce qu'elle représente," motivates its exclusion, its non-emergence into the Outside. The affect of the representation was already there and alone decided to exclude its content. So, as Schopenhauer saw, a force foreign to the content determines its destiny. But, against his most frequent claims, this force is not withdrawn from all phenomenality, not really unconscious: it is Affect, Arch-phenomenality, which lets it know what it does absolutely and do it with infallible certainty — the certainty that marks all the steps of the so-called unconscious. Desire knows what it desires without ever being mistaken, so that the image it produces will be the image of this desire, a content wanted and chosen by it (pp. 144–145). The infallibility Henry's opponent attributed to the "intelligent strategist" is reassigned to affect's self-knowledge: the unconscious seems omniscient because it is in fact absolute consciousness in Chapter I's sense.

More decisively, this force is not as foreign to representation as Schopenhauer said: it is foreign to the light into which representation throws itself, not to the power that opens this medium of light. It is the force of this power and first its affect, since a power has force and can be exercised only if it first possesses itself in the self-affection of its affectivity. Affect is at the heart of representation, of the power that deploys it; it makes that deployment possible and is also its motive, what leads it to act (p. 145). Schopenhauer's dualism is thus overcome without monism: affect and light remain heterogeneous, but the power of producing light is affectively self-given.

Affect is always singular. At the heart of representation, it belongs to life, changing like it, being nothing but the infinitely diverse way life experiences itself, according to life's own laws, not those of representation. Because it is affective at its ground, representation obeys laws other than those of consciousness, which are also those of the world, space, time, causality, perception, association, science. Representation, and ultimately the world, obey "la loi du désir," the great law of the inner connection of our fundamental affective tonalities and their transformations into one another. These laws are rigorous — both those governing the transformations of affects, what Freud calls their vicissitudes (*Triebschicksale*), and those that determine the fundamental tonalities themselves, rooted in the essence of Life, of absolute subjectivity. A rigorous phenomenological analysis of it shows why the subjectivity that defines our life bears the tonalities Joy, Suffering, Anxiety and why it oscillates necessarily from one to another according to predefined potentialities rooted in its nature (pp. 145–146). The "laws of life" — the passage of suffering into joy — are Henry's alternative to associationist and causal psychology, developed in the final sections of *L'essence de la manifestation* and in *Généalogie*.

Not the least merit of a problematic of repression, and so of Schopenhauer, is to lead us from the luminous universe of world-thought that traditionally defines our humanity to more difficult and essential problems, the dimension before the world where our true being stands — and the world's being too, insofar as it draws its ultimate possibility from us. By displacing us from the visible world of representation to invisible life, repression forced, if only to establish its own possibility, a redefinition of phenomenality and of phenomenology. That neither Schopenhauer nor Freud made this progress — that, keeping the Greek model, they cast everything that did not conform to it into a mythical back-world, absurdly repeating there the structure of representation — does not prevent the irruption of the new problems of sexuality, desire, force, anxiety, and the still systematically unexplored ontological field in which they belong. Since the destiny of representations, of thought, depends on that of drives and affects, their status must be clearly defined, "s'ils constituent la réalité profonde de l'homme, et si l'homme doit encore savoir ce qu'il est" (p. 146). The verdict on psychoanalysis is consistent with Chapter I: its problems are genuine, its ontology inherited and wrong; the phenomenology of life claims to supply the ontology its discoveries require.

## VIII. On Nietzsche's Saying: "We the Good… the Happy"

With the saying "nous les bons... les heureux" — the nobles' self-designation quoted in *Genealogy of Morals* I §10 ("we the noble, the good, the beautiful, the happy") — Nietzsche thinks two things: explicitly, the condition of the strong; implicitly but fundamentally, the essence of life (p. 147). Henry's method is announced: the explicit typology of strong and weak is to be read as a phenomenology of life.

The condition of the strong is grasped antithetically from that of the weak. This opposition runs through Nietzsche's work, to the astonishment and perhaps the embarrassment of commentators. Henry acknowledges the discomfort the master/slave typology causes and defuses it by relocating it in the inner structure of life.

Four questions structure the elucidation: who are the strong and in what does their strength consist; who are the weak and in what does weakness consist; since Nietzsche holds that the weak inevitably prevail over the strong, what gives the weak their strength; and correlatively, how are the strong defeated, what makes the weakness of the strong (pp. 147–148). The fourth question receives no separate treatment; Henry's answer emerges from the third, since the strength of the weak and the weakness of the strong are one and the same structure seen from two sides.

The first seems easy: the strong are strong insofar as they are, since the essence of being is force, the Will to Power. This is intelligible only from Schopenhauer's Will, a "hungry reality," an unquenchable thirst, a desire unsatisfiable because it desires a reality that does not exist, there being no reality but this objectless desire — whence its indefinite reiteration and vanity (p. 148). The genealogy Schopenhauer–Nietzsche, prepared in Chapters II and VI, frames the reading.

By contrast, as has rightly been said, Nietzsche's Will to Power is not a desire, an aspiration to a reality it lacks, a will toward a power it does not yet have. It is already power, wholly force. The "will" added to power is only its self-movement, the accomplishment of its own essence resting on it. "As has rightly been said" alludes to the standard reading (Heidegger's and Deleuze's, in different ways) that will to power does not will power as an object.

Yet already in Schopenhauer the Will is not a desire in the sense of a velleity cut off from reality. It is the original body — not the represented body but the body one is, the body of real movements and real effectuations — and that these lead to no true satisfaction and begin again does not lessen their reality. Schopenhauer's Will is never separated from power; it is Power, the only real power in the world, and all its power gathers at each point of its action — hence the vertiginous character of the Schopenhauerian universe. Henry corrects the contrast he just drew: what distinguishes Nietzsche from Schopenhauer cannot be power versus lack.

What then does Nietzsche's addition of power to a Will already all-powerful mean? That whatever the degree or intensity of a power, power and force must first *be*, and this being consists in a prior, presupposed power by which they have already seized themselves and their own essence. "Volonté de Puissance veut dire [...] puissance de la Volonté": not merely that will is power, that it is the body, but that all power, force and the body itself are only by the work of a more original power "qui les jette en eux-mêmes et les contraint d'être." Only in this hyperpower of the origin can power and force unfold (pp. 148–149). Henry inverts the genitive: will to power is the power by which the will is given to itself — his own concept of self-affection as the "hyperpower" that delivers every power to itself.

Even supposing degrees of power and quantities of force, the prior power by which they are knows no degree, quantity, increase, decrease or alteration; it is the hyperpower omnipresent and omnipotent in all power, delivering it to itself so that it can be what it is and do what it does — present in the weakest as in the strongest. Thus no force, however insignificant, fails to bring with it the incommensurable of this hyperpower, which cannot be measured by any force and measures none, since it is in every force before it unfolds and takes or gives its measure. It is in every force "l'incoercibilité et l'inconditionnalité du lien par lequel elle s'empare de soi et s'éprouve ainsi elle-même" (p. 149). The distinction between quantities of force and the non-quantitative self-givenness of force will decide the analysis of weakness; it is aimed at quantitative readings of Nietzsche such as Deleuze's typology of active and reactive forces.

Here must be considered the decisive though unnoticed event of modern philosophy: Henry italicizes, "avec Schopenhauer et Nietzsche l'être reçoit pour la première fois le sens d'être la vie." This involves no forgetting of being, no reduction of being to the mode of being of a particular entity, the living. Determining force as living force returns to being's original work, the original self-embrace of what comes into itself and grows from itself and which, in the intoxication of this self-experience and self-increase without beginning or end, is life — in Nietzsche's mythic language, Dionysus. Will to power is the self-increase included in this original self-embrace, the "plus riche" of oneself (*Beyond Good and Evil* §295), the "need of self." This self-increase as coming into oneself, self-experience, being-in-oneself, Henry calls "immanence" (pp. 149–150). The denial that "life" is an ontic region answers Heidegger's charge (in *Being and Time* and the *Nietzsche* lectures) that philosophies of life miss the question of being: for Henry, life is the meaning of being.

Nietzsche thought immanence in many ways, notably in saying life is forgetting. Ordinarily forgetting is not-thinking-of, opposed to remembering; both are modes of thought, of representation, one positive, one negative. Nietzsche means something else: life forgets not by passing distraction but because it does not bear the essence of thought, the possibility of thinking of anything. Life is forgetful by nature as immanence, which expels all ek-stasis and so every form of thought and representation. Nietzsche figures this original structure in the animal, which runs through his work — rightly, if the point is to express the absence of thought that traditionally defines man as rational animal. By essential necessity the animal, figuring the essence of life which excludes representation, is defined by forgetting — man, "cet animal nécessairement oublieux" (*Genealogy* II §1; p. 150). Forgetting is reinterpreted as a phenomenological category: not a lapse of memory but the non-ecstatic mode of life. Chapter VII's denial of stored representations finds an ally here.

How can what is forgetful by essence remember? This paradox opens the Second Essay of the *Genealogy*: "Élever un animal qui puisse promettre," that is, remember, even though life's essence excludes thought. To resolve it Nietzsche displaces the problem of memory entirely: instead of a faculty of thought, a representative faculty, he subjects it to will to power, to life, and to what appears here for the first time, affectivity. "On grave quelque chose au fer rouge pour le fixer dans la mémoire : seul ce qui ne cesse de faire mal est conservé dans la mémoire" (*Genealogy* II §3). As always when he uncovers life's ground — affectivity, suffering — Nietzsche's text catches fire: images crackle, history's great conflagrations are summoned, each proof a pyre: "supplices, martyrs [...] les mutilations les plus repoussantes," and for the German man stoning, the wheel, impalement, flaying. Everywhere pain is "l'adjuvant le plus puissant de la mnémonique"; "la souffrance tient lieu de pensée et la fonde ultimement" (pp. 150–151). The "mnemotechnics of pain" is the Nietzschean counterpart of Chapter VII's thesis: affect, not representation, governs what becomes conscious. Henry's description of the text's heat also marks the point where Nietzsche's style itself becomes pathos.

The immanence of life, its unconditional coherence with itself, is the condition of its unfolding, of the central phenomenon of action — as the fable of the birds of prey swooping on lambs shows (*Genealogy* I §13). This difficult but essential analysis takes the form of a critique of morality: refuting the lambs' argument, which seeks to save their lives by condemning the birds' action — a condemnation the birds need only share for the lambs to be safe. The lambs' argument presupposes a doubling of force, its separation from itself, the denial of its radical immanence: a dissociation, in every action, between a subject able to decide for or against it and the action itself, understood as the effect of its free will. This is the people's doubling of lightning and flash, taking one phenomenon twice, as cause and then as effect. "Les savants ne font pas mieux [...] quand ils disent 'la force fait mouvoir', 'la force produit un effet'" (pp. 151–152). Nietzsche's critique of the "subject" behind the deed is reread as a defence of immanence: force is not separated from itself by a choosing ego.

The lambs regard the birds as subjects free to exercise their force or not, free to be or not be birds of prey; in this freedom of force not to be force lies the lambs' salvation. But neither force nor life possesses this freedom not to be itself. Non-freedom, the impossibility of not being oneself, is implied by the essence governing life's relation to itself, its self-experience in "l'inconditionnalité du lien qui le lie à elle-même pour être ce qu'elle est à jamais" — its eternity, what Nietzsche calls the eternal return of the Same (p. 152). Eternal return is read not cosmologically but as the self-identity of life bound to itself forever — "eternal" because life cannot detach itself from itself.

Non-freedom, the incoercibility of the bond delivering life to itself, is the hyperpower in which being gathers itself and seizes itself in self-experience. Because it is non-freedom, it is also its impotence: life's inability to rid itself of itself. Henry adds that §13 thinks this through: it is not about birds or lambs, nor merely that they are not free to do otherwise, but that they cannot be other than they are — and they are so only on the ground of being in them, insofar as being's structure is non-freedom, "sa passivité insurmontable à l'égard de soi," the inability to undo itself of what coheres in itself as life (p. 152). The hyperpower and the impotence are one: this unity of power and passivity, the core of *L'essence de la manifestation*'s analysis of suffering, is here found in Nietzsche.

Such is the essence of the lavishing force: it "honore tout ce qu'elle trouve en soi," foregrounds "le sentiment de la plénitude, de la puissance qui veut déborder," so that all it does, such as helping the unfortunate, proceeds from it "par l'effet d'un besoin qui naît de la surabondance de sa force" (*Beyond Good and Evil* §260; p. 153). If aristocracy's essence is life's, the strength of the strong is not some given force, greater or lesser, but the hyperpower that, throwing every force into itself, lets it grow of itself and overflow. Henry's answer to the first question: the strong are those in whom life's self-givenness is undisturbed, so that their action flows from fullness rather than lack.

What weakness consists in, by contrast, is a problem: if will to power is the essence of being and everything is by this overflowing power, how is weakness possible at all? An external explanation says: all is force, but there are quantities; one force facing a greater is weaker, quantitative difference yields qualitative difference, weakness and strength. The weaker, undergoing the stronger, becomes "reactive," its action determined by the stronger action it constantly undergoes, while the stronger alone remains truly "active" (p. 153). The active/reactive vocabulary is Deleuze's (*Nietzsche et la philosophie*), though unnamed; Henry presents it as the external explanation he will reject.

This extrinsic determination is at the antipodes of what Nietzsche means by will to power, the internal nature of force as overflowing itself and never ceasing to be what it is. That there is an essence of force that cannot become something else, not even its contrary — that masters do not suddenly become slaves on meeting a stronger one — Nietzsche posits by making aristocracy a "race," that is, an essence. The plebs is one too: weakness must be understood not extrinsically but from its inner possibility. And this possibility is the same as that of force — there is nothing else — the essence of life, immanence. Henry italicizes: force "est celle de l'immanence, c'est l'inquantifiable, incoercible et insurpassable force du lien qui lie la vie à elle-même." Nietzsche understands weakness not as less force but as negation of its essence — since this essence is immanence, as its rupture (pp. 153–154). "Race" is glossed philosophically as essence, a gesture that neutralizes the term's biological sense; the decisive move is defining weakness as an attempted rupture of immanence.

This is what nihilism means — the no said to life, not the external negation of its factual existence but the destruction of its inner essence. But this destruction is self-destruction — life says no to life — and it collides with an impossibility of essence, since the bond binding life to itself is unbreakable. The impossible, therefore endless, self-destruction of life's inner essence is what Nietzsche calls the sickness of life, which makes man — insofar as immanence is animality and what must be shaken — a "sick animal," "the sickliest animal" (*Genealogy* III §13; p. 154). Nihilism is thus life's will to break with itself, structurally doomed — the formula for despair Henry will name shortly.

Nietzsche simply looked with open eyes at the mystery of this sickness, life's will to strike at its own essence. He described all forms of the project of rupture: self-doubt, loss of belief, scepticism, objectivism, scientism, self-criticism in all its aspects — "on est tenté d'écrire 'l'analyse'" — all doctrines of bad faith, bad conscience, self-scrutiny, interpretation, suspicion, all that place life's truth outside life, making our time a "spoiled present" which, as Osip Mandelstam would say, smells of rotten fish. All these partisans of self-division, "les malvenus [...] les plus faibles," most undermine life among men, poisoning "notre confiance en la vie, en l'homme, en nous-même" (*Genealogy* III §14; pp. 154–155). The list aligns Ricoeur's "masters of suspicion" and the hermeneutics of the next chapter with Nietzsche's weak: the aside "analysis" points toward psychoanalysis.

That rupture of immanence is the mark of weakness appears more clearly in the struggle of the weak against the strong, how the former bring down the latter's strength. The destruction of strength is the destruction of its self-immanence, achieved if the weak manage to insert their weakness into the souls of the strong, "s'ils réussissaient à mettre leur propre misère et toute la misère du monde dans la conscience des heureux," so that one day these would say among themselves: "c'est une honte d'être heureux ! Il y a trop de misère !" (*Genealogy* III §14; p. 155). Strength and weakness thus divide happiness and shame, life's immanence and its rupture. Henry partly answers the fourth question here: the strong are defeated when shame introduces into them the self-distance that is weakness.

That this rupture is impossible leads to weakness's true essence. What ultimately makes the weak weak is not only what hides beneath the mire of shame and self-contempt — "Sur ce terrain du mépris de soi, véritable marécage, poussent toute mauvaise herbe, toute plante vénéneuse" — namely the monstrous project of self-destruction, but the failure of this project. Life's willing not to be itself, the Self's will to rid itself of itself, is weakness as such, since it collides in principle with a greater force, the greatest, life's. Nietzsche's "limit text" says how: the inward-turned gaze of the man ill-formed from the start, betraying how he speaks to himself, a gaze that is a sigh: "Puissé-je être quelqu'un d'autre [...] mais il n'y a pas d'espoir ! Je suis ce que je suis : comment me débarrasser de moi ? Et pourtant j'en ai assez de moi !" (*Genealogy* III §14, Henry's emphasis; pp. 155–156). "Such is the essence of weakness." Need one say it resembles to the point of confusion Kierkegaard's despair? The comparison is exact: *The Sickness unto Death* defines despair as the self's willing to be rid of itself, which it cannot. "Je suis ce que je suis," cited in Chapter III as Nietzsche's adequate cogito, reappears as the formula of despair — the same self-identity undergone as an ordeal.

Third question: how do the weak prevail? It seems an insurmountable aporia: how could the weakness of life's project of ridding itself of itself prevail over the unsurpassable force of its self-coherence? In truth, weakness seems to and can prevail because it bears the greatest force within it, since it is, and, however feeble, coheres with itself in life's hyperpower: "pas un instant le vouloir se défaire de soi de la vie n'a cessé d'appartenir à celle-ci ni de porter en lui son essence" (p. 156). The hyperpower of Chapter VIII's beginning, present "in the weakest as in the strongest," resolves the aporia.

Nietzsche says this in the extraordinary analysis of the ascetic priest. For the first time, casting retroactive light on the whole work, weakness and strength are no longer two separate entities in two individuals: the ascetic priest holds both, showing their internal connection. He is weak, as the man of bad conscience, life turned against itself. He differs from the other weak in being their nurse — wherein he still belongs to them, since to avoid contagion of this terrible sickness those in contact with the sick must themselves be sick. Yet he is strong, perhaps stronger than the strongest: "il lui faut aussi être fort, maître de soi plus encore que des autres, intact surtout dans sa volonté de puissance" (*Genealogy* III §15). His task is crushing: to defend the flock against the strong and against itself. Against the strong, by inventing the ascetic ideal, which legitimates *ressentiment* through the transvaluation of values, making weakness good and strength evil, securing the dominion of the weak. Against the flock: having organized *ressentiment*, he must keep its unleashing from shattering the flock, channelling, guiding and soothing it — this great magician "empoisonne et il calme la blessure en même temps" (pp. 156–157). The priest is the figure in which the typology internalizes: strength and weakness in one life.

In him appears the mysterious interweaving of weakness and strength and how he converts one into the other. In him an exhausted, cornered life undertakes to survive. What, in extreme weakness, gives it the unheard-of force to go on living, resist the strong and even subjugate them — what instinct of life remained intact? Nietzsche's hyperlucid gaze finds it at the bottom of the ascetic ideal: "L'idéal ascétique a sa source dans l'instinct de défense et de salut d'une vie en voie de dégénération," against which "les instincts de vie les plus profonds, restés intacts, ne cessent de combattre" (*Genealogy* III §13, Henry's emphasis). The ascetic ideal is then the opposite of what it seemed: not life turned against life but the pathetic effort of a dying life to survive: the priest, "cet ennemi apparent de la vie [...] fait partie [...] des très grandes forces conservatrices et affirmatrices de la vie"; "En lui et par lui la vie lutte contre la mort." The death it fights is not death but the mortal, metaphysical sickness of life: "la lutte de l'homme contre la mort (plus exactement : contre le dégoût de la vie, contre la fatigue, contre le désir de la 'fin')." How the greatest force unfolds within degenerating life to save it is now understood — Henry italicizes — "en tant que cette vie vient en soi dans l'hyperpuissance de son immanence" (pp. 157–158). The reading of the ascetic ideal as life's self-preservation is Nietzsche's own; Henry's contribution is grounding it in the non-quantitative hyperpower of self-affection.

A doubt remains: do these analyses fully explain the possibility and origin of weakness? Why does life turn against itself; whence the aberrant project of ridding itself of itself? Nietzsche says: from suffering — "le troupeau des mal venus [...] de ceux qui souffrent d'eux-mêmes." We are sent to life's second essential determination, affectivity (p. 158). Immanence was the first; the turn to affectivity completes Henry's two-part phenomenology of life in Nietzsche.

Nietzsche seems now to disparage suffering, now to praise it unconditionally. Read closely, the texts never condemn suffering itself, only the hatred or vengeance it may provoke — more precisely, the particular will it engenders when, becoming intolerable, unable to bear itself, it undertakes to rid itself of itself. That will — life's will no longer to be what it is — is the very project of weakness. Elsewhere, when it does not engender nihilism, suffering is celebrated: "La culture de la souffrance, de la grande souffrance [...] c'est là l'unique cause des dépassements de l'homme" (*Beyond Good and Evil* §225). Oedipus "par l'excès même de ses souffrances, exerce autour de lui une action magique bienfaisante" (*Birth of Tragedy* §9). Conversely, Christianity and the democratic movement that inherits it are condemned for their "inaptitude quasi féminine à voir souffrir, à laisser souffrir" (*Beyond Good and Evil* §202; p. 158). Henry's distinction — suffering versus the will to escape suffering — makes Nietzsche consistent; his own Christian philosophy of life (*C'est moi la vérité*) will dispute Nietzsche's reading of Christianity, but not here.

The good, the strong, the masters are the happy ones. What then does the celebration of suffering mean? It leads to Nietzsche's abyssal intuition — Henry italicizes — "que souffrance et joie ne s'opposent pas mais sont le Même et, bien plus, elles composent ensemble le fond des choses." Their unity, the essence of life, is what Nietzsche calls the primordial One. From *The Birth of Tragedy* this identity appears as the magical transformation of suffering into joy. In tragedy we witness the tribulations of a hero — Tristan or Oedipus — who is Schopenhauer's hero, agitated by desire, struggling against the course of the world that inevitably turns on him and annihilates him. As we watch his trials and death in anguish, an incomprehensible and far higher enjoyment overcomes us: the annihilation of the hero is that of the phenomenal world, and through it the ground of things is unveiled, will to power. This unveiling is itself the infinite enjoyment tragedy gives — or rather it is at once suffering and joy. The sight of the hero's sufferings only recalls and relives in us "la souffrance infinie de la vie qui est identiquement sa félicité, son plaisir d'exister" (pp. 158–159). The *Birth of Tragedy* thesis on tragic pleasure is the textual base for Henry's doctrine of the reversal of suffering into joy.

How so? Life is what experiences itself, the fact of self-experience without distance, an immediate suffering, suffering itself, in which suffering finds its possibility. But for life, to suffer itself is to come into itself, to increase from itself, to enjoy itself. Suffering and joy are not two separate, opposed, self-sufficient tonalities; each is the substance of the other, forming the "surprenant mélange des affects" revealed by "la duplicité des fous de Dionysos" (*Birth* §2), those identified with the god, the single essence of things — "ce phénomène par lequel le plaisir s'éveille de la douleur même." And this phenomenon is the original essence of phenomenality, being's original coming into itself — self-suffering in the self-increase of self-enjoyment. Henry italicizes: "L'être n'est pas, il est une venue, l'éternelle venue en soi de la vie." This coming does not come from the future nor pass into the past; it is the coming of enjoyment from suffering, so that suffering, "l'effectuation phénoménologique du se souffrir soi-même de la vie," is identically its self-enjoyment. The more strongly life experiences itself in the intensification of its suffering, and finally at its paroxysm, the more powerfully it seizes itself and the more intense its enjoyment (pp. 159–160). This is the most condensed statement of Henry's "fundamental tonalities" doctrine: suffering and joy are not empirical opposites but two faces of self-affection, life's passive undergoing of itself and the fullness it thereby attains. "Coming" without future or past contrasts with ek-static temporality: life's coming is not a temporal advent.

This inner connection of suffering and joy runs through all of Nietzsche's work as its unnoticed support. Take the genealogy of morals. It apparently starts from a first social relation, debtor and creditor, arising from exchange of products. Here originate debt, the feeling of personal obligation, the need to remember and so to breed an animal that can promise, the recognition of damage and acceptance of punishment as compensation. The web of these relations is dense enough to explain the individual's relation to society, ancestors and finally God (p. 160). Henry reconstructs the Second Essay's sociological surface before exposing its affective core.

The essential sequence occurs when the debtor does not repay, and the creditor is entitled to a strange compensation, no longer in money or kind but in the right to make the debtor suffer — strike him, mistreat him, insult him, even violate him. Hence the abyssal question: "Comment la souffrance peut-elle être une compensation pour des dettes ?" And the no less abyssal answer: "Parce que ce faire souffrir donnait un très grand plaisir et que celui qui avait subi le dommage [...] obtenait en échange une extraordinaire contre-jouissance : faire souffrir — véritable fête" (*Genealogy* II §6; p. 160). The equivalence of pain and debt presupposes that pain can be converted into pleasure — the structure Henry has just described.

One will say: this is mere vengeance. But vengeance refers to the same problem: "comment faire souffrir peut-il être une satisfaction ?" One will say: mere cruelty. But cruelty too is only the enjoyment that proceeds from suffering (pp. 160–161). Psychological explanations presuppose what they would explain, as in Chapter III's remark that all explanations of affectivity presuppose it.

In cruelty and vengeance, suffering and joy seem external to each other, attached to two persons — suffering to the debtor, joy to the creditor. This is mere appearance. One must still explain how, in the creditor himself, the suffering of the damage he underwent can turn into the joy of making suffer; how suffering turns into joy in the spectator of comedy; and finally in Dionysus himself, who is the essence of life. Like the strong and the weak, vengeance and cruelty are only figures of life: "elles séparent les constituants intérieurs de l'Un afin de nous les donner à voir." Among the horrors of ancient Greece that fascinated Nietzsche was the sacrifice of a young man whose lacerated, bloody limbs were scattered so that the blood would fertilize the earth and give it life. "La philosophie de Nietzsche est ce meurtre rituel, elle est la dis-jonction et la pro-jection agrandie, dans le ciel du mythe, de la structure de la subjectivité absolue" (p. 161). The final image states Henry's hermeneutic of Nietzsche: his typologies and myths project outward, as separate persons and scenes, the internal structure of self-affection — immanence and the passage of suffering into joy. The hyphens of "dis-jonction" and "pro-jection" mark the ek-static operation by which the invisible unity of life is dismembered into visible figures; like the Dionysian sacrifice, this makes life visible at the price of tearing it apart.

## IX. Ricoeur and Freud: Between Psychoanalysis and Phenomenology

Henry credits Ricoeur's *De l'interprétation. Essai sur Freud* (1965) with one of the best available expositions of psychoanalysis, rigorous and clear. But because Ricoeur is a philosopher, it is more than a restatement: it is a critique in the Kantian sense, "une critique épistémologique" aiming to "deduce" — justify — the realist concepts of psychoanalysis, topographical and energetic, by showing "leur pouvoir d'ordonner un nouveau domaine d'objectivité et d'intelligibilité" (Ricoeur, p. 419). This critique engages and questions phenomenology, so that the whole book can be read as a debate between psychoanalysis and phenomenology. Phenomenology is not an arbitrary preliminary for interpreting psychoanalysis: a single problem, that of meaning, summons both major currents of modern culture to the same task — deciphering the deep, "true" meaning of human behaviour from and through its apparent meaning. Which discipline, with its categories and methods, does this best and goes furthest in understanding the psyche? Testing phenomenology by psychoanalysis and psychoanalysis by phenomenology, as two hermeneutics: such is the stake of Ricoeur's problematic (pp. 163–164). Henry's Cerisy lecture (1988) states the framework he will contest: that both disciplines share the problem of *meaning*.

The appeal to phenomenology is motivated by the failure of the epistemological case against psychoanalysis, especially American epistemology tinged with positivism and scientism. That case is "sans réplique si l'on entreprend de situer la psychanalyse parmi les sciences d'observation" (Ricoeur, p. 340); but this very attempt is inadequate if the analytic situation is first a "situation de langage." Hence the concepts of analysis must be judged "à titre de condition de possibilité de l'expérience analytique, en tant que celle-ci se déploie dans le champ de la parole" (p. 366). For Ricoeur these concepts concern relations of signifier and signified; they belong to the hermeneutic sciences, whose method is interpretation. The analytic relation to its object is irreducible to a description of observables and intelligible only through procedures closer to historical understanding (Weber's "types") than to natural-scientific explanation. Even if the ground of the psyche refers to natural facts, as Freud's energetics suggests, access to it — Ricoeur's crucial thesis — can only be through the psychic representatives of energy, all signifying processes open to interpretation. Hence the necessity of returning to phenomenology as hermeneutic discipline par excellence, or as hermeneutics itself (pp. 164–165). The thesis that drives are accessible only through their representatives is the point Henry will ultimately reject.

Ricoeur's striking parallelism starts from reading the reduction as immediate consciousness's letting go of itself, after which the meaning first intended and taken as true appears doubtful or suspect. Ricoeur interprets Husserl's reduction in Hegelian fashion: it becomes the critique of immediate knowledge. In Husserl the philosophy of evidence, "the principle of principles," inevitably comes with a critique of evidence, since evidence can ground being and knowledge only if one says unambiguously what deserves the title. This critique perhaps extends much further than Husserl wanted, and Ricoeur instinctively plays on its extension to bring phenomenology and psychoanalysis as close as possible (p. 165). Henry agrees with the extension — he made it himself in Chapter II — but will draw the opposite conclusion.

Husserl's critique of evidence is double. Aimed first at the object, it shows that only a momentary, fleeting subjective appearance is really given, while the whole context of objective reality reduces to a presumptive series of potential, non-evident evidences. Then it turns menacingly on the cogito itself, of which only a living, concrete core, also limited to an evanescent present, escapes the ever-open horizon of potential givenness (*CM* §9), the co-intended, the implicit and finally the unconscious (p. 165). The summary recalls Chapter II's analysis of *CM* §9; the new step is the word "unconscious" at the end of the series.

More: if the cogito is considered not quasi-psychologically as a privileged datum, however fragile, but transcendentally, "comme le pouvoir de constituer le sens de tout ce qui est pour nous," a more formidable difficulty arises. As meaning-constituting operation, as the ego "functioning in the last instance" (*Crisis* §55), transcendental subjectivity remains in an "anonymity" that contradicts its claim to define appearing as such. "C'est l'ultime échec de la phénoménologie husserlienne, incapable de résoudre son problème le plus propre, celui de la phénoménalité — plus précisément : du comment de la phénoménalisation originelle de la phénoménalité —, qui fournit à l'herméneutique son argument majeur." If the ultimate meaning-constituting power escapes phenomenality — if, with Ricoeur, "l'intentionnalité en exercice déborde l'intentionnalité thématique" (p. 369) — its implications define the nature of the hermeneutics of which phenomenology of meaning and psychoanalysis of the unconscious are two modes — unless, on common presuppositions, they are one inquiry (pp. 165–166). Henry identifies the common root: hermeneutics lives off phenomenology's failure to account for the self-appearing of constituting life.

Phenomenality then reigns only at the level of thematic intentionality, "qui sait son objet et qui se sait sur son objet" — immediate consciousness. That it knows its object means the object shows itself; that it knows itself as directed at the object means the same: constituting and showing the object, it is nothing but its appearing. To say operative intentionality (*fungierende Intentionalität*) exceeds thematic intentionality, remains anonymous, does not show itself as operative, singularly limits thematic intentionality in principle. It says that within the act of making visible there is something unseen, so that what is produced in light is never wholly so, never exhibits its whole meaning, since that meaning is also the effect of a power that does not show itself. "L'invisible inscience de soi de l'intentionnalité en exercice" (Ricoeur, p. 370) entails that its correlate has or can always have another meaning than the one revealed to thematic intentionality — whatever in the object results from the hidden operation (pp. 166–167). "Unknowing of self" (*inscience de soi*) is Ricoeur's term for exactly what Henry calls the self-givenness of life — misdescribed as a lack.

A post-Husserlian current to which Ricoeur explicitly refers (De Waelhens and Vergote) expresses this dissociation as a historical lag between a meaning already constituted by self-unknowing intentionality and its apperception by thematic consciousness, which finds it before itself without understanding it, without having constituted it consciously. This lag is the one between unreflected consciousness and a reflection always behind it, trying to recapture factual meaning by reactivating its genesis. That is impossible if the ultimate operative intentionality, including reflection's, always remains anonymous. The project of absolute knowledge in Hegel's sense, total recovery of operative intentionality, breaks on its very essence, "sur le primat incontournable de l'irréfléchi sur le réflexif, et cela au sein de la réflexion" (p. 167). The reference to Vergote (Louvain) and De Waelhens marks the Lacanian-phenomenological milieu Henry has in view.

The result is the emergence of a misunderstood meaning, more precisely — Henry italicizes — "la séparation possible du sens et de la conscience," consciousness facing a meaning it did not constitute, whose true content escapes it, is unconscious. Ricoeur: "Il est possible de définir directement le psychisme sans recourir à la conscience de soi par la seule visée de quelque chose ou sens" (p. 370; pp. 167–168). This definition of the psyche by intentionality alone, without self-consciousness, is the precise target: for Henry, removing self-givenness from the definition of the psyche is removing life.

A meaning that is there without being understood, unconscious, ignorant of its truth — a text to decipher, offered to deciphering since, issuing from a prior constitution, it is meaning in itself, belonging to a hermeneutic region whose constituents are all meanings not yet understood: that is all a hermeneutics needs, phenomenological or Freudian, "le matériel idéal prédestiné à son traitement" (p. 168). Henry describes the hermeneutic field as made to measure: the unconscious as unread text is exactly what interpretation requires.

Husserl explicitly describes a situation that gives hermeneutics its chosen terrain: the distinction between active and passive genesis of meaning. In active genesis the ego intervenes actively and consciously, creating a meaning by specific acts, overseeing its appearance and able to reproduce it at will. Passive genesis places it in a different condition, submission to a meaning-object it encounters without having made it. This passivity is not the mere reverse of the productive activity of categorial objectities, its modalization into habit. Passive genesis is the indispensable precondition of active genesis, supplying a pre-discovered field of objects presupposed by categorial syntheses. It not only produces objects the ego will find; as originally constituting operation, it occurs in the ego without the ego, independently of its will and consciousness, which is why its products are given as an opaque, already-there world. The passive syntheses of association and temporality give Husserl's phenomenology the character of an "archaeology of meaning" (p. 168). Henry's account of passive genesis is accurate; his objection, as in Chapter II, will be that Husserl's passivity is still the passivity of constitution, not that of life undergoing itself.

Since endless syntheses have constituted superposed strata of meaning, from passively preconstituted archaic meanings to thematically intended ones — which themselves fall out of phenomenological actuality, producing ever thicker sedimentations even on the plane of active genesis — research must proceed as a constantly renewed effort to exhume buried meanings, the deepest and most fundamental of which were never conscious. "Prise de conscience noématique renvoyant à une prise de conscience noétique": such is phenomenology's explicit programme, which makes it exemplarily a hermeneutics (pp. 168–169). Sedimentation and reactivation (from the *Crisis* and "Origin of Geometry") supply the bridge to analytic "excavation."

Suppose now that the operative intentionalities constituting archaic and fundamental meanings are not merely association and temporalization as in Husserl but those of desire, of drive and affective activity in general — with the double feature of essential passivity and unconsciousness. Then the theory of passive genesis and phenomenology as a whole sketch a privileged reception-structure for psychoanalysis, which can be understood as a hermeneutics of desire. Ricoeur: "Ne suffit-il pas [...] d'étendre au désir et à ses objets cette explicitation des couches de sens, cette recherche d'une 'fondation originelle' ? L'histoire de l'objet libidinal, à travers les stades de la libido, n'est-elle pas une telle explicitation, de renvoi en renvoi ?" (p. 372; p. 169). Henry notes the decisive substitution: desire is made a kind of intentionality, which is precisely what he denies.

No sooner has desire supplied the model of an unconscious production of meaning, showing that meaning can exist without being conscious, than the lived body — Merleau-Ponty's flesh — gives the model a concrete exemplification: is it not the seat of desire, a set of "signifying behaviours" in which meaning is not the ideal correlate of a theoretical intentionality but the behaviour itself, meaning caught in a body, whose principle is an "intentionality made flesh," the body as intentional body? (pp. 169–170). Henry's earlier rejection of Merleau-Ponty's motor intentionality (Chapter VII) applies here: "flesh" in Merleau-Ponty is still intentional, still a relation to the world.

Under the influence of, or simply in agreement with, the "Paris School," Ricoeur defined the analytic situation as a language situation. But the model of unconscious meaning must be applied to language itself: before any "explicit apophantic," before being formulated thematically in an ideal statement, every meaning is already at work as "meaning at work," effected by behaviour. The uttered meaning is usually only the after-the-fact expression of this operative meaning and presupposes it (p. 170). The "Paris School" is Lacan's; Henry notes Ricoeur's partial alignment with the thesis that the unconscious is structured like a language, transposed to Merleau-Ponty's operative meaning.

Transferred to perception, this analysis of language shows the same dialectic of absence and presence without which language is unintelligible. To speak is to mourn the object, give up an actual presence (the mother's, say) to find it again as absence in the sign. But perception, the most primitive experience, already figures this play: nothing is intended except against the co-intended; each intuitive fragment, each face of the object presupposes the indefinite horizon of its potential appearances. Perception is surrounded by a horizon of absence, unconsciousness, so that every actual presentation seems derisorily limited against its background. If this finite presence is that of thematically intended meaning, the totality of underlying, archaic and fundamental meanings without which the apparent meaning is unintelligible remains plunged in Night, and Ricoeur can write: "La phénoménologie devient une contestation aussi radicale que peut l'être la psychanalyse à l'égard de l'illusion du savoir immédiat de soi-même" (p. 170). The allusion to the *fort-da* game (*Beyond the Pleasure Principle*) and to language as mourning ties Ricoeur's hermeneutics to Lacan's theory of the symbol as murder of the thing.

This is because the pattern of phenomenality on which the thesis of radical finitude — ending in an overwhelming primacy of the unconscious — is cut out is perception, the world, as Ricoeur admits: "L'ambiguïté de la 'chose' devient le modèle de toute ambiguïté de la subjectivité en général et de l'intentionnalité sous toutes ses formes" (p. 376; pp. 170–171). Henry has his diagnosis in Ricoeur's own words: the unconscious of hermeneutic phenomenology is the perceptual horizon generalized.

It is a mark of Ricoeur's lucidity and probity that, while establishing this concordance, he marks the difference. In Husserl the becoming-conscious that brings sedimented meanings to light is a continuous progress unopposed in principle; like perception, the process of elucidation, though endless, happens spontaneously as the immanent teleology of intentional consciousness, raising and explicating new horizons (p. 171). Henry credits Ricoeur with recognizing the limit of the parallel.

In psychoanalysis, by contrast, conscious and unconscious systems are separated by a bar very hard to cross, requiring procedures wholly different from reflection and phenomenological elucidation. What must be revived are no longer intentional references like those between the visible and hidden faces of a cube or house, which will appear if "we walk around" them. The associations analysis confronts lack the docility of those of the world of representation, homogeneous with it, visible and legible in it. They obey another principle than intentional thought: forces. Their true meaning — the desires and drives that determine them — cannot be unveiled without first mastering those desires and drives, without a method enabling this mastery and the modification on which alone the modification of what is natured, the apparent meanings, depends (pp. 171–172). The "bar" of censorship marks where hermeneutics meets force — the "economic" problem.

Phenomenological method and analytic therapy therefore differ totally. Psychoanalysis can be the therapy it claims only by changing level: it must abandon the telos of an elucidation that remains prisoner of thematic and apparent meanings and plunge into another order of reality, a play of forces that feeds meaning. "Plunge" is literal: the analyst enters this universe of forces and becomes one of them, which, confronting the patient's force and modifying it by this confrontation — and only so — modifies in him the power that produced the meanings at work: neurotic symptoms, hallucination, phobia, stammering, forgetting (p. 172). Henry's account of the cure as an encounter of forces repeats Chapter I's: the cure works on affect and force, not on representations.

Ricoeur's subtle analyses show the analytic treatment always doubling itself. The investigative method leading to correct interpretation is only an "intellectual segment," effective only within the intersubjective relation of analyst and analysand, a relation of forces, of resistances. A resistance was at the origin of the neurosis and must be overcome before becoming-conscious is possible and interpretation can bear fruit. Victory over resistance occurs only in analysis, through transference, insofar as it reproduces the traumatic situation and seeks another outcome. The remembering Breuer's catharsis aimed at, as if it were an autonomous intellectual phenomenon, is replaced by repetition and the reawakening of the forces and drives behind the symptoms, which, as substitute satisfactions, can only fail. The transference repeats the traumatic situation as conflict of drives, since the patient seeks in the analyst the same substitute satisfaction, and the analyst's work, immersed in this lived relation, is to have him avoid the old trap (pp. 172–173). Henry reports Ricoeur faithfully because Ricoeur's account of transference supports his own thesis.

Because "knowledge" intervenes in psychoanalysis only within a dynamic, conflictual relation whose resolution alone makes possible the emergence of this knowledge as healing power, because one must do justice to Freud's thesis that "ce n'est pas l'ignorance en soi qui constitue le facteur pathogène" but the inner resistances that produced and maintain it, so that "le nom de psychanalyse ne s'applique qu'aux procédés où l'intensité du transfert est utilisée contre les résistances" (cited by Ricoeur, pp. 398, 401), Ricoeur concludes that "il y a un problème économique de la prise de conscience qui distingue entièrement la psychanalyse de la phénoménologie," and that "le philosophe formé à la réflexion phénoménologique se sent et se sait exclu de l'intelligence vive de ce qui advient dans la relation analytique" (pp. 400, 402; p. 173). The quotation from Freud ("'Wild' Psycho-Analysis," 1910) is the hinge: if ignorance is not pathogenic, consciousness is not the cure.

What Ricoeur finds under "the economic problem" is what his reading confronts from the start: the enigmatic universe of energies and forces at work as the true naturing power of the psyche and of the whole Freudian construction. The confrontation has two stages. The first leads from the description of immediate consciousness, stuck in deceptive meanings, to the power that produces them unknown to consciousness and to itself — Henry italicizes — the unconscious drives whose play topography and energetics try to circumscribe, through a great decentring of the locus of meanings, placed henceforth in the unconscious (p. 173). The "two stages" reconstruct Ricoeur's dialectic of archaeology and teleology.

This displacement is painful: the time of "anti-phenomenology," compelling man to recognize that the meanings amid which he lives come not from him but from powers that exceed him and toy with him, speech and desire especially. In this "réduction non à la conscience mais de la conscience" (p. 412), one must lose in turn the object of representation, its subject, the ego itself, and undergo the triple humiliation of being master neither of the universe, nor of others, nor of oneself (pp. 173–174). The triple humiliation adapts Freud's three blows to narcissism (Copernicus, Darwin, psychoanalysis), shifted to object, others and self.

The second stage is quite different: Ricoeur's extraordinary effort to reintroduce into the hermeneutic field what seems to escape it at the borders of psyche and organism — brute force, quasi-physical blind energy, heir to Schopenhauer's Will, which knows and wants to know nothing of the light where meaning shines. How does the drive relate to the universe of meanings deployed by intentional consciousness, which seems to define man's humanity? By entering the psyche not in itself but through "representatives," which constitute its ground. These representatives of a material, bioenergetic force differ from it as representation in general differs from what it represents. As representations, they are like those of consciousness, homogeneous with it. That they remain at first unconscious does not prevent them, determined by the structure of representation, from being representable, aspiring to representation as their true condition. "Une réalité encore inconsciente mais tendant à la conscience, et cela en tant que représentation" — such is the general essence of the psyche, understood identically by Freudian psychoanalysis and Ricoeur's hermeneutic phenomenology (p. 174). Henry's point: Ricoeur reintegrates force by turning it into its representative, so that force as such disappears again.

Not only representations whose proto-constitution is the work of desire are first delivered to night. Perceptual representations obey the same law of radical finitude, by which only one at a time is offered to the brightness of intuitive presence while the others stay in shadow, in the state the metaphysics of representation has always called "virtuality." Because psychic phenomena bear the same essence as phenomenality — consciousness as representation, as intentional — they obey the same law of transformation of virtual into actual consciousness. Interpreting psychic reality as drive-representative and representation thus allows what escapes phenomenological actuality in its original being to be brought back under the light of meaning. The hermeneutic reading of psychoanalysis becomes clear at its source: "Il y a un point où la question de la force et la question du sens coïncident : ce point est celui où la pulsion se désigne dans le psychisme par des représentations et des affects qui la 'présentent'" (p. 416; pp. 174–175). The concept of the virtual reappears from Chapter I: the hermeneutic unconscious is representational virtuality, the shadow of representation.

The theory of the representative (*Repräsentanz*), "magisterially" expounded in two passages of Ricoeur's book (pp. 137–153, 416–417), is the keystone of the hermeneutic interpretation of Freudianism. After the critique of immediate consciousness and its reduction by topographical and economic explanations, it allows the reverse movement, the recovery of meaning and the reconquest, by man dispossessed of himself, of his autonomy and freedom. What grounds this vast movement, placing psychoanalysis back in the orbit of Western thought, is the structural ontological homogeneity established between unconscious and conscious systems on either side of the bar by one definition of psychic reality, reinscribing all of it within the empire of meaning. Ricoeur is explicit: "aussi éloignés que soient les représentants primaires de la pulsion [...] ils appartiennent encore à la circonscription du sens ; ils peuvent par principe être traduits en termes de psychisme conscient ; bref, la psychanalyse est possible comme retour à la conscience parce que, d'une certaine façon, l'inconscient est homogène à la conscience ; il est son autre relatif, non pas l'absolument autre" (p. 417; pp. 175–176). Henry accepts Ricoeur's statement as the clearest formulation of the presupposition he rejects: the unconscious as the "relative other" of consciousness.

The return of meaning — the becoming-conscious of the drive's psychic representatives and, through them alone, of the original drive of desire — is not a mere return to the start; what is regained does not coincide with what was lost. If intentional consciousness, defining man's humanity, is saved, it and this humanity emerge profoundly changed. A solitary consciousness is dispossessed of what it thought it possessed, the meaning of its own existence. The decentring leads to "une autre instance du sens," which Ricoeur calls "transcendance de la parole ou position du désir" (p. 410). But the discovery of this second instance still belongs to a consciousness: the disjunction effected by Freud's topographical realism "n'est pas une disjonction à l'égard de toute conscience" (p. 418). The work of interpretation, in which private subjectivity — analyst's and analysand's — surpasses itself, ends in a consciousness of truth, or rather is the work of this consciousness, which coincides with the hermeneutic field as it constitutes itself in this work as the new focus of intelligibility from which true meaning is perceived (p. 176). The hermeneutic consciousness replaces the immediate consciousness as subject; the structure of consciousness-of-meaning survives the decentring.

It is in and through this hermeneutic field that the network of psychic processes laid bare by interpretation is built. Freud's topographical reality thus remains subordinate to a sense-giving consciousness, the scientific consciousness of truth: "Cette réalité n'existe que comme réalité diagnostiquée" (pp. 422–423). Hence the whole Freudian enterprise, as archaeology of the subject, can appear as an "extraordinary discipline of reflection," internal to it and coinciding with its movement, so that consciousness — immediate consciousness of apparent meaning — is lost only for reflection — apodictic grasp of scientific truth — to be saved (pp. 176–177). Henry's summary makes Ricoeur's project a reflective philosophy in Nabert's lineage, in which psychoanalysis serves reflection's self-recovery.

To this rigorous thought, which makes psychoanalysis and phenomenology corresponding moments of one great process of reflection, Henry puts a double question: which phenomenology fits which psychoanalysis? The answer is already in hand: a phenomenology of intentional, sense-giving consciousness fits a psychoanalysis understood as deciphering of meanings. More categorically — Henry italicizes — "c'est à l'intérieur d'un même horizon philosophique, constitué par des présuppositions ontologiques communes, que phénoménologie et psychanalyse se laissent apercevoir comme le Même : comme une herméneutique." This horizon is classical thought, dominated across its most different formulations by one concept of consciousness, of phenomenality. It is all the more essential to recognize this concept since the antithetical concept of the unconscious is delimited in relation to it (p. 177). Henry's double question reorganizes Ricoeur's debate: the convergence he celebrates is a symptom of a shared ontology.

That psychoanalysis borrows its concept of consciousness from classical thought, Freud says explicitly: "Il n'est pas besoin d'expliquer ici ce que nous appelons conscient et qui est la conscience même des philosophes et celle du grand public" (*Outline*, GW XVII 81; p. 177). The quotation of Chapter I recurs, now directed at Ricoeur.

What is this self-evident concept? Another text says: "Appelons donc 'conscience' la représentation qui est présente à notre conscience et que nous percevons comme telle, et posons que c'est là le seul sens du terme 'conscient'" (GW VIII 432). Henry italicizes: "La conscience est la représentation." Representing, placing-before, as such produces consciousness, phenomenality, which consists in being-placed-before considered in itself, so that nothing becomes conscious except as placed before, re-presented (p. 178). The formula is the volume's constant definition of the classical concept, here sharpened to an identity.

This phenomenological presupposition of the metaphysics of representation is wholly general, governing all Western thought and Husserl's phenomenology. To say consciousness is intentional, that all consciousness is consciousness of something, is to entrust the manifestation of everything, meaning included, to the original upsurge of exteriority, a primordial transcendence. Whether this upsurge is conceived as the objectification of a Subject exhausted in it or as the hatching of a Nature that illuminates it, phenomenality consists in the opening of an Outside where every object becomes visible, in the Ek-stasis of Being. Hence thoughts that tirelessly denounce Western metaphysics as metaphysics of representation remain under its sway so long as the phenomenality of Being coincides for them with this Ek-stasis (p. 178). The sentence summarizes Chapters I, IV and V: Heidegger's critique of representation remains within its ontology.

The connection of consciousness and unconscious on this ecstatic conception of phenomenality is easy to grasp: it comes down to the finitude of the place where the entity stands as ob-ject, the finitude of ek-stasis and of Being understood from it. This finitude governs the exchange of themes between Husserl's phenomenology and Freud's psychoanalysis that Ricoeur's reading reveals, ending in their identification or at least parallelism as hermeneutic disciplines. Ricoeur names it: "Toute manière d'être consciente est pour la subjectivité une manière d'être inconsciente, comme toute manière d'apparaître est corrélative d'un non-apparaître, voire d'un disparaître"; he speaks profoundly of "la signification plénière du modèle perceptif de l'inconscient pour la phénoménologie" (p. 376) — and, Henry adds, for psychoanalysis itself, insofar as it remains subject to this perceptual model, to the unthought presuppositions of a metaphysics of representation (pp. 178–179). The unconscious of finitude is the unconscious of horizon: what is absent from a perspective, not what is outside appearing.

But has the concept of the unconscious no other function than to mark the finitude of representation and ek-stasis? Might it not mean in Freud, even unconsciously — Henry italicizes — "une mise en question radicale de la métaphysique de la représentation et ainsi du mode de phénoménalité qui trouve son modèle dans la perception"? Psychoanalysis remained largely dependent on classical thought, but its true historical origin lies in "le grand philosophe Schopenhauer dont la Volonté inconsciente [...] équivaut aux instincts psychiques de la psychanalyse" (GW XII 12): a Will constituting the ground of our being and defined by the exclusion of all representation. So it is not representation, even as drive-"representative," that constitutes the Ground of the Psyche, but "l'être-hors-représentation" to which belong the fundamental forms of experience called Will, drive or desire, which, never rising before our gaze in a world, are nonetheless lived by us, in the abyssal Night of our primitive subjectivity, as what we are. Is it not remarkable that Freud designates this archaic dimension — the Ground of Being and of our own being — both as the unconscious and as Energy and Force? (pp. 179–180). Henry turns Freud's own genealogy against Ricoeur's homogenization: the unconscious as heir of Schopenhauer's will is the absolute other of representation, not its relative other.

In the 1912 "Note on the Unconscious," a singular turn occurs. The "justification" of the unconscious by the latency of most psychic contents — virtual representations, such as memories we no longer think of — gives way to a consideration of the force and efficacy of unconscious thoughts, which they seem to owe to their very unconsciousness. As if action were conceivable only in the Night of a subjectivity before the world, where every power and force seizes itself immediately without the slightest gap, without a Difference by which it could represent, contemplate and see itself — but no longer act, since, separated from itself by the distance of a first objectivity, it would be forbidden to rejoin itself, seize its power and deploy it (p. 180). The 1912 text (written in English for the Society for Psychical Research) is Henry's evidence that Freud's "dynamic" unconscious points beyond the representational one — and his reading of it applies the thesis of Chapter VII on power and self-affection.

The emergence of an "efficient unconscious" (GW VIII 435), of action finding its site and possibility in the radical immanence of an archaic subjectivity, carries Freud's thought outside the field of a metaphysics of meaning. The competence of a hermeneutics subject to the presuppositions of Western thought is in question. If the force and Energy constituting the Ground of the Psyche find the principial possibility of their being — of acting — outside representation, is the problem really to bring this being back to representation, to inscribe it at all costs in the hermeneutic field? Has the cure not shown that interpretation, the declension of meanings, is decisively subordinate to the work of forces and to what they are in themselves before any interpretation? Must not this being of force be thought in its own right? Does its status outside representation mean the negation of all phenomenality — or only of the phenomenality that shines on the Greek phenomenon and that modern philosophy gathers in representation? (pp. 180–181). The last question restates the alternative of the whole volume: non-representational means either unconscious or differently phenomenal.

At the bottom of the psychic unconscious there are not only unconscious representations — which in any case do not exist, existing only as the illusion of a metaphysics that conceives being solely as being-represented and realizes it in that form even where the phenomenality it is meant to promote has vanished — there is affect. And, by Freud's decisive statement, affect is never unconscious: "Il est de l'essence d'un sentiment d'être perçu, donc d'être connu de la conscience" (*Metapsychology*, GW X 276). That the ground of the psychic unconscious is never unconscious, and is called so only because it insurmountably rejects the world's visibility, set phenomenology and psychoanalysis one essential task: not to return this ground to a light it excludes in principle, but "de penser pour elle-même la dimension archaïque où la phénoménalité se phénoménalise originellement comme affectivité." Testing psychoanalysis by phenomenology: urging it to understand otherwise than negatively, by mere rejection of *Bewusstheit*, the status of the deepest layers of the psyche that determine our whole life, "qui sont la vie." Testing phenomenology by psychoanalysis: imposing the same programme, requiring a rigorous definition of the archaic dimension of the Psyche as irreducible to the natural entity, to an "unconscious" like that of things or of neuronal biochemical processes (p. 181). Henry reformulates Ricoeur's own phrase — the mutual testing of the two disciplines — with his own content: the common task is a phenomenology of affect. The denial of unconscious representations (Chapter VII) is reaffirmed.

The problem, then, is not to offer an interpretation — and a purely hypothetical one, as Ricoeur concedes: "Cette fonction de *Repräsentanz* est certainement un postulat ; Freud n'en donne aucune preuve" (p. 139) — of how material processes are represented in the Psyche, but, within the Psyche, within phenomenality itself, to draw the line separating not the conscious and unconscious systems naively reified by the topography but "deux modes hétérogènes et originellement conjoints selon lesquels se phénoménalise originellement la phénoménalité : dans l'Ek-stase d'un monde, dans la chair impressionnelle de l'affect." Heterogeneous, since the second excludes the ecstatic dimension in which the first occurs. Originally joined, because the gaze moving in Ek-stasis and Ek-stasis itself affect themselves, "en sorte que rien ne parvient dans la lumière du visible qui ne soit déjà parvenu en soi dans l'étreinte invisible de son propre pathos" (pp. 181–182). The duality and co-originarity of the two modes — announced in Chapter II as the two affections and in Chapter III as the major problem of philosophy — is Henry's alternative to the topography.

Because the two modes are heterogeneous, one never has to attempt the impossible leap from the first into the second. Becoming conscious of the unconscious takes place exclusively within the field of representation; it is representation's own movement, the actualization of what otherwise remains virtual, not yet formed by the act of consciousness that will show it. Because they are originally joined, the actualization of a representation or the ban on it presuppose the self-affection of the process of coming into ecstatic light — Henry italicizes — the "affect qui produit ou qui refoule la représentation." Hence the destiny of representations is subject to that of affects; hence abreaction and repetition in the transference are the condition of becoming-conscious — which is never, contrary to the illusion of classical philosophy and hermeneutics, the end for which affective and instinctual modification would only be a means, "comme si le but n'était pas la vie elle-même, son bien-être ou son bonheur" but once again becoming-conscious, intentional knowledge of an objective and thus "true" meaning (p. 182). Henry's theory of the cure follows: its aim is life's affective transformation, not knowledge — the sharpest point of disagreement with Ricoeur's reflective teleology.

Hence too it is misleading to juxtapose, at the bottom of the Psyche and under the same title of psychic "representative" of the drive, unconscious representations and affects: affectivity and representation are not on the same plane, "la première étant le naturant et la seconde le naturé." The very designation of affect as "representative" falsifies its being and illustrates the attempt of a metaphysics of representation to take back what escapes it in principle. Nor is affect an equivalent of the drive, its psychic double. The task of a radical phenomenology — which alone can give psychoanalysis a philosophy (cf. François Roustang, "Une philosophie pour la psychanalyse ?", *Critique* 1985) — is to think their identity. The inner connection of Force and Affectivity is not only that Affectivity, as the self-affection of force, is the essence of every force. Because affect, in its self-affection, is radically passive toward itself and so charged with its own being to the point of unbearability, it aspires to discharge itself; it is the very movement of the drive, which in Freud designates the endogenous excitation that never ceases — "en d'autres termes, l'auto-affection en tant que constitutive de l'essence de la subjectivité absolue et de la vie" (pp. 182–183). This is the volume's last and most compact thesis: the drive is self-affection's own pressure, life's burden of itself seeking discharge. It completes the reading of the *Project*'s endogenous excitations in Chapter II and is the core of *Généalogie*'s final chapter. Roustang's article discussed *Généalogie de la psychanalyse*, which explains the reference.

The hermeneutic reading of psychoanalysis has had not only the immense merit of systematically elucidating the domain of meaning as intentional meaning. By provoking first the confrontation and then the collaboration of phenomenology and psychoanalysis, it has raised many often decisive problems and obliges us to become more sharply aware of the presuppositions that have guided the great thought of our time in a domain where the understanding of what makes man's humanity is decided. By inviting us to reflect on these often identical presuppositions and perhaps to question them, "elle a ouvert à la recherche des horizons nouveaux" (p. 183). The closing courtesy to Ricoeur is genuine but framed: his merit is to have exposed, by the very success of the convergence he achieved, the shared ontology of representation that a phenomenology of life must overturn — the task with which the volume began in Chapter I.

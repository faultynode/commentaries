# ***Die Grundprobleme der Phänomenologie*** **(GA 24\)**

# **Introduction**

## **§ 1\. Exposition and General Articulation of the Theme**

The opening paragraph sets the lecture course's task with deliberate severity: not to expound a Begriff (concept) of phenomenology and then derive consequences from it, but to *raise* (stellen), *work out* (ausarbeiten), and partially *bring closer to a solution* (einer Lösung näherzubringen) the basic problems of phenomenology. The asymmetry is decisive. Heidegger explicitly inverts the textbook order in which definition would precede application. The Begriff of phenomenology, he insists, must develop *out of* what phenomenology takes as its theme and how it investigates its object — not the reverse. The point is methodological in the strictest sense: phenomenology cannot be characterized in advance because it has no domain that could be circumscribed from outside; its self-understanding is to be won only through the actual labor of putting its fundamental problems to work. The footnote at this point, identifying the lecture course as a "new working-out of the third division of the first part of *Being and Time*" (the never-published "Zeit und Sein"), is one of the most important biographical-philosophical facts in Heidegger's corpus. *Sein und Zeit*, published only months earlier, had broken off after Division Two of Part One; GA 24 is Heidegger's attempt to deliver what the book promised but did not deliver — the temporal interpretation of being itself. The aim, he states with finality, is the "clarification of these \[basic problems\] out of their ground" — *Aufklärung aus ihrem Grunde* — a phrase that already announces that grounding will not mean derivation from axioms but exhibition of what makes the problems possible in the first place.

The second paragraph specifies what the task is *not*. The negative formulation has two layers. First: the lecture is not historical reportage about a contemporary philosophical movement; second, and more sharply, it does not even content itself with treating *what* phenomenology treats so that one could then report on what was discussed. The crucial distinction Heidegger draws is between *handeln von* (to treat of) and *mithandeln* (to act-along, to participate). The lecture itself is to be an enactment of the matter, and the students "sollen mithandeln bzw. mithandeln lernen" — they are to *do philosophy along with* the lecturer, or to learn to do so. The point culminates in the formulation: "Es geht nicht darum, Philosophie zu kennen, sondern philosophieren zu können" — it is not a matter of *knowing* philosophy but of *being able to philosophize*. This is Heidegger's pedagogical and existential creed: philosophy is not a body of doctrine to be acquired but a possibility of Dasein to be activated. The use of the verb *einleiten* ("to lead in," from which *Einleitung*, "introduction," derives) is exploited in its literal sense: an introduction "leads in" to philosophizing, it does not introduce a *subject matter*.

The third paragraph poses the question of how the Grundprobleme are themselves to be identified. The danger Heidegger anticipates is dogmatism: that a list of "basic problems" should simply be accepted *auf gut Glauben* (in good faith). The answer is methodologically programmatic: the basic problems will not be reached *direkt* but only on the *Umweg* (detour) of treating certain *Einzelprobleme* (individual problems). The Grundprobleme are to be "shelled out" (herausschälen) from these particular problems, and their systematic interconnection determined. This already commits Heidegger to what he will later call the phenomenological-critical procedure of Part One: rather than expounding the fundamental question of the meaning of being directly, he will approach it through four traditional theses about being. The understanding so won is meant to make it evident in what manner philosophy is *necessarily required as science* through these problems.

The fourth paragraph supplies the rough articulation of the three parts: concrete phenomenological questions as introduction to the basic problems; the basic problems in their systematic ordering and grounding; and the scientific manner of treating these problems together with the idea of phenomenology. This tripartite structure mirrors the projected (but never published) structure of *Being and Time* as a whole — preparatory analysis, fundamental ontological exposition, and methodological reflection on the procedure — and the lecture course should be read as Heidegger's serious attempt to carry through what the published portion of *Sein und Zeit* had only set in motion.

The fifth paragraph reasserts the methodological worry: the way of consideration leads from particular problems to the basic problems, and the question therefore arises how the Einzelprobleme themselves are to be selected. Is the choice left to chance or arbitrariness? The introductory consideration is meant precisely to guard against the appearance of arbitrariness. There is here a delicate balance Heidegger must strike: he cannot derive the choice deductively from a concept of phenomenology (since that concept is precisely what is to be won), yet he must motivate the choice in such a way that it does not appear capricious. The whole of §§2–4 will be devoted to providing this motivation by securing the concept of philosophy as science of being.

The sixth paragraph turns to the apparently most natural way of selecting the particular problems — namely, derivation from the concept of phenomenology — and rejects it. The argument has two steps. First, the simple observation that the concept of phenomenology is what is to be obtained, so the derivation would be circular. Second, the more subtle point that even orientation toward a "present-day" concept of phenomenology — what currently passes under that name — would not suffice, because (a) the phenomenological movement itself contains varying determinations of its essence and tasks, and (b) even if one could unify these into an average concept, it would remain questionable whether such an averaged conception could provide orientation regarding which concrete problems to select. The implicit critique is directed at Husserl's followers and at the various phenomenological schools (Munich, Göttingen, Freiburg) that, in Heidegger's view, had each domesticated phenomenology into a particular position.

The seventh paragraph drives the critique further. The reason that orientation toward the contemporary phenomenological landscape would fail is that for such orientation to function, it would have to be presupposed that phenomenological research has *attained the center of philosophical problematic* and has determined its essence out of the possibilities of that problematic. This is not the case — and so far from being the case, Heidegger declares, that one of the principal aims of the lecture course is to show that phenomenology, grasped in its fundamental tendency, can represent nothing other than "the more explicit and more radical understanding of the idea of scientific philosophy" as that idea has been pursued from antiquity through Hegel in continuously interconnected efforts. This is one of the most programmatic statements of the early Heideggerian project: phenomenology is not a novelty of the twentieth century but the radicalization of what the entire Western tradition of *prima philosophia* has always wanted. The pivot point is the identification of phenomenology with the tradition of metaphysics-as-science.

The eighth paragraph attacks a particular self-understanding of phenomenology that Heidegger considers fatally inadequate: phenomenology as *Vorwissenschaft* (preparatory science) that prepares the ground for the "proper philosophical disciplines" — logic, ethics, aesthetics, philosophy of religion. The polemical edge here is sharp, directed against the Munich-Göttingen reception of Husserl that took phenomenology to be a propaedeutic providing intuitive evidence for these established disciplines. Heidegger's counter-thesis is twofold: such a determination uncritically takes over the traditional stock of philosophical disciplines without asking whether phenomenology might not itself *shake* (erschüttern) and *call into question* this very stock — without asking whether phenomenology might not contain the possibility of reversing the *Veräußerlichung* (externalization, alienation into outwardness) of philosophy into these compartmentalized disciplines and of *re-appropriating* (neu anzueignen) and bringing back to life the great tradition out of its essential answers in their fundamental tendencies. The thesis follows: phenomenology is neither one philosophical science among others nor the propaedeutic science for the rest; rather, the expression "phenomenology" is the *title for the method of scientific philosophy as such*. This identification of phenomenology with method is essential to the entire course: phenomenology will be characterized not by a subject matter but by *how* it proceeds — and the second half of §5 will identify the three "constitutive pieces" (Grundstücke) of this method: reduction, construction, destruction.

The ninth paragraph extracts the immediate methodological consequence: clarifying the idea of phenomenology is equivalent (gleichbedeutend) to exposing the concept of scientific philosophy. This still gives us no determination of the content of what phenomenology means, and even less does it show how the method is carried out — but it indicates *that and why* one must dispense with orientation toward any present-day phenomenological tendency. Concrete phenomenological problems are therefore not to be deduced from a dogmatically posited concept of phenomenology; one allows oneself to be led to them through a more general and provisional consideration of the concept of scientific philosophy itself. This consideration will be carried out *in einer stillschweigenden Anmessung* — "in a silent measuring-against" — the fundamental tendencies of Western philosophy from antiquity to Hegel. The word "Anmessung" is precise: not derivation, not citation, but a tacit measuring of the present inquiry against the historical thrust of the tradition. The naming of "Antike bis Hegel" as the relevant span is itself substantive: post-Hegelian philosophy (with the partial exception of Nietzsche, whose presence in this course is muted) does not figure as a tradition against which to measure phenomenology.

The tenth paragraph begins the historical-conceptual orientation. In early antiquity *philosophia* meant science in general; later individual sciences — medicine, mathematics — detached themselves from philosophy, and the name remained for that science which underlies and encompasses all the special sciences. Philosophy thus became "science as such" (Wissenschaft schlechthin) and increasingly identified itself as the first and highest science — or, in the language of German Idealism, *absolute* science. If that is what philosophy is, then the expression "scientific philosophy" contains a pleonasm: it says "scientific absolute science." It would suffice to say "philosophy," and the scientific character would be contained in that. Why then does Heidegger nevertheless append the adjective "wissenschaftlich" to "Philosophie"? Because conceptions of philosophy prevail that not only *endanger* but *negate* philosophy's character as science as such. These conceptions are not recent; they have run alongside the development of scientific philosophy since there has been philosophy as science. On this view, philosophy is supposed to be not only and not primarily a theoretical science but is to guide practical comprehension of things and their interconnection, to regulate the interpretation of Dasein and its meaning — philosophy is *Welt- und Lebensweisheit*, world- and life-wisdom; philosophy is to give a *Weltanschauung*. So scientific philosophy can be distinguished from worldview-philosophy. With this the next section's theme has been announced: the distinction between philosophy as science and philosophy as worldview-formation, and the question whether this distinction is legitimate or must be resolved into one of its members.

The final paragraph of §1 reasserts the provisional character of the whole proceeding. The discussion of the difference between scientific philosophy and worldview-philosophy is meant to clarify the concept of philosophy and put us in a position to justify the selection of individual problems to be treated in Part One. But Heidegger reminds us that these discussions about the concept of philosophy can only be preliminary — preliminary not only with respect to the lecture course as a whole but preliminary *überhaupt*, in general. The reason is given in one of the most characteristic formulations of the early Heidegger's conception of philosophical method: "Denn der Begriff der Philosophie ist das eigenste und höchste Resultat ihrer selbst" — the concept of philosophy is its own most proper and highest result. Likewise, the question whether philosophy is possible at all can be decided only through philosophy. This circular structure — philosophy presupposing what it sets out to determine and being its own ground — is not, for Heidegger, a defect; it is the inescapable structure of any inquiry into the conditions of inquiry itself. It anticipates what *Being and Time* §32 called the *hermeneutic circle*, here transposed onto the level of the meta-philosophical reflection that opens the lecture course.

## **§ 2\. The Concept of Philosophy. Philosophy and Worldview**

Heidegger begins by announcing his methodological strategy: in treating the difference between scientific philosophy and worldview-philosophy, he will start from the second member of the opposition, and indeed first from the verbal concept *Weltanschauung* itself. The word, he notes, is not a translation from Greek or Latin — there is no Greek expression like *kosmotheōria* — but a specifically German coinage that arose within philosophy itself. Its first appearance is in Kant's *Kritik der Urteilskraft*, where it has its "natural" sense: worldview as the contemplation of the sensibly given world, *mundus sensibilis*; worldview as simple comprehension of nature in the widest sense. This usage continues in Goethe and Alexander von Humboldt, but dies out in the 1830s under the influence of a new meaning given to the term by the Romantics, principally by Schelling. Schelling's *Einleitung zu dem Entwurf eines Systems der Naturphilosophie* of 1799 is cited: intelligence is productive in a twofold way, either blindly and unconsciously, or freely and with consciousness — unconsciously productive in worldview, with consciousness in the creation of an ideal world. Heidegger emphasizes two features of Schelling's usage: worldview is no longer assigned simply to sensible contemplation but to intelligence, even if unconscious intelligence; and the moment of productivity — of independent forming of intuition — is foregrounded. With this, the word approaches its present-day meaning: a self-enacted, productive, and consciously articulated manner of comprehending and interpreting the totality of beings (*das All des Seienden*). Schelling already speaks of a "schematism" of worldview, i.e., of a schematized form for the various possible, factically arising worldviews. Crucially, such intuition of the world need not be carried out with theoretical intention or by means of theoretical science. Heidegger then assembles a quick gallery of nineteenth-century uses — Hegel's "moral worldview" in the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, Görres's "poetic worldview," Ranke's "religious and Christian worldview," democratic and pessimistic worldviews, the medieval worldview, Schleiermacher's claim that our knowledge of God is completed only with worldview, even Bismarck's offhand letter to his fiancée — to demonstrate that *Weltanschauung* always already includes *Lebensanschauung*, a view of life as well as of nature. The worldview arises from a comprehensive *Besinnung* (reflective deliberation) on world and human Dasein, and does so in varying modes: explicitly and consciously in individuals, or through inherited assumption of a prevailing worldview. One *grows up into* such a worldview and *lives oneself into* it. Worldview is determined by environment — people, race, social standing, stage of cultural development — and every explicitly formed worldview arises from a *natürliche Weltanschauung* (natural worldview), a horizon of takings-of-the-world and determinations of human Dasein that are given more or less explicitly with every Dasein. The natural worldview must be distinguished from the explicitly formed, "cultivated" worldview (Bildungsweltanschauung).

The next paragraph drives home the decisive point about the *kind* of cognition worldview is: it is not a matter of theoretical knowledge — neither in its origin nor in its use. It is not retained in memory like a stock of knowledge but is a matter of *zusammenhaltende Überzeugung* — a "holding-together" conviction — that more or less explicitly and directly determines conduct (*Handel und Wandel*, a German idiom for dealings and behavior). Worldview is essentially related to the present-day Dasein in its concreteness: it is *Wegweisung* (signposting, direction-giving) and *Kraft* (force) for that Dasein in its immediate distress. Whether the worldview is determined by superstition and prejudice, or rests purely on scientific knowledge and experience, or — as is typical — mixes superstition with knowledge and prejudice with reflection, makes no difference to its essence. The point is not epistemic certification but existential function. Worldview, by its very being, *guides life*.

Heidegger then notes that this rough characterization will have to suffice; a strict factual definition would have to be obtained on a different path — one to be opened later, in the lecture course's eventual confrontation with the question of the structure of Dasein itself. He cites Jaspers's *Psychologie der Weltanschauungen*: worldviews are "ideas, the ultimate and total of the human, both subjectively as lived-experience and force and disposition, and objectively as the world shaped into objects." For Heidegger's present purpose — distinguishing worldview-philosophy from scientific philosophy — the decisive insight is that worldview, by its very meaning, arises out of the respective factical Dasein of the human being in accordance with its factical possibilities of reflection and stance-taking, and so arises *for* that factical Dasein. Worldview is something that exists historically out of, with, and for factical Dasein. A *philosophical* worldview, then, is one that is to be cultivated and communicated specifically and explicitly, or at least predominantly, through philosophy — i.e., through theoretical speculation, with artistic and religious interpretations of world and Dasein bracketed out. The sting of the definition follows: on this conception, the philosophical worldview is not a by-product of philosophy but its actual goal and essence. Philosophy according to its very concept *is* worldview-philosophy. The differentia that supposedly elevates philosophy above the special sciences (which treat only particular regions of world and Dasein) and above artistic and religious comportments (which do not primarily ground themselves in theoretical comportment) is that philosophy aims at the universal of world and the ultimate of Dasein — at the whence, whither, and wherefore of world and life — in the mode of theoretical knowledge of the world. That philosophy has the formation of a worldview as its goal seems beyond question.

The next paragraph develops the apparent self-evidence of this conception. If worldview-formation is philosophy's task, this must determine philosophy's essence and concept. Philosophy is worldview-philosophy, it seems, so essentially that one might even reject the appended adjective as redundant. To want to add "scientific philosophy" as a further specification appears a misunderstanding — because the philosophical worldview, one says, is *of course* meant to be scientific. By this one understands two things: first, it should take into account the results of the various sciences and use them to construct the picture of the world and the interpretation of Dasein; second, it should be scientific in that the formation of the worldview proceeds strictly according to the rules of scientific thinking. This conception of philosophy as theoretical worldview-formation is so self-evident that it universally and broadly determines the concept of philosophy and accordingly prescribes, in vulgar consciousness, what one is supposed to expect of philosophy. Conversely, if philosophy fails to answer the worldview-questions, vulgar consciousness regards it as *nichtig* — null, trifling. Claims made on philosophy and stances taken toward it are regulated by this representation of it as scientific worldview-formation. Whether philosophy succeeds or fails in this task, one points to its history and sees there the unambiguous documentation that philosophy treats cognitively of the ultimate questions: of nature, of the soul (i.e., of the freedom and history of the human), of God.

If philosophy is scientific worldview-formation, then the distinction between "scientific philosophy" and "worldview-philosophy" collapses. The two in one constitute its essence, and ultimately the worldview-task acquires the decisive weight. This also appears to be Kant's view — Kant, who placed the scientific character of philosophy on a new foundation. Heidegger therefore turns to Kant's well-known distinction from the Introduction to the *Logik* between philosophy *nach dem Schulbegriff* (according to the concept of the school) and philosophy *nach dem Weltbegriff* (according to the worldly concept) — a distinction often invoked as evidence for the difference between scientific philosophy and worldview-philosophy, and more precisely as evidence that even Kant, for whom the scientific character of philosophy stood at the center of interest, conceived philosophy itself as worldview-philosophy.

The next paragraph expounds Kant's *Schulbegriff*. Philosophy according to the concept of the school, or, as Kant also says, philosophy in the scholastic signification, is the doctrine of the skill of reason, which contains two pieces: first, "a sufficient supply of cognitions of reason out of concepts"; second, "a systematic connection of these cognitions, or their combination in the idea of a whole." Kant has in mind here that philosophy in the scholastic signification includes both the connection of the formal principles of thought and reason in general, and the discussion and determination of those concepts that underlie the comprehension of the world (i.e., for Kant, nature) as necessary presuppositions. Philosophy according to the school-concept is the whole of the formal and material basic concepts and principles of cognition of reason. This is, in modern terms, what we might call *theoretical philosophy as systematic doctrine* — what Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* itself develops.

Then Kant's *Weltbegriff*. Philosophy according to the worldly concept, or, as Kant also says, philosophy in the cosmopolitan signification (in sensu cosmico), is defined by Kant as "a science of the highest maxim of the use of our reason, insofar as one understands by maxim the inner principle of choice among various ends." Philosophy according to the worldly concept treats of that for the sake of which all use of reason — even the use of reason in philosophy itself — is what it is. Kant continues: philosophy in this latter signification is the science of the relation of all use of cognition and reason to the ultimate end of human reason, to which, as the highest end, all other ends are subordinate and in which they must combine into unity. The field of philosophy in this cosmopolitan signification reduces to four questions: What can I know? What ought I to do? What may I hope? What is the human? The first three concentrate in the fourth — what is the human? — because from the clarification of what the human is, the determination of the final ends of human reason follows. And to these final ends philosophy in the sense of the school-concept must also be related.

The eighth paragraph poses the question: does this Kantian distinction between philosophy in the scholastic signification and philosophy in the cosmopolitan signification coincide with the distinction between scientific philosophy and worldview-philosophy? The answer is *Ja und nein* — yes and no. Yes, insofar as Kant distinguishes *within* the concept of philosophy and on the basis of this distinction moves the ultimate and limit-questions of human Dasein into the center. No, insofar as philosophy according to the worldly concept does not have the task of cultivating a worldview in the characterized sense. What Kant ultimately has in view — without being able to say this explicitly — even as the task of philosophy in the cosmopolitan signification is nothing other than the *a priori* and to that extent *ontological* delimitation of the determinations that belong to the essence of human Dasein and that also determine the concept of a worldview in general. As the most fundamental a priori determination of the essence of human Dasein, Kant knows the proposition: the human is a being that exists as an end in itself. Even philosophy according to the worldly concept has to do, for Kant, with essential determinations — with what belongs to world as such, to Dasein as such, and thereby to worldview as such. The methodological character of philosophy according to the *Weltbegriff* is for Kant exactly the same as that of philosophy according to the *Schulbegriff* — only Kant, for reasons Heidegger does not pursue here, does not see the connection between the two; more precisely, he does not see the ground on which both concepts might be grounded in a common originary basis. (Heidegger promises to return to this, and in fact §13 of GA 24 will discuss it.) For now it is only clear that one cannot appeal to Kant for the conception of philosophy as scientific worldview-formation. Kant, fundamentally, knows philosophy only as science. This is a striking reading: Heidegger rescues Kant from the worldview-philosophical reception of the *Weltbegriff* and reinscribes the *Weltbegriff* as a still-ontological enterprise concerned with the a priori essence-structures of human Dasein.

The ninth paragraph carries through the argument that worldview-formation cannot be the task of philosophy. The worldview, as we saw, grows up in each case out of a factical Dasein in accordance with its factical possibilities and is what it is in each case *for* this determinate Dasein — which, Heidegger insists, in no way amounts to a relativism of worldviews. What such a worldview says can be brought into propositions and rules that by their meaning are referred to a determinate really existing world, to a determinate factically existing Dasein. *All* worldview and view-of-life is *setzend* — positing — i.e., it relates to beings as being. It posits beings; it is *positive*. Worldview belongs to every Dasein and, like Dasein, is determined factically and historically. To worldview belongs this multiple positivity: it is rooted in a Dasein that is in such-and-such a way, it relates as such to the existing world, and it interprets the factically existing Dasein. Because this positivity belongs to the essence of worldview and to worldview-formation as such — i.e., the referredness to beings, to the existing world, to existing Dasein — therefore worldview-formation cannot be the task of philosophy. This does not exclude but rather includes that philosophy itself is an *ausgezeichnete Urform der Weltanschauung* — a "distinguished originary form of worldview." Philosophy can and must show, among many other things, that to the essence of Dasein something like worldview belongs; philosophy can and must delimit what makes up the structure of a worldview in general. But it can never cultivate and posit any *particular* worldview as this or that. Philosophy is in its essence not worldview-formation, but perhaps just for that reason has an elementary and principled relation to all worldview-formation — including the non-theoretical, factically historical kind. The dialectic is delicate: philosophy is not worldview-formation, but is the *condition of possibility* of any worldview-formation insofar as it delimits the structure of worldview as such.

The final paragraph of §2 marks the decisive transition. The thesis that worldview-formation is not the task of philosophy holds, of course, only under the presupposition that philosophy does not positively relate to beings as this and that, positing them. Can this presupposition — that philosophy does not relate positively to beings as the sciences do — be justified? With what then is philosophy to be concerned, if not with beings, with what is, with beings as a whole? What is *not* is the *nothing*. Is philosophy supposed, as absolute science, to have the nothing as its theme? Apart from nature, history, God, space, number — what can there be? All of these we say *are*, even if in different senses. We call them *beings*. Referred to them, whether theoretically or practically, we comport ourselves toward beings. Outside these beings there is nothing. Perhaps there is no other being than those enumerated — but perhaps there is yet something which, although it *is not*, nonetheless *es gibt* — "there is" — in a sense still to be determined. More: ultimately there must be something which *must* be (es geben muß), so that we can make beings as beings accessible to ourselves and comport ourselves toward them — something which is not itself a being but which must be there, must "be given" (geben), so that we can in general experience and understand anything like beings. Beings we can grasp as such, as beings, only if we understand something like *being*. If we did not understand, however roughly and unconceptually, what *Wirklichkeit* (actuality) says, then the actual would remain hidden from us. If we did not understand what *Realität* (reality) means, then the real would remain inaccessible. If we did not understand what life and aliveness say, then we would not be able to comport ourselves toward the living. If we did not understand what *Existenz* and existentiality say, then we ourselves could not exist as Dasein. If we did not understand what *Bestand* and constancy mean, then existing geometrical relations or number-relations would remain closed to us. We must understand actuality, reality, aliveness, existentiality, constancy in order to comport ourselves positively to determinate actual, real, living, existing, constant beings. We must understand being so that we can be delivered over to a being-world (an eine seiende Welt ausgeliefert sein) in order to exist in it and to be able to be our own being-Dasein itself. We must be able to understand actuality before any experience of the actual. This understanding of actuality or being in the widest sense, over against the experience of beings, is *früher* — earlier — than the latter in a determinate sense. The pre-given understanding of being prior to all factical experience of beings does not mean that we must have an explicit concept of being beforehand in order to experience beings theoretically or practically. We must understand being — being, which itself may no longer be called a being, being, which does not occur among other beings as a being, but which nevertheless *must be given* (geben muß) and which *is also given* (auch gibt) in the understanding of being, in *Seinsverständnis*. With this paragraph the entire issue of the lecture course is positioned: there is something — being — which is not a being, yet must be "there," must be "given," for any comportment to beings to be possible at all. This pre-conceptual *Seinsverständnis* is the proper theme of philosophy, and the next section will identify it explicitly.

## **§ 3\. Philosophy as Science of Being**

The opening paragraph states the thesis that has been preparing itself throughout §2: being is the genuine and sole theme of philosophy. Heidegger insists immediately that this is not his own invention but the very theme that comes alive with the beginning of philosophy in antiquity and works itself out in its grandest form in Hegel's *Logic*. The assertion is therefore framed as a recovery, not an innovation. Negatively stated: philosophy is *not* science of beings (Seiendem), but of being (Sein), or — in the Greek expression — *ontology*. Heidegger insists that "ontology" is to be taken in the widest possible sense, not in the narrower meaning the term has in Scholasticism or in modern philosophy in Descartes and Leibniz, where it functioned as a specific discipline within metaphysics (metaphysica generalis as distinct from metaphysica specialis with its three branches of rational psychology, rational cosmology, and rational theology). For Heidegger, ontology in the widest sense names the philosophical inquiry into being as such, prior to and grounding any regional ontology.

The next paragraph develops what it means to discuss the basic problems of phenomenology under this thesis. To do so is nothing other than to ground this very claim — that philosophy is science of being, and how it is so — from the ground up. It means demonstrating the possibility and necessity of the absolute science of being and exhibiting its character through the path of investigation itself. Philosophy is the theoretical-conceptual interpretation of being, of being's structure and its possibilities. It is *ontological*. Worldview, by contrast, is positing cognition of beings and positing stance-taking toward beings — not ontological but *ontic*. Worldview-formation falls outside the circle of tasks of philosophy *not* because philosophy is in an incomplete state and does not yet suffice to give a univocal and universally convincing answer to the worldview-questions, but because philosophy is fundamentally *not* referred to beings. The distinction is one of principle, not of progress. Philosophy does not divest itself of worldview-formation out of a *deficiency* but on the basis of an *advantage* (Vorzug): that philosophy treats of what every positing of beings, including the worldview-positing, must essentially already presuppose. The distinction between scientific philosophy and worldview-philosophy therefore collapses — not because, as it earlier seemed, scientific philosophy has worldview-formation as its highest end and must therefore be *aufgehoben* into worldview-philosophy, but because the concept of a worldview-philosophy is itself a *Unbegriff* — a non-concept, a misconception. It says that philosophy as science of being is supposed to carry out determinate stance-takings and determinate positings of beings. The concept of a worldview-philosophy, Heidegger declares, is — if one understands the concept of philosophy and its history even approximately — a *hölzernes Eisen*, "wooden iron," a contradiction in terms. If one member of the distinction is a non-concept, the other must also be inadequately determined. Once one has seen that worldview-philosophy is in principle impossible if it is to be philosophy, one no longer needs the qualifying adjective "scientific" to designate philosophy; that philosophy is scientific lies in its very concept. That all great philosophies since antiquity have understood themselves more or less explicitly as ontology and have sought themselves as such can be historically shown, just as it can be shown that these attempts repeatedly failed and *why* they had to fail. Heidegger refers to his lecture courses of the two previous semesters on ancient philosophy and on the history of philosophy from Thomas Aquinas to Kant in which he conducted this historical demonstration. (These are GA 22 and GA 23: *Die Grundbegriffe der antiken Philosophie* and *Geschichte der Philosophie von Thomas v. Aquin bis Kant*.) In GA 24 itself he will not draw on this historical proof but will rather try to ground philosophy from itself, insofar as philosophy is a *Werk der Freiheit des Menschen* — a work of human freedom. Philosophy must justify itself out of itself as universal ontology. The phrase "work of human freedom" is striking: philosophy is not a natural product, not a cultural artifact, not a historical inheritance, but an *act* of free disclosure of being that must be enacted again.

The next paragraph pulls back from the polemical assertion and reminds us that the proposition — philosophy is science of being — remains so far merely a bare assertion. Accordingly, the exclusion of worldview-formation from philosophy's tasks is not yet justified. The distinction between scientific philosophy and worldview-philosophy was brought in only to clarify the concept of philosophy *vorläufig* (provisionally) and to delimit it against the vulgar concept. Clarification and delimitation in turn served the purpose of grounding the selection of the concrete phenomenological problems initially to be treated and removing from this selection the appearance of complete arbitrariness. Heidegger is keeping his methodological promises strictly: every step has been propaedeutic.

Heidegger then articulates the formal contrast between philosophy and the positive sciences. We will henceforth understand by philosophy "scientific philosophy and nothing else." Accordingly, all non-philosophical sciences have *beings* as their theme — and indeed in such a way that beings are *vorgegeben* (pre-given) to them as beings. The beings are posited in advance; they are for these sciences a *positum*. All propositions of the non-philosophical sciences, including those of mathematics, are *positive* propositions. Heidegger therefore calls all non-philosophical sciences *positive sciences*, in contradistinction to philosophy. The terminology has nothing to do with "positivism"; it derives from the Latin *positum*, "that which is posited or given in advance." Positive sciences treat of beings, that is, in each case of determinate *regions* (Gebiete) — for example, of nature. Within this region, scientific questioning carves out determinate *districts* (Bezirke): nature as physical-material lifeless nature and nature as living nature. The region of the living is articulated into individual fields (Felder): the plant world, the animal world. Another region of beings is beings as history, whose districts include the history of art, of the state, of science, and of religion. Yet another region is the pure space of geometry, which is carved out of the pre-theoretically discovered space of the environing-world (umweltlichen Raum). Beings of these regions are known to us, even though initially and for the most part we are not in a position to delimit them sharply and unambiguously against one another. We can, however, for a provisional characterization that suffices practically for positive science, always *name* a being that falls as a case into the region. We can always, as it were, "pass ourselves" a determinate being from a determinate region as an example. The actual division of regions takes place historically not according to a pre-set plan of a system of sciences, but according to the respective fundamental questioning of the positive sciences themselves. This last point is significant: regional differentiation is historically driven by the inner movement of inquiry, not architectonically imposed.

Heidegger then shifts the contrast onto the philosophical side. Beings we can always and easily give and represent to ourselves from some region; we can, as we customarily say, "think something" thereby. But how does it stand with the *object* of philosophy? Can one represent to oneself something like *being*? Does not vertigo seize one in the attempt? Indeed, we are at first at a loss and grasp into the void. A being — that is something, a table, chair, tree, sky, body, words, action. A being, yes — but being? Such a thing appears as the *Nichts*, the nothing — and no less a philosopher than Hegel has said: being and nothing are the same. (The reference is to the opening movements of Hegel's *Wissenschaft der Logik*, where pure being passes over into pure nothing.) Philosophy as science of being, then — the science of nothing? Heidegger insists on candor at the outset: under "being" I can, initially, think nothing. And yet just as firmly stands the contrary: we think being constantly. As often as we say, in actual utterance or tacitly — and we do so countless times each day — that this or that is so-and-so, that the other is not so, that something was, will be, in every use of a verb we have already thought being and have always somehow understood it. We immediately understand: today is Saturday, the sun has risen. We understand the "is" we use in speech, and we *do not grasp* it (begreifen). The sense of this "is" remains closed to us. This understanding of the "is" — and thereby of being as such — is so much taken for granted that an unchallenged dogma has been able to establish itself in philosophy: that being is the simplest and most self-evident concept; that it is neither capable of nor in need of further determination. One appeals to common sense. But whenever common sense is made the final court of appeal in philosophy, philosophy must become suspicious. Heidegger then cites Hegel from "On the Essence of Philosophical Criticism in General": philosophy is by its nature esoteric, made neither for the rabble nor capable of being prepared for the rabble; it is philosophy only insofar as it is opposed to understanding and even more so to common sense, by which one means the local and temporal limitedness of a generation of humans; in relation to common sense the world of philosophy is *an und für sich* a *verkehrte Welt* — an inverted world. The claims and standards of common sense may claim no validity and represent no court of appeal regarding what philosophy is and is not. This invocation of Hegel's "inverted world" is a recurring motif in Heidegger; it will return later in §5 in connection with the *Apriori* and the proper order of being and beings.

The final paragraph of §3 turns the dogma of being's self-evidence upside down. *What if* being were the most entangled and obscure concept? What if bringing being to its concept were the most pressing and ever newly to-be-undertaken task of philosophy? Today, when one philosophizes so barbarously and St. Vitus-dance-like (veitstänzerisch) as perhaps in no period of Western intellectual history, and today, when one nonetheless cries out from every alley a resurrection of metaphysics, one has completely forgotten what Aristotle says in one of the most important investigations of the *Metaphysics*: "What from of old and now and always is sought and that on which the question always fails — namely, what is being? — this is the problem: what is *ousia*?" (Met. Z1, 1028b2 ff.). The Aristotelian text is the very opening of *Metaphysics* Z, which Heidegger considered the central document of ancient ontology. If philosophy is the science of being, the result is: the beginning, end, and ground question of philosophy is — What does *being* mean? *From where* (Von wo aus) is such a thing as being to be understood at all? How is understanding of being in general possible? The triplicity of the question — meaning, origin-horizon, possibility-condition — articulates exactly the program of *Sein und Zeit* and of the lecture course before us. The phrase "From where is being to be understood?" already anticipates the answer that the second part of the course will defend: *temporality is the horizon out of which being is understood*.

## **§ 4\. The Four Theses about Being and the Basic Problems of Phenomenology**

The opening paragraph of §4 marks the methodological pivot from the introduction's exposition of the concept of philosophy to the substantive program of Part One. Before we can roll out the fundamental questions, Heidegger says, we must first familiarize ourselves with discussions about being as such. To this end, in the first part of the lecture course we will treat as concrete phenomenological individual problems several characteristic theses about being that have been pronounced in the course of the history of Western philosophy since antiquity. The interest is not in the historical connections of the philosophical investigations within which these theses appear, but in their *specific factual content* (sachlicher Gehalt). This content is to be critically discussed in such a way that we are led over from it to the basic problems of the science of being named above. The discussion of these theses should at the same time familiarize us with the *phenomenological manner of treating problems* that have to do with being. The pedagogical strategy is sophisticated: by working through historical positions with a particular technique, the student learns both the content of ontological problems and the method of phenomenological analysis.

Heidegger then names the four theses that will structure Part One. *First*: Kant's thesis — "being is not a real predicate" (the formula from the refutation of the ontological argument). *Second*: the thesis of medieval ontology (Scholasticism) going back to Aristotle — to the being-constitution of a being belong *Was-sein* (essentia) and *Vorhandensein* (existentia), that is, "what-being" and "being-on-hand." *Third*: the thesis of modern ontology — the fundamental modes of being are the being of nature (res extensa) and the being of spirit (res cogitans). *Fourth*: the thesis of logic in the widest sense — every being can be addressed, regardless of its respective mode of being, by means of the "is"; the being of the *copula*. These four theses are not chosen by historical accident; they correspond, as Heidegger will indicate, to the four basic problems of phenomenology to be treated in Part Two.

The next paragraph anticipates the objection that the four theses might appear arbitrarily assembled (willkürlich aufgerafft). On closer view, Heidegger insists, they are intimately interconnected. Consideration of what is named in these theses leads to the insight that these theses cannot adequately be raised — even merely as problems — so long as the fundamental question of all science of being is not posed and answered: the question of the meaning of being as such. This question is to be treated in the second part of the lecture course. The discussion of the fundamental question of the meaning of being as such and of the problems arising from it constitutes the total content of the basic problems of phenomenology in their systematic ordering and grounding. The circle of these problems can, for the moment, only be roughly characterized.

The next paragraph poses the methodological question: on what path can one advance to the meaning of being as such? Is the question of the meaning of being and the task of clarifying this concept not a pseudo-question (Scheinfrage) if one — as is customary — dogmatically holds the opinion that being is the most general and simplest concept? Whence is it to be determined, and into what is it to be resolved? The reference is to the long tradition, going back to Aristotle and made canonical in Suárez and the early modern logic textbooks, of treating being as the *generalissimum* not susceptible of definition because no higher genus can be found under which it could be subsumed.

The answer is announced: something like being is given to us in *Seinsverständnis*, in the understanding of being, which underlies every comportment toward beings. Comportments toward beings belong, in turn, to a determinate being that we ourselves are — *menschliches Dasein*. To Dasein belongs the understanding of being that first makes possible every comportment toward beings. The understanding of being itself has the mode of being of human Dasein. The more originarily and adequately we determine this being with respect to its being-structure (i.e., ontologically), the more securely we are placed in the position of grasping in its structure the understanding of being that belongs to Dasein, and the more unambiguously can the question be posed: *what is it that makes this understanding of being in general possible?* From where, that is, out of what pre-given horizon, do we understand something like being? Here is the move that distinguishes Heidegger's approach absolutely from any traditional ontology: the meaning of being is to be sought through the analysis of that being whose mode of being it is to understand being. The ontological question and the analytic of Dasein are inseparable.

The analysis of *Seinsverständnis* with respect to its specific understanding and to what is understood in it, that is, to its understandability, presupposes an analytic of Dasein arranged toward this end. This analytic has the task of bringing out the basic constitution of human Dasein and characterizing the meaning of the being of Dasein. As the originary constitution of the being of Dasein, the ontological analytic of Dasein discloses *Zeitlichkeit* — temporality. The interpretation of temporality leads to a more radical understanding and conceiving of time than was possible in previous philosophy. The familiar and traditionally treated philosophical concept of time is only an offshoot (Ableger) of temporality as the originary meaning of Dasein. If temporality constitutes the being-sense of human Dasein, but to the being-constitution of Dasein belongs the understanding of being, then this understanding of being must also become possible only on the ground of temporality. From this grows the prospect of a possible verification of the thesis: *the horizon out of which something like being in general becomes intelligible is time*. We interpret being out of time (tempus). The interpretation is *temporal*. The basic problematic of ontology as the determination of the meaning of being out of time is that of *Temporalität*. Heidegger here distinguishes terminologically between *Zeitlichkeit* (the temporality of Dasein) and *Temporalität* (the temporality of being itself, the temporal horizon out of which being is understood) — a distinction that will become crucial in Part Three of the course (which corresponds to the unwritten Division Three of Part One of *Sein und Zeit*).

The next paragraph introduces the *ontological difference*. We said: ontology is the science of being. But being is always being of a being. Being is essentially distinguished from beings. How is this difference between being and beings to be grasped? How is its possibility to be grounded? If being is itself not a being, how does it then itself belong to beings — given that beings, and only beings, *are*? What does it say: being belongs to beings? The right answer to these questions is the basic presupposition for actually carrying out the problems of ontology as the science of being. We must be able to unambiguously enact the distinction between being and beings in order to make something like being the theme of investigation. This distinction is not arbitrary but is the one through which alone the theme of ontology and thereby of philosophy itself is first won. It is a distinction that first *constitutes* ontology. We call it *die ontologische Differenz* — the ontological difference, the distinction between being and beings.

Only in the carrying-out of this distinguishing — Greek *krinein*, not of one being from another being but of being from beings — do we enter the field of philosophical problematics. Only through this critical comportment do we remain within the field of philosophy. Therefore ontology, or philosophy in general, in distinction from the sciences of beings, is the *critical science* or also the *science of the inverted world*. With this distinguishing of being from beings, and with the thematic lifting-out of being, we fundamentally step out of the region of beings. We *übersteigen*, we transcend it. We may therefore also call the science of being as critical science the *transcendental* science. In so doing we do not simply take over the concept of the transcendental from Kant, but we do take over its originary sense and its proper tendency, which is perhaps still hidden from Kant himself. We transcend beings to arrive at being. In this overstepping we do not climb up further to a being that would lie behind familiar beings as some sort of *Hinterwelt* — a "world-behind." The transcendental science of being has nothing to do with vulgar metaphysics, which treats of some being behind familiar beings; rather, the scientific concept of metaphysics is identical with the concept of philosophy as such: critical-transcendental science of being, i.e., ontology. One sees easily, Heidegger continues, that the ontological difference is to be clarified and unambiguously enacted for ontological investigation only if the meaning of being as such has been explicitly brought to light, that is, only if it is shown how temporality makes possible the distinguishability of being and beings. Only on the ground of this consideration is Kant's thesis — being is not a real predicate — first brought to its originary sense and adequately grounded. With this we have the *first* basic problem: the ontological difference, the problem of the distinction between being and beings, which corresponds to and grounds Kant's thesis.

The next paragraph introduces the *second* basic problem. Every being is something — that is, it has its What and has, as this, a determinate possible *manner of being* (Art zu sein). In the first part of the lecture course we will show, in the discussion of the second thesis, that ancient and medieval ontology dogmatically and as a matter of course articulated this proposition — that to every being belong its What and a manner of being, *essentia* and *existentia*. For us the question arises: can it be grounded, out of the sense of being itself, that is, *temporal*, why every being *must* and *can* have a What, a *ti*, and a possible manner of being? Do these determinations — what-being and manner-of-being, taken sufficiently broadly — belong to being itself? *Is* being, by its essence, articulated through these determinations? Here we stand before the problem of the *Grundartikulation des Seins* — the basic articulation of being — that is, the question of the necessary belonging-together of what-being and manner-of-being and of the belonging of both in their unity to the idea of being as such. The second basic problem corresponds to and grounds the medieval-Aristotelian thesis of essentia and existentia.

The next paragraph introduces the *third* basic problem. Every being has a manner-of-being. The question is whether this manner-of-being has the same character in all beings — as ancient ontology supposed, and which fundamentally even the subsequent period up to the present has had to maintain — or whether individual modes of being are distinguished among themselves. What are the basic modes of being? Is there a manifold? How is the multiplicity of modes of being possible and intelligible from the meaning of being as such? How can one nevertheless speak of a unified concept of being in general despite the multiplicity of modes of being? These questions can be combined into the problem of the *possible modifications* of being and the unity of its multiplicity. This third problem corresponds to and grounds the modern thesis of res extensa and res cogitans, which posits two different basic modes of being (the being of nature and the being of spirit) without grounding the unity that allows them to be addressed as modes of *being*.

The next paragraph introduces the *fourth* basic problem. Every being to which we comport ourselves can — regardless of its specific mode of being — be addressed and discussed (angesprochen und besprochen) with the "*es ist*" so-and-so. The being of a being is encountered for us in the understanding of being. Understanding is what first discloses or, as we say, *erschließt* something like being. Being "is given" (es gibt) only in the specific *Erschlossenheit* (disclosedness) that characterizes understanding of being. The disclosedness of something, however, we call *Wahrheit* — truth. This is the proper concept of truth as it dawns already in antiquity. Being "is given" only when disclosedness, that is, when truth, *is*. But truth *is* only when a being exists that opens up, that discloses, in such a way that to the being-mode of this being belongs the *erschließen* itself. Such a being is what we ourselves are. *Dasein exists itself in truth*. To Dasein essentially belongs a disclosed world and, together with this, the disclosedness of itself. Dasein is, according to the essence of its existence, "in" truth, and only because it is so does it have the possibility of being "in" untruth. Being is given only when truth, that is, when Dasein, exists. And *only therefore* is the addressability of beings not only possible but, within certain limits, in each case — presupposing that Dasein exists — necessary. These problems of the connection between being and truth we summarize in the problem of the *Wahrheitscharakter des Seins* — the truth-character of being (*veritas transcendentalis*). The fourth problem corresponds to and grounds the logical thesis about the "is" of the copula: the "is" presupposes disclosedness, and disclosedness presupposes the existence of Dasein as the being whose being it is to disclose.

The closing paragraph of §4 sums up: we have thus characterized four groups of problems that make up the content of the second part of the lecture course — the problem of the ontological difference, the problem of the basic articulation of being, the problem of the possible modifications of being into its modes of being, and the problem of the truth-character of being. To these four basic problems correspond the four theses treated preparatorily in the first part. More precisely, it will be shown retrospectively from the discussion of the basic problems in the second part that the problems with which we provisionally occupy ourselves in the first part along the guideline of the named theses are not arbitrary but grow out of the inner systematics of the problem of being as such. The whole architecture of the lecture course — first part on theses, second part on problems — is justified: the theses are not arbitrary points of historical departure but exhibit the inner articulation of the problem of being itself.

## **§ 5\. The Methodical Character of Ontology. The Three Basic Pieces of the Phenomenological Method**

The opening paragraph announces the transition to the third part. The concrete carrying-through of the ontological investigations in the first and second parts simultaneously opens for us a view of the manner in which these phenomenological investigations proceed. This drives toward the question of the methodical character of ontology. Thus we arrive at the third part of the lecture course: the scientific method of ontology and the idea of phenomenology. The method of ontology, i.e., of philosophy as such, is distinguished by the fact that it has nothing in common with the method of any other science — for all other sciences, as positive sciences of beings, treat of beings, not of being. On the other hand, the analysis of the truth-character of being shows that being itself in a certain sense is *grounded* in a being, namely in Dasein. Being is given only when understanding of being, that is, when Dasein, exists. This being thus claims a distinguished priority in the problematics of ontology. This priority is announced in all discussions of the basic ontological problems, above all in the fundamental question of the meaning of being as such. The working-out and answering of this question demands a general *analytic of Dasein*. Ontology has as its fundamental discipline the analytic of Dasein. In this lies at the same time: ontology cannot ground itself purely ontologically. Its own enabling is referred back to a being, that is, to something ontic: Dasein. Ontology has an *ontic foundation* (ontisches Fundament). This is one of Heidegger's most provocative theses, often called the "ontic foundation of ontology" — the claim that the science of being cannot found itself but rests on the prior analysis of a particular being, that being which we ourselves are. Even the previous history of philosophy has always glimpsed this and expressed it, for example, in Aristotle's declaration that the first science, the science of being, is theology. (Heidegger reads Aristotle's "first philosophy is theology" not as the claim that ontology has *theos* as its highest object but as the recognition that ontology is grounded in the most excellent being.) The possibilities and fates of philosophy, as a work of the freedom of human Dasein, are bound to Dasein's existence — that is, to its temporality and historicality — and indeed in a more originary sense than is any other science. So the first task within the clarification of the scientific character of ontology is the demonstration of its ontic foundation and the characterization of this foundedness.

The second task within the clarification of ontology's method is the characterization of the mode of cognition that is carried out in ontology as the science of being. This means working out the methodical structures of the *ontologisch-transzendentales Unterscheiden* — the ontological-transcendental distinguishing. Already early in antiquity it was seen that being and its determinations underlie beings in a certain way, that they precede beings, that they are a *proteron*, an earlier. The terminological designation for this character of the priority of being before beings is the expression *a priori*, *Apriorität*, the "earlier." Being is, as a priori, earlier than beings. To this day the sense of this a priori — the sense of the "earlier" and its possibility — has not been clarified. It has not even been asked why the determinations of being and being itself must have this character of the "earlier" and how such an "earlier" is possible. The "earlier" is a time-determination, but one which does not lie in the temporal order of the time we measure with the clock — but rather an "earlier" that belongs to the *verkehrte Welt*, the inverted world. Therefore this "earlier" that characterizes being is grasped by vulgar understanding as the *later*. The interpretation of being out of temporality alone can make intelligible why and how being is bound up with this character of the "earlier," the apriority. The a priori character of being and of all being-structures accordingly demands a determinate manner of access and grasping of being: *a priori cognition*. This passage is one of the early Heidegger's most important reinterpretations of the a priori: it is not the *logical* priority of categories but the *temporal* priority of being-determinations, which can be understood only out of *Temporalität*.

The next paragraph introduces phenomenology as the method appropriate to a priori cognition. The basic pieces that belong to a priori cognition make up what we call phenomenology. Phenomenology is the title for the method of ontology, i.e., of scientific philosophy. Phenomenology, if it understands itself correctly, is the concept of a *method*. It is therefore excluded from the outset that phenomenology should pronounce any determinate content-theses about beings or represent a so-called *Standpunkt* — standpoint. This explicitly counters the popular image of phenomenology as a philosophical school with definite doctrines (e.g., the reality of essences, the priority of consciousness, etc.).

Heidegger then dispatches an aside about contemporary characterizations of phenomenology and gives one example: it has been said that his philosophical work is *catholic phenomenology* — presumably because he is convinced that thinkers like Thomas Aquinas or Duns Scotus understood something of philosophy, perhaps more than the moderns. The concept of a catholic phenomenology, Heidegger replies, is even more nonsensical than the concept of a *protestant mathematics*. Philosophy as the science of being is fundamentally different in its method from every other science. The methodical difference, for instance, between mathematics and classical philology is not as great as the difference between mathematics and philosophy, or philology and philosophy. The magnitude of the difference between positive sciences (to which mathematics and philology belong) and philosophy cannot in general be quantitatively estimated. In ontology, being is to be grasped and comprehended on the path of the phenomenological method, where we note that phenomenology has become alive today but that what it seeks and wants has been alive from the beginning in Western philosophy. The polemic here is double-edged: phenomenology is not the property of any confessional or philosophical sect, and what it pursues is co-extensive with the deepest aspiration of the philosophical tradition itself.

The next paragraph begins to develop the positive characterization of phenomenology. Being is to be grasped and made thematic. Being is in each case being of a being, and is therefore initially accessible only by way of departure from a being. The grasping phenomenological gaze must indeed direct itself toward a being, but in such a way that thereby the *being* of this being comes to relief and to possible thematization. The grasping of being, i.e., the ontological investigation, in each case goes initially and necessarily out to a being, but is then in a determinate way *led back* from this being to its being. We name this basic piece of the phenomenological method — in the sense of the leading-back of the investigating gaze from the naively grasped being to being — the *phenomenological reduction*. In so doing we link up — in wording, but not in the matter — with a central term of Husserl's phenomenology. For Husserl, the phenomenological reduction, which he first worked out explicitly in *Ideen zu einer reinen Phänomenologie und phänomenologischen Philosophie* (1913), is the method of leading back the phenomenological gaze from the natural attitude of the human being living-into the world of things and persons to the *transcendental life of consciousness* and its noetic-noematic lived-experiences (Erlebnisse), in which objects constitute themselves as correlates of consciousness. For us — Heidegger emphasizes the distinction sharply — the phenomenological reduction means the leading-back of the phenomenological gaze from whatever determinate grasping of a being to the *understanding of the being* (the projection upon the manner of the being's unconcealedness) of this being. The distinction is decisive: Husserl's reduction leads from world to consciousness, Heidegger's reduction leads from beings to being. The two are opposed in their direction. Like every scientific method, the phenomenological method also grows and changes on the basis of the advancement to the matters that it has just accomplished by its means. *Scientific method is never a technique.* As soon as it becomes that, it has fallen away from its own essence. The remark guards against the routinization of phenomenology into a "method" applicable like a tool.

The next paragraph introduces the second basic piece of phenomenological method. The phenomenological reduction as the leading-back of the gaze from beings to being is not, however, the only — nor even the central — basic piece of the phenomenological method. For this leading-back of the gaze from beings to being needs at the same time the positive *bringing-itself-toward* (positives Sichhinbringen) of being itself. Mere turning-away is only a negative methodical comportment, which not only needs supplementation by something positive but explicitly needs the leading-toward being, the *Leitung*, the guiding. Being is not accessible in the way beings are; we do not simply find it before us, but it must — as will have to be shown — in each case be brought into view in a *free projection*. This projecting of the pre-given being onto its being and its structures we designate as the *phenomenological construction* (phänomenologische Konstruktion). The pair reduction/construction articulates the negative and positive sides of philosophy's access to being: turning away from beings as merely given, and projectively bringing being itself into view.

The third basic piece is introduced in the following paragraph. With construction the method of phenomenology is still not exhausted. Every projecting of being, we heard, is carried out in the reductive going-back from beings. The investigation of being takes its departure in each case from beings. This departure is obviously determined in each case by the factical experience of beings and by the circle of possibilities of experience that in each case belong to a factical Dasein, that is, to the historical situation of a philosophical research. Not at every time and for everyone are all beings and determinate regions of them equally accessible — and even when a being is accessible within the circle of experience, the question still remains whether it has already been adequately understood in its specific being-mode in naive and vulgar experience. *Because Dasein, according to its own existence, is historical*, the possibilities of access and the manners of interpretation of beings themselves are different and variable in different historical situations. A look at the history of philosophy shows that diverse regions of beings were very soon discovered — nature, space, soul — but that they nevertheless could not be comprehended in their specific being. Already in antiquity an average concept of being emerged that was used for the interpretation of all beings of the various being-regions and their modes of being, without the specific being itself being made explicitly into a problem in its structure and being delimited. Thus Plato saw very well that the soul and its *logos* is a different being from sensible beings. But he was not able to delimit the specific being-mode of this being against the being-mode of any other being or of a non-being; for him as for Aristotle and the following period up to Hegel — and even more for the post-Hegelians — all ontological investigations move within an average concept of being as such. Even the ontological investigation we are now carrying out is determined by its historical situation and, with this, by certain possibilities of access to beings and by the *tradition* (Überlieferung) of previous philosophy. The stock of philosophical fundamental concepts from the philosophical tradition is still so effective today that this effect of the tradition can scarcely be overestimated. Hence all philosophical discussion — even the most radical, newly beginning kind — is shot through with inherited concepts and thereby with inherited horizons and points of view, of which it is not without further ado established that they have arisen originally and genuinely from the being-region and the being-constitution they claim to grasp. Hence to the conceptual interpretation of being and its structures — i.e., to the reductive construction of being — there *necessarily belongs* a *Destruktion*, that is, a *critical dismantling* (kritischer Abbau) of the inherited and initially necessarily-to-be-used concepts back to the sources from which they have been drawn. Only through Destruction can ontology phenomenologically assure itself fully of the genuineness of its concepts. The Destruktion is therefore not a *Negation* of the tradition (Heidegger emphasizes this in the next paragraph) but the *positive appropriation* of the tradition by retrieving its concepts at their source.

These three basic pieces of the phenomenological method — reduction, construction, destruction — belong together in content and must be grounded in their belonging-together. Construction of philosophy is necessarily *destruction*, that is, a dismantling-of-the-handed-down accomplished in historical going-back to the tradition. This is not negation and condemnation of the tradition to nullity but, conversely, precisely positive appropriation of it. *Because destruction belongs to construction*, philosophical cognition is in its essence at once, in a determinate sense, *historical cognition*. To the concept of philosophy as science, to the concept of phenomenological research, belongs — as one says — "the history of philosophy." The history of philosophy is not an arbitrary appendix in the philosophical curriculum, providing an opportunity to acquire some convenient and easy theme for the state examination, or to look around once at how it used to be; rather, *historical-philosophical cognition is in itself one*, where the specific manner of historical knowing in philosophy is, in accord with its object, distinguished from every other scientific historical cognition. This is one of the most characteristic theses of the early Heidegger: philosophy is essentially history of philosophy, not because of any cultural piety but because the very tradition of philosophical concepts is the medium in which the contemporary ontological investigation must work, and only by Destruction of this tradition can the originary phenomena be retrieved.

The closing paragraphs gather the consequences. The method of ontology so characterized makes possible a characterization of the idea of phenomenology as the scientific procedure of philosophy. With this we acquire the possibility of delimiting the concept of philosophy more concretely. Thus the consideration of the third part leads back to the beginning of the lecture course. The structure is self-closing: the course begins with the question of the concept of philosophy, develops the analysis of being through the four theses and the four basic problems, and ends with the methodical reflection that, in clarifying the manner in which all this was done, returns to the initial question. The third part is thus the *meta-philosophical* completion of the course, in which the procedure becomes transparent to itself.

## **§ 6\. The Articulation of the Lecture Course**

The closing section of the introduction provides the explicit table of contents of the lecture course. The course of thought of the lecture is articulated into three parts. *First Part*: phenomenological-critical discussion of certain traditional theses about being. *Second Part*: the fundamental-ontological question of the meaning of being as such; the basic structures and basic modes of being. *Third Part*: the scientific method of ontology and the idea of phenomenology.

The first part falls into four chapters, corresponding to the four theses: (1) Kant's thesis — being is not a real predicate; (2) the thesis of medieval ontology going back to Aristotle — to the being of a being belong what-being (essentia) and being-on-hand (existentia); (3) the thesis of modern ontology — the basic modes of being are the being of nature (res extensa) and the being of spirit (res cogitans); (4) the thesis of logic — every being can be addressed and discussed, regardless of its respective mode of being, by means of the "is"; the being of the copula.

The second part articulates correspondingly into four problems: (1) the problem of the ontological difference (the distinction between being and beings); (2) the problem of the basic articulation of being (essentia, existentia); (3) the problem of the possible modifications of being and the unity of its multiplicity; (4) the truth-character of being.

The third part also articulates into four chapters: (1) the ontic foundation of ontology and the analytic of Dasein as fundamental ontology; (2) the apriority of being and the possibility and structure of a priori cognition; (3) the basic pieces of the phenomenological method — reduction, construction, destruction; (4) phenomenological ontology and the concept of philosophy. With this the entire architectonics of the course is laid out: the parts in each case have four divisions corresponding to one another, and the third part culminates in a fourth chapter that returns to the concept of philosophy with which the introduction began. The symmetry is not merely decorative; it expresses Heidegger's conviction that the various aspects of the problem of being — the historical theses, the systematic problems, the methodical considerations — articulate in a single fourfold pattern that is itself grounded in the structure of being's relation to time.

# **Part One: Phenomenological-Critical Discussion of Certain Traditional Theses about Being**

# **Chapter One: Kant's Thesis: Being Is Not a Real Predicate**

## **§ 7\. The Content of the Kantian Thesis**

The opening paragraph identifies Kant's two principal treatments of the thesis. The first is in the small writing *Der einzig mögliche Beweisgrund zu einer Demonstration des Daseins Gottes* (1763), "The Only Possible Argument in Support of a Demonstration of the Existence of God." This writing belongs to the so-called *vorkritische Zeit* — the pre-critical period of Kant — that is, to the time before the *Kritik der reinen Vernunft* of 1781\. It falls into three divisions. Our thesis is treated in the first division, which discusses the fundamental questions and articulates itself into four considerations: first, "On Existence in General"; second, "On Inner Possibility, insofar as it Presupposes an Existence"; third, "On Absolutely Necessary Existence"; fourth, "Ground for a Demonstration of the Existence of God." The structure of the *Beweisgrund* already reveals what is at stake: Kant's thesis about being is initially formulated in the context of his critique of the *ontological argument* for God's existence and his attempt to provide an alternative argument, the so-called "only possible" one (based on the impossibility of all possibility being annihilated, which would require an absolutely necessary ground of all possibility).

The next paragraph continues with the *Critique of Pure Reason*. Kant then takes up the thesis in the first (1781) and second (1787) editions of the *Critique*, specifically in the *transcendental logic*. Heidegger announces that henceforth he will cite according to the second edition (B). The transcendental logic — or, as we may also say, the ontology of nature — falls into two divisions: the transcendental analytic and the transcendental dialectic. Within the dialectic, in the second book, third main piece, fourth section (B 620 ff.), Kant goes again into the thesis he had discussed in the *Beweisgrund*. The section is titled "On the Impossibility of an Ontological Proof of the Existence of God." Heidegger thus identifies the second locus: the famous refutation of the ontological argument in the *Critique*. The equivalence of "transcendental logic" with "ontology of nature" is a Heideggerian gloss: for Kant, the categories and principles of pure understanding are precisely the a priori determinations of what it is to be an object of nature, and so transcendental logic is *ipso facto* a regional ontology of natural beings.

The next paragraph notes that in both places — in the *Beweisgrund* and in the *Critique* — the thesis is treated in the same sense. For the purpose of presentation, which Heidegger says he will deliberately keep detailed, he will refer to both writings. He cites these as "Beweisgrund" (in the Cassirer edition) and "Kritik." Before laying out the content of the Kantian thesis, he will briefly characterize the substantive context within which it is discussed in both places. The methodical posture is one of *detailed* (ausführlich) phenomenological-historical reading rather than merely registering Kant's claim.

Before this contextual sketch, however, a general terminological remark is required. Kant speaks, as the title of the *Beweisgrund* shows, of the *proof of the existence (Dasein) of God*. He likewise speaks of the *Dasein* of things outside us, of the *Dasein* of nature. This concept of *Dasein* in Kant corresponds to the Scholastic term *existentia*. Kant therefore often uses, instead of *Dasein*, the expressions *Existenz* and *Wirklichkeit*. Our terminological usage is, however, a different one — one that, as will be shown, is factually grounded. What Kant calls *Dasein* or *Existenz* and what the Scholastics call *existentia*, we designate terminologically with the expression *Vorhandensein* or *Vorhandenheit* (being-on-hand, on-handness). This is the title for the *mode of being of natural things* in the broadest sense. The choice of this expression itself must justify itself in the course of the lecture from the specific sense of this mode of being, which demands the expression *Vorhandenes*, *Vorhandenheit*. Husserl in his terminology follows Kant and uses the concept of *Dasein* in the sense of *Vorhandensein*. The word *Dasein*, however, designates *for us* — Heidegger emphasizes — not, as for Kant, the mode of being of natural things; it does not designate any mode of being at all but rather a *determinate being that we ourselves are*, the human Dasein. We are in each case a Dasein. This being, Dasein, has, like every being, a specific mode of being. The mode of being of Dasein we determine terminologically as *Existenz* — where it must be noted that *Existenz*, or the speech "Dasein exists," is not the only determination of the mode of being of ourselves; we will come to know a threefold determination, which is, however, in a specific sense rooted in *Existenz*. This terminological clarification is decisive for the entire course: from now on, *Vorhandensein* names the mode of being of objects (the Scholastic *existentia*, Kant's *Dasein*), while *Existenz* names the mode of being of Dasein — and the eventual contrast between these two modes of being will be the linchpin of Heidegger's whole engagement with Kant's thesis. The remark also explains why Heidegger cannot just take over Kant's vocabulary as it stands. Can this presupposition — that philosophy does not relate positively to beings as the sciences do — be justified? With what then is philosophy to be concerned, if not with beings, with what is, with beings as a whole? What is *not* is the *nothing*. Is philosophy supposed, as absolute science, to have the nothing as its theme? Apart from nature, history, God, space, number — what can there be? All of these we say *are*, even if in different senses. We call them *beings*. Referred to them, whether theoretically or practically, we comport ourselves toward beings. Outside these beings there is nothing. Perhaps there is no other being than those enumerated — but perhaps there is yet something which, although it *is not*, nonetheless *es gibt* — "there is" — in a sense still to be determined. More: ultimately there must be something which *must* be (es geben muß), so that we can make beings as beings accessible to ourselves and comport ourselves toward them — something which is not itself a being but which must be there, must "be given" (geben), so that we can in general experience and understand anything like beings. Beings we can grasp as such, as beings, only if we understand something like *being*. If we did not understand, however roughly and unconceptually, what *Wirklichkeit* (actuality) says, then the actual would remain hidden from us. If we did not understand what *Realität* (reality) means, then the real would remain inaccessible. If we did not understand what life and aliveness say, then we would not be able to comport ourselves toward the living. If we did not understand what *Existenz* and existentiality say, then we ourselves could not exist as Dasein. If we did not understand what *Bestand* and constancy mean, then existing geometrical relations or number-relations would remain closed to us. We must understand actuality, reality, aliveness, existentiality, constancy in order to comport ourselves positively to determinate actual, real, living, existing, constant beings. We must understand being so that we can be delivered over to a being-world (an eine seiende Welt ausgeliefert sein) in order to exist in it and to be able to be our own being-Dasein itself. We must be able to understand actuality before any experience of the actual. This understanding of actuality or being in the widest sense, over against the experience of beings, is *früher* — earlier — than the latter in a determinate sense. The pre-given understanding of being prior to all factical experience of beings does not mean that we must have an explicit concept of being beforehand in order to experience beings theoretically or practically. We must understand being — being, which itself may no longer be called a being, being, which does not occur among other beings as a being, but which nevertheless *must be given* (geben muß) and which *is also given* (auch gibt) in the understanding of being, in *Seinsverständnis*. With this paragraph the entire issue of the lecture course is positioned: there is something — being — which is not a being, yet must be "there," must be "given," for any comportment to beings to be possible at all. This pre-conceptual *Seinsverständnis* is the proper theme of philosophy, and the next section will identify it explicitly.

The next paragraph turns to the substantive context in which Kant's thesis appears. This context strikes one immediately from the title of the first writing (the *Beweisgrund*) and likewise from the heading of the relevant section of the *Critique*. The issue is the proof of the *Dasein* — existence, *Wirklichkeit*, and (in Heidegger's terminology) *Vorhandenheit* — of God. We stand before the striking fact that Kant discusses the *most general concept of being as such* precisely where he treats of the knowability of a quite *determinate, distinguished* being, namely God. But for someone who knows the history of philosophy (ontology), the fact is not strange at all — it rather makes clear how directly Kant stands in the great tradition of ancient and Scholastic ontology. God is the *summum ens*, the *ens perfectissimum*, the most perfect being. What is most perfect is evidently most suitable as the *exemplary* being from which the idea of being can be read off. God is not only the ontological paradigm-case for the being of a being but at the same time the *Urgrund* — original ground — of all beings. The being of non-divine, created beings must be understood out of the being of the highest being. This is why it is no accident that the science of being, in a distinguished sense, is oriented toward the being qua God. It goes so far that already Aristotle called the *prōtē philosophia*, the first philosophy, *theologia*. Heidegger notes — significantly — that Aristotle's concept of theology has nothing to do with the present-day concept of Christian theology as a positive science; the two share only the word. The Aristotelian *theologikē* is, in Heidegger's reading, the science of the highest being qua highest, which functions as a methodological lens for the question of being as such. This orientation of ontology toward the idea of God was of decisive importance for the subsequent history of ontology and its fate. Heidegger says he will not here treat the rightness of this orientation; it is enough that the fact of Kant's discussing being in the context of the possibility of knowledge of God has nothing surprising about it. More precisely, what is at stake for Kant is the problem of the possibility of what he first named the *ontological* proof of God's existence. This reveals the remarkable fact — which we encounter again and again in philosophy before Kant and even after Kant, most extremely in Hegel — that the problem of being as such is most intimately bound up with the problem of God, with the determination of God's essence and the proof of God's existence. The reasons for this remarkable connection — which is by no means self-evident — Heidegger declines to discuss here, since it would require examining the foundations of ancient philosophy and metaphysics. The fact is established also in Kant, and it is, at first quite externally, the proof that Kant's questioning still moves entirely in the paths of traditional metaphysics. The point is one of Heidegger's signature insights: Kant, despite his critical project, remains structurally within the *onto-theological* constitution of metaphysics.

The next paragraph specifies that Kant treats the possibility of the ontological proof — the proof that attempts to draw God's existence from the concept of God. The philosophical science that, on Kant's view, tries dogmatically and purely from concepts to make something out about beings is *ontology* — or, traditionally, *metaphysics*. Therefore Kant calls this proof from the concept of God the *ontological* proof, where "ontological" means as much as dogmatic, metaphysical. Kant himself does not deny the possibility of metaphysics; he seeks rather a scientific metaphysics, a scientific ontology, whose idea he determines as the *system of transcendental philosophy*. The point is important: Kant's anti-ontologism is itself in the service of a properly grounded ontology.

The next paragraph sketches the history of the ontological proof. It is old; one usually leads it back to Anselm of Canterbury (1033–1109). Anselm presented his proof in the small treatise *Proslogium seu alloquium de Dei existentia*. In chapter three the actual core of the proof is given. The proof is often called in the literature the "Scholastic proof of God," but this term is unsuited, because medieval Scholasticism has in many ways disputed the cogency and validity of this proof. Not Kant alone but above all Thomas Aquinas disputed the cogency of this proof, whereas Bonaventure and Duns Scotus allowed it. But the Kantian refutation of the possibility of the ontological proof is far more radical and fundamental than the one given by Thomas.

The next paragraph analyzes the structure of the ontological proof. Its characteristic feature lies in inferring God's existence from the concept of God. Belonging to the concept, the idea, of God is the determination that he is the most perfect being, the *ens perfectissimum*. The most perfect being is that to which no possible positive determination can be lacking and to which every positive determination accrues in an infinitely perfect manner. The most perfect being, as which we conceptually think God, cannot fail to have any positive determination. Every lack is, in accordance with the concept of this being, excluded from it. To the perfection of the most perfect being belongs evidently — perhaps above all else — that it *is*, its *existence*. God is not what he essentially is as the most perfect *without* existing. From the concept of God it follows: God exists. The proof says: if God is thought according to his essence, that is, according to his concept, his existence must be co-thought. The natural question presses itself: does it follow from our having to think God as existing that he in fact exists? Heidegger declines to pursue the prehistory of the proof — back through Boethius and Dionysius the Areopagite into Neoplatonism — or its various reformulations and receptions; only Thomas's refutation he will briefly characterize, because it serves to set Kant's refutation in sharp relief.

Heidegger then discusses Thomas's four loci on the proof: the Commentary on the Sentences of Peter Lombard (Sent. I, dist. 3, qu. 1, art. 2 ad 4); the *Summa theologica* I, qu. 2, art. 1; the *Summa contra gentiles* I, cap. 10–11; and *De veritate*, qu. 10, art. 12\. The last is the most transparent treatment. There Thomas asks whether God's being is *per se notum* to the human mind, like the first principles of demonstration (the principle of identity, the principle of non-contradiction), which cannot be thought as non-existing. Thomas's question: do we know, by means of the concept of God — according to which God cannot not exist — of his existence? The criterion for something's being *per se notum* is that the *ratio subjecti* (where *ratio* means *essentia* or *natura* or, as Heidegger will show, *Realität*) is known to us. Then the subject cannot be thought without that which shows itself in the predicate. For such a cognition — which Kant will later call *analytic* — we must have insight into the *ratio subjecti*, that is, into the concept of the thing. For the proof of God's existence this means: the concept of God, his full essence, must be evident to us. But Thomas continues: since the *quidditas Dei* — what God is, his whatness, his essence — is not known to us, since with respect to us God is not transparent in his essence but requires the proof from experience of what he has created, the proof of God's existence from his concept lacks the sufficient grounding of its starting-point — the concept itself.

For Thomas, then, the ontological proof is impossible because we are not in a position to expound from ourselves the pure concept of God in order to derive from it the necessity of his existence. Heidegger emphasizes the contrast with Kant: Kant attacks the ontological proof at a different point, strikes at the actual nerve of the proof, and thereby first really lifts the proof off its hinges.

To see Kant's attack-point more clearly, Heidegger now schematizes the proof as a formal syllogism: *Major premise*: God is, according to his concept, the most perfect being. *Minor premise*: To the concept of the most perfect being belongs existence. *Conclusion*: Therefore God exists. Kant disputes neither that God is by his concept the most perfect being nor God's existence. On the form of the syllogism: Kant lets the major and the conclusion stand. If he nevertheless attacks the proof, the attack must hit the minor premise: to the concept of the most perfect being belongs existence — Dasein. Kant's thesis, whose phenomenological interpretation Heidegger makes his theme, is nothing other than the *fundamental denial of the possibility of the assertion fixed in the minor premise* of the ontological proof. Kant's thesis — being or *Dasein* is not a real predicate — does not merely claim that to the concept of the most perfect being existence cannot belong, or that we cannot recognize it as belonging (Thomas); rather, the thesis goes *further*. It says fundamentally: such a thing as *Dasein* and existence does not belong to the determinations of a concept at all. The radicality is general: not "we cannot determine that existence is contained in this particular concept," but "existence is not the *kind of thing* that can be contained in any concept."

Heidegger now turns to *how* Kant grounds his thesis. On this path it will of itself become clear how Kant elucidates the concept of existence and *Dasein* — in Heidegger's sense, of *Vorhandenheit*.

The First Division of the *Beweisgrund* falls into four considerations, of which the first treats "On Existence in General" (Vom Dasein überhaupt). It discusses three theses, or questions: first, "*Dasein* is no predicate or determination of any thing whatsoever"; second, "*Dasein* is the absolute position of a thing and is thereby distinguished from every predicate, which as such is always set merely relatively in respect to another thing"; third, "Can I really say that more lies in *Dasein* than in mere possibility?" The first proposition is the negative characterization of the essence of *Dasein*. The second proposition determines the ontological sense of *Dasein* positively: *Dasein* \= absolute position. The third — formulated as a question — takes a stance on a contemporary elucidation of the concept of *Dasein* given by Wolff and his school, according to which *Dasein* (existence) means *complementum possibilitatis*: the actuality of a thing, or its *Dasein*/existence, is the completion of its possibility.

The more compressed treatment of the same thesis is found in the *Critique of Pure Reason*. The first proposition from the *Beweisgrund* coincides with the proposition of the *Critique* that Heidegger chose as the formulation of the first thesis, and which reads in full: "Being is evidently no real predicate, that is, a concept of something that could be added to the concept of a thing." This is followed by the further proposition that positively determines the essence of being or *Dasein* and likewise coincides with the second proposition of the *Beweisgrund*: being "is merely the position of a thing or of certain determinations in themselves." Between being as such and *Dasein* Kant initially does not distinguish.

What does the negative thesis say at first? Being is not a real predicate — or, as Kant also says, is not a predicate of a thing at all. *Sein ist kein reales Prädikat* means: it is no predicate of a *res*. It is not a predicate at all, but merely position. Can we say that existence — *Dasein* — is not a predicate at all? "Predicate" means what is said in an assertion (judgment). Yet existence is asserted when I say "God exists," or in our terminology, "the mountain is on hand." Here being-on-hand and existing are being asserted. So it appears, and Kant himself emphasizes: "This proposition — *Dasein* is no predicate of any thing — appears strange and absurd, but it is unquestionably certain."

How does it stand with the question whether existence is asserted or not, is a predicate or not? How does Kant determine the essence of predication? The formal concept of assertion is, for Kant, the *combining of something with something*. The basic act of the understanding is, on his view, "I combine." This characterization of the essence of assertion is a purely formal determination — what Kant also calls a *formally logical* characterization, in which one abstracts from what it is that is being combined with another. Every predicate is in each case something determinate, something material. Formal logic thematizes only the *form* of predication as such — relation, combination, separation. In formal logic, the *Sachhaltigkeit* (factual content) of the predicate is, as we say, set aside, just as is the factual content of the subject. It is a logical characterization of assertion with respect to its emptiest form, i.e., formally as relation of something to something or combination of both.

If we orient ourselves to the formally logical concept of predication, of predicate, we cannot yet decide whether existence — *Dasein* — is a predicate. For existence has a determinate content, says something. Hence it must be asked more precisely: is existence or *Dasein* a *real* predicate, or — as Kant says abbreviating — a *determination*? Determination, says Kant, is a predicate that comes to the concept of the subject in addition and enlarges it. The determination, the predicate, must not already be contained in the concept. Determination is a real predicate that enlarges the thing, the *res*, in its content. This concept of the real and of reality must be held fast from the beginning if we want to understand Kant's thesis — *Dasein* is not a real predicate, that is, no determination of the factual content of a thing — correctly. The concept of reality and of the real does not have, in Kant, the meaning one mostly intends today when one speaks of the reality of the external world or of epistemological realism. *Realität* does not mean the same as *Wirklichkeit* or *Dasein* or *Existenz* or *Vorhandenheit*. It is *not identical* with *Dasein* — though Kant does use the concept *objektive Realität* identically with *Dasein*.

The Kantian meaning of the expression *Realität* is the one appropriate to the literal sense of the term. Kant once very aptly translates *Realität* with *Sachheit* — thing-ness, factual-determination. *Real* is what belongs to the *res*. When Kant speaks of the *omnitudo realitatis*, the totality of realities, he does not mean the totality of what is actually on hand, but, conversely, precisely the totality of possible *Sachbestimmtheit*, the totality of factual contents, the *Wesenheiten* — essences — of possible things. *Realitas* is accordingly equivalent to Leibniz's *possibilitas* — possibility. The realities are the *Wasgehalte* — what-contents — of possible things in general, regardless of whether they are actual (in our modern sense, "real") or not. The concept of *Realität* is equivalent to the concept of the Platonic *idea* as what is grasped of a being when I ask *ti esti* — what is the being? The answer gives me the *Wasgehalt* of the thing, what Scholasticism designated with *res*. The proximate source of Kant's terminology is the linguistic usage of Baumgarten, a student of Wolff. Kant frequently held his lectures in connection with Baumgarten's *Compendium of Metaphysics*, i.e., of ontology, and from there took over the corresponding terminology.

In discussing Kant's thesis — and indeed whenever one occupies oneself with Kant — we may not shy away from terminological discussion and a certain elaborateness. For precisely in Kant the concepts are clearly delimited and determined, in a sharpness that perhaps no philosophy before or after him has attained — though this does not say that the factual content of the concepts and what is meant with them corresponds in every respect radically to the interpretation. Precisely with respect to the expression *Realität*, it is hopeless to understand Kant's thesis and his position if one has not made clear to oneself the terminological sense of this expression that reaches back to Scholasticism and to antiquity. The immediate source for the term is Baumgarten, who was not only influenced by Leibniz and Descartes but goes directly back to Scholasticism.

Heidegger then expounds Baumgarten's definitions. In the sections in which Baumgarten delimits the *ens*, beings as such, he says: "What is posited as A-being, or is posited as not-A-being, is determined." The A so posited is a *determinatio*. Kant speaks of the *Bestimmung* — determination — that comes to the *What* of a thing, the *res*. Determination, *determinatio*, means that which determines a *res*: a real predicate. Hence Baumgarten says: "What in the way of determining is posited in any thing — marks and predicates — are determinations." When Kant uses the expression "*Dasein* is no determination," this expression is not arbitrary but is terminologically delimited: *determinatio*. These determinations, these *determinationes*, can be of two kinds. "The one positive and affirmative, which, if it truly is, is *Realität* (reality); the other negative, which, if it truly is, is *negatio* (negation)." *Realität*, accordingly, is the rightful, content-bearing, real determination — *determinatio* — that belongs to the *res*, the thing itself, to its concept. The opposite of *Realität* is *negatio* — negation.

Kant adopts these conceptual determinations not only in the pre-critical period but even in the *Critique of Pure Reason*. Thus he speaks of the concept of a thing and adds in parenthesis "of a real thing" (*eines Realen*), which does not mean "of an actual thing"; for *Realität* means the affirmatively-set, content-bearing predicate. Every predicate is at bottom a real predicate. Hence Kant's thesis reads: being is no real predicate — that is, being as such is no predicate of any thing whatsoever. Heidegger then sketches the categorial origin of Kant's terms: Kant derives the table of categories — to which both *Realität* and *Dasein* (existence) belong — from the table of judgments. Judgments, formally seen, are combinations of subject and predicate. All combining or uniting takes place in each case with respect to a *possible unity*. In every uniting, the idea of a unity is in view, even if not thematically grasped. The various possible *forms* of unity that come into view in judging (uniting) — these possible vantages or contents-of-vantage for the judging combining — are the categories. This is the logical concept of category in Kant, sprung from a purely *phenomenological* analysis (Heidegger insists), if one only follows what Kant means. The category is not a form with which one kneads some pre-given matter; the categories are *what stands in view of a uniting* as the idea of unity, the possible forms of the unity of combination. If the table of judgments is given to me — i.e., the totality of possible forms of uniting — then I can read off from this table the idea of unity presupposed in each judgment-form; I can deduce from it the table of categories. Kant here assumes that the table of judgments is in itself certain and stands by right — which is questionable. The categories are forms of the unity of possible unifyings in judging. To these unity-forms belong both *Realität* and existence (*Dasein*). We can clearly take the distinction between these two categories — *Realität* and *Dasein* — from the fact that they belong to quite different *classes* of categories. *Realität* belongs to the categories of *quality*. *Existence* — *Dasein*, *Wirklichkeit* — by contrast, belongs to the categories of *modality*. *Realität* is a category of quality. By "quality" Kant designates the character of judgment-positing that indicates whether to a subject a predicate is attributed, whether it is affirmed of it or opposed to it, that is, negated. *Realität* is accordingly the form of the unity of the affirmative, positive, positing judgment. This is exactly the definition Baumgarten gives of *Realität*. Existence, by contrast — *Dasein*, *Wirklichkeit* — belongs to the class of categories of modality. Modality says how the cognizing subject stands to what is judged in the judgment. The contrasting concept to *Dasein*, existence, *Wirklichkeit* is not, as is the case for *Realität*, negation, but possibility or necessity. *Dasein* corresponds, as category, to the *assertorical* — simply asserting — be it positive or negative judgment.

Heidegger then notes the ontological vocabulary tied to the category of reality: the expression *Realität* functions in the fixed meaning of factual content also in the term that traditional ontology often used of God — *ens realissimum*, or as Kant says, "the all-most-real being." This expression does not mean an actual being of the highest degree of actuality but the essence of the greatest possible factual content, the essence to which no positive *Realität*, factual determination, is lacking — or in Anselm's formulation: *aliquid quo maius cogitari non potest*. From this concept of *Realität* must be distinguished the Kantian concept of *objektive Realität*, which is equivalent with *Wirklichkeit*. *Objektive Realität* means the factualness that fulfills itself in some object thought-of in it — that is, the factualness that proves itself in the experienced being as actual, as existing. Kant says of objective reality and reality as such that one cannot think such a thing *in concreto* without the aid of experience, since *Realität* can refer only to sensation as the matter of experience, and not to the form of relation, with which one might play in fictions. Kant here distinguishes objective reality as *Wirklichkeit* from possibility. If I devise a possible thing, I move within the pure content-relations of this represented thing without thinking it as actual, on hand. This use of *Realität* is also found retrospectively in Descartes. Descartes says, for example, that *error* — and in general anything *malum*, anything unworthy — *non esse quid reale*, is nothing real. This does not mean: there is in fact no error; rather, error is indeed quite actual, but it and everything evil is not a *res* in the sense of being an independent factual content for itself. It is always only obtained, and is only through negation of an independent factual content, through the negation of the good. Likewise when Descartes in the proof of God's existence in the Third Meditation speaks of *realitas objectiva* and *realitas actualis*, he understands *realitas* in the named sense of factualness — Scholastically equivalent to *quidditas*. Descartes's *realitas objectiva* is not identical with Kant's *objektive Realität* but precisely its opposite. *Realitas objectiva* in Descartes means, in accordance with Scholasticism, the *objectified* — held-against-me-in-pure-representation — What, the essence of a thing. *Realitas objectiva* is equivalent to possibility, *possibilitas*. By contrast, what corresponds to Kant's concept of objective reality (or *Wirklichkeit*) is the Cartesian/Scholastic concept of *realitas actualis*: that What which is realized, *actu*. This remarkable difference between Descartes's concept of *realitas objectiva* as "subjectively-represented possibility" and Kant's concept of objective reality as the being-in-itself rests on the fact that the concept of the "objective" has at this time *reversed itself into its opposite*. The objective — that which is only held-against-me — is, in Kantian and modern parlance, the *subjective*. What Kant calls the subjective is, for Scholasticism, in accordance with the literal sense of "subject" (*subiectum* \= *hypokeimenon*), the underlying, the *objektive*. This historical observation about the inversion of the terms "subject" and "object" is one of Heidegger's significant philological insights.

Kant now says: *Dasein* is no reality. That means: it is no content-bearing determination of the concept of a thing, or, as he says shortly, no predicate of the thing itself. "A hundred actual thalers contain not the least bit more than a hundred possible ones." A hundred possible thalers and a hundred actual thalers do not differ in their *Realität*. Everything falls into confusion if one does not hold fast to the Kantian concept of *Realität* and instead reinterprets it in the modern sense of *Wirklichkeit*. Then one would want to say: a hundred possible thalers and a hundred actual thalers undoubtedly differ in respect to their reality — for the actual ones are actual and the possible ones have no reality in the non-Kantian sense. Kant says, in his usage: a hundred possible thalers and a hundred actual thalers do not differ in their *Realität*. The What-content of the concept "a hundred possible thalers" coincides with the What-content of the concept "a hundred actual thalers." In the concept "a hundred actual thalers" there are not more thalers thought, no higher *Realität* — but exactly the same number. What is possible is, with respect to its What-content, the same actually; the What-content, the *Realität* of both, must even be the same. Kant: "If I think a thing through whichever and however many predicates I want (even in thoroughgoing determination), then nothing in the least is added to the thing through my adding, this thing *is* — exists. For otherwise not exactly the same, but more, would exist than I had thought in the concept, and I could not say that exactly the object of my concept exists." This is the famous thaler example: existence adds nothing to the *concept* of the thing.

On the other hand, the fact still stands that "exists" — "a thing exists" — does occur in common usage as a predicate. The expression "is" in the widest sense even underlies every predication — also when I do not posit what I judge and predicate of as existing — when I only say "the body is by its essence extended," regardless of whether a body exists or not. Here too I use an "is" — the "is" in the sense of the *copula*, which is distinguished from the "is" when I say "God is" (God exists). Being as copula, as connective concept, and being in the sense of existence — *Dasein* — must accordingly be distinguished. With this terminological distinction the fourth thesis of the lecture course (the being of the copula, treated in Chapter Four) is already foreshadowed.

How does Kant elucidate this distinction? If being or *Dasein* is no real predicate, how can being be positively determined, and how does the concept of *Dasein* (*Vorhandenheit*) differ from the concept of being as such? Kant: "The concept of position or *Setzung* is wholly simple and one and the same with that of being as such. Now, something can be thought merely relatively-set, or, better, merely the relation (*respectus logicus*) of something as a mark to a thing — and then being, that is, the position of this relation, is nothing but the *connecting* concept in a judgment \[A is B\]. If not merely this relation \[i.e., if being and 'is' are not used merely in the sense of the copula, A is B\] but the thing in and for itself is regarded as set, then this being is as much as *Dasein* \[= *Vorhandensein*\]." *Dasein* is thereby distinguished from every predicate, which as such is always set merely relatively in relation to another thing. Being as such is identical with positing (Position) as such. In this sense Kant speaks of the "mere positions" (Realitäten) of a thing that make up its concept (its possibility), and which must not be mutually contradictory, insofar as the principle of (non-)contradiction is the criterion of logical possibility. Every predicate is, according to its concept, in each case set merely relatively. If, on the other hand, I say "something is there, exists," I do not take in this position any relative reference to some other thing or other thing-determination, to another *Reales* — but here I posit the thing in and for itself relationlessly, that is, here I posit *without* relation, not relatively, but *absolute*. In the assertion "A exists, A is on hand," there lies an *absolute Setzung* — absolute positing.

Being in the sense of "mere position" (to be something) must not be confused with being qua *Dasein*. While Kant in the *Beweisgrund* characterizes *Dasein* as absolute position, in the *Critique* he says: "It is merely the position of a thing or of certain determinations in themselves. In the logical use it is merely the *Kopula* of a judgment." *Dasein* is not "mere position"; when Kant says it is merely the position, this restriction applies with regard to its being no real predicate. "Mere" has in this context the meaning of "not relative." Being is neither in the meaning of "mere position" nor in the meaning of "absolute position" a real predicate. What being as position means, Kant clarifies in the named passages only with respect to being qua *Dasein*. He elucidates the concept of absolute position in accordance with the problem-context of the proof of God's existence. So we must hold fast to the provisional interpretation of being as "mere position" and of *Dasein* as "absolute position."

In the Baumgarten citation the expression *ponitur* — positing — also occurred. For also the real — i.e., the mere *what* of a thing — is in the pure representing of it in a certain way posited at itself. But this positing is merely the positing of the possible, *bloße Position*. Kant once says: "Since possibility was merely a position of the thing in relation to the understanding..., so actuality (*Wirklichkeit*, existence, *Dasein*) is at the same time a connection of the thing with perception." *Wirklichkeit*, *Dasein* is absolute position; possibility, by contrast, is mere position. "The proposition 'God is almighty' contains two concepts that have their objects: God and almightiness; the little word 'is' is not yet a predicate to boot, but only that which sets the predicate relatively to the subject." With this setting of "is" — the *bloße Position* — nothing is said about existence. Kant: "Hence this being \[of the copula\] is quite rightly used even in the relations that non-things \[Undinge\] have to one another" — for example, when I say "the circle is square." "Now if I take the subject (God) with all his predicates (under which almightiness also belongs) together, and say 'God is' or 'there is a God,' I set no new predicate to the concept of God, but only the subject itself with all its predicates — and indeed \[now the *absolute Setzung* is more precisely discussed\] the *object* \[Kant means the actual being\] in relation to my concept." The object — that is, the actual existent corresponding to the concept — comes in the assertion "God exists" *synthetically* to my concept, without my concept itself being augmented in the least by this being, this existence outside of my concept. From this it results: in the existence-assertion "God exists" (or "A is on hand") there also lies, indeed precisely, a *synthesis* — a positing of a relation — only it has an essentially different character than the synthesis of predication "A is B." The synthesis of the existence-assertion does not concern real determinations of the thing and their relations; rather, what is posited in the existence-assertion and what is set in addition to the mere representation, to the concept, is "a relation of the actual thing to myself." The relation that is posited is that of the entire concept-content, the full *Realität* of the concept, to its *object*. The thing meant in the concept is set in and for itself simply. The predicative synthesis moves within the relations among the factual contents; the existence-synthesis concerns the *totality of these factual relations in relation to their object*. The object is simply set. In existence-positing we must go *out of the concept*. The relation of the concept to the object — that is, to the actual — is what is synthetically added to the concept.

In the positing of an actual, existing thing, Kant says, I can ask doubly: *what* is posited, and *how* it is posited. To the question what is posited, the answer is: not more and nothing other than in the positing of a possible thing — indeed, exactly the same What-content, as the thalers-example shows. But I can also ask how it is posited. Then it is to be said: through *Wirklichkeit*, more is posited. Kant gathers the difference shortly: "In an existing thing nothing more is posited than in a merely possible one (for then talk is of its predicates); but through something existing, more is posited than through something merely possible, for the former \[the existing thing\] also reaches to the absolute position of the thing itself." With this the concept of *Dasein* in the sense of absolute position is, according to Kant, clarified — or rather, it is indicated from where something like *Dasein* or being as such *can* be clarified.

The relation set in absolute position is the relation of the existing object itself to its concept. If now, according to Kant, in common usage *Dasein* occurs as a predicate — so that the fact stands which speaks against Kant's thesis — then it is not so much a predicate of the thing itself, says Kant, but rather of the *thought* one initially has of the thing. "E.g., 'existence belongs to the sea unicorn.'" This means, according to Kant: "the representation of the sea unicorn is an *empirical concept*, that is, the representation of an existing thing." "God exists" should more precisely read: "*Something existing is God*" — with which reformulation Kant wants to indicate that existence is not thought in the predicate but in the *subject* of the proposition.

The application of this elucidation to the possibility of the ontological proof of God's existence follows of itself. Because *Dasein* is at all not a real predicate — therefore essentially cannot belong to the concept of a thing — I can never on the basis of the thinking of pure concept-content be assured of the *Dasein* of what is thought in the concept, unless I have already co-presupposed in the concept of the thing its actuality. But then, says Kant, this alleged proof is nothing but a wretched tautology.

Kant attacks the *minor premise* of the ontological proof: to the concept of God belongs existence. He attacks this proposition fundamentally by saying: existence, *Dasein*, does not belong to the concept of a thing at all. What Kant precisely doubts — that existence is a real predicate — is for Thomas self-evidently certain. Thomas only finds another difficulty: we are not in a position to know the belonging of the predicate of existence to God's essence (alongside other determinations) so transparently that we could draw from it a proof of the actual *Dasein* of what is thought. The Thomistic refutation is a refutation with regard to the *incapacity and finitude of our understanding*; the Kantian refutation is a *fundamental* one with regard to what the proof claims in its minor premise (and the minor premise is the hinge of every syllogism).

The problem of the proof of God's existence does not interest us here, Heidegger reminds us; rather, it is Kant's elucidation of the concept of being and *Dasein*: being \= position, *Dasein* \= absolute position. We do not yet ask whether this interpretation of the sense of being and *Dasein* is tenable, but only: does the elucidation given by Kant *satisfy*? Kant himself once emphasizes: "So simple is this concept \[*Dasein*, being\] that nothing can be said for its unfolding except to note the caution that it not be confused with the relations that things have to their marks." This can evidently only mean: the concept of being and *Dasein* is to be guarded against confusion, can be negatively delimited, but positively is accessible only *directly* in a simple understanding. For Heidegger the question arises: can this understanding of being and *Dasein* be driven further in the direction of Kant's discussion ("being \= position") — can we attain a higher degree of clarity *within* Kant's starting-point itself? Can it be shown that Kant's elucidation does not in fact have the clarity it claims? Does the thesis — "being \= position, *Dasein* \= absolute position" — perhaps lead into *darkness*? With this transition we move from the exposition of the Kantian thesis (§7) to its phenomenological analysis (§8).

## **§ 8\. Phenomenological Analysis of Kant's Elucidation of the Concept of Being and *Dasein***

### **a) Being (*Dasein*, Existenz, Vorhandensein), Absolute Position, and Perception**

The opening paragraph recapitulates the content of Kant's thesis: being or *Dasein* is no real predicate. The center of the elucidation of this thesis was the delimitation of the concept *Realität*. The determination of this concept is all the more necessary because today's philosophical concept of this term is *different* from the Kantian, which for its part agrees with the entire preceding tradition. *Realität* in Kant means as much as *Sachheit* — factualness, factual-determinacy. *Real* is what belongs to a *res*, to a thing, to its factual content. To the thing "house" belong: foundation walls, roof, door, size, extension, color — that is, real predicates or *determinationes*, real determinations of the thing "house," regardless of whether it is actually on hand or not.

The next paragraph isolates the crux. Kant says: *Wirklichkeit* of an actuality, existence of an existing thing, is *no* real predicate. A hundred thalers do not differ in their What-content, be they a hundred possible or a hundred actual thalers. *Wirklichkeit* does not touch the What — the *Realität* — but the *How* of being: here, whether possible or actual. Yet we say "the house exists" — in our terminology, "it is on hand." We attribute to this thing something like existence. The question arises: what kind of determination is existence and *Wirklichkeit*? Kant says negatively: *Wirklichkeit* is no real determination. As we will see later, the sense of this negative proposition is: *Wirklichkeit*, existence, is itself nothing actual, nothing existing; *being is itself no being*. This is one of the most important crystallizations of Heidegger's reading of Kant: Kant's negative thesis is read as an early formulation of the ontological difference between being and beings — a thesis Heidegger considers Kant intuitively saw but could not properly articulate.

Heidegger then refocuses on the positive determination. How does Kant positively determine the sense of *Dasein*, existence, *Vorhandenheit*? He sets *Dasein* equal to absolute position and identifies being with position as such. Kant himself only undertook this investigation for the purposes of clarifying the concept of existence with a view to the possibility of the ontological proof of God's existence. When he says that *Dasein* is no real predicate, he denies thereby the possible sense of the minor premise of the ontological proof: to the essence of God — to his *Realität* — belongs existence. Insofar as this minor premise is shaken in its fundamental possibility, the entire proof is demonstrated as impossible. Heidegger sets aside the God-proof question. The question is: how is this Kantian interpretation — being \= position, *Dasein* \= absolute position — to be understood more precisely? Does it stand by right? What does the closer grounding of this interpretation itself demand? We attempt a phenomenological analysis of the elucidation Kant gives of the concept of being and *Dasein*.

A methodical caveat seems to oppose our attempt to penetrate further into the interpretation of the concept of being and accordingly to clarify Kant's own elucidation. Kant himself, against the exaggerated method-mania that proves everything and ultimately nothing, wants to make "circumspection" (Behutsamkeit) in the clarification and resolution of concepts a methodological principle, and not decide from the outset "with a formal explanation what the fully determined concept of \[existence, *Dasein*\] consists in"; rather, he wants to assure himself first of "what one can say with certainty affirmatively or negatively of the object of the explanation." Heidegger cites Kant: "For as to the flattering representation that one makes oneself — that through greater perspicacity one will hit the matter better than others — one well understands that all those who have wanted to draw us out of a foreign error into their own have at every time spoken thus." Yet Kant does not thereby exempt himself from the task of clarifying the concept of existence. He continues, with characteristic noble elaborateness: "I fear, by too prolix a discussion of so simple an idea \[as being\], to become unintelligible. I might also fear to offend the tenderness of those who principally complain about dryness. But without holding this reproach as something small, I must this time ask permission of myself for it. For although I take just as little pleasure as anyone in the over-refined wisdom of those who exaggerate, distill, and refine sure and usable concepts in their logical smelting-kitchen until they evaporate in fumes and volatile salts, still the object of the consideration before me is of such a kind that one must either entirely give up ever attaining demonstrative certainty about it, or one must let it be that one resolves one's concepts down to these atoms." Kant explicitly points out that our entire cognition ultimately leads to unresolvable concepts. "If one sees that our entire cognition ultimately ends in unresolvable concepts, one also comprehends that there will be some that are almost unresolvable, i.e., where the marks are only very little clearer and simpler than the matter itself. This is the case in our explanation of existence. I gladly confess that through it the concept of the explained becomes distinct only in a very small degree. But the nature of the object in relation to the capacities of our understanding admits of no higher degree either." This confession is decisive: by Kant's own admission, the elucidation of *Dasein* and being cannot be driven further than the characterization "being \= position, *Dasein* \= absolute position."

Heidegger's strategy in response is precise: we do not first try to do better than Kant. We rather remain with Kant's elucidation — with what he has hit upon — and ask only whether this elucidation does not in fact, *in itself, apart from any other standard*, admit of "a higher degree" of distinctness. Is this elucidation — being \= position — *in every respect* sun-clear? Does the proposition "being \= position" leave everything in light or in darkness? Does not everything dissolve into an *Unbestimmtheit* — an indeterminacy? What does "position" say? What can this expression mean? We try first to gain a clarification of this conceptual elucidation *from Kant himself*, and then ask whether the phenomena thus drawn upon for clarification are themselves transparent, and whether the elucidation itself is determinate with respect to its methodical character and grounded in its right and necessity. The hermeneutical procedure is rigorously immanent: rather than importing a foreign standard, Heidegger forces Kant's own elucidation to display its own limits.

Heidegger then takes his first concrete step: working out what the existence-synthesis amounts to. We saw, in the experience of an existing being, there also lies a synthesis — only not the synthesis of predication, that is, of adding a predicate to a subject. In the assertion "A is B," B is a real predicate added to A. In the speech "A exists," by contrast, A — and indeed with the totality of its real determinations B, C, D, etc. — is *absolutely* set. To A this positing accrues, but not as B was added to A in the previous example. What is this added position? Evidently itself a relation — not, however, a factual or real relation within the real determinations of the thing A, but the relation of the *whole thing* (A) to my *thought* of the thing. Through this relation, what is so posited comes into relation with my I-state. Since A, originally only thought, in this thought-relation of mere thinking already stands in relation to me, evidently this mere thought-relation, this only-representing of A, becomes *something else* through the accrual of the absolute positing. In absolute position, the object of the concept — i.e., the actual existing being corresponding to the concept — is *as actual* set into relation with the merely thought concept.

Existence, *Dasein*, accordingly expresses a relation of the object to the *cognitive faculty* (Erkenntnisvermögen). Kant says at the beginning of the elucidation of the "Postulates of Empirical Thinking in General": "The categories of modality \[possibility, *Wirklichkeit*, necessity\] have this peculiarity about them: that they do not in the least increase the concept to which they are appended as predicates as determination of the object, but only express the *relation* \[of the object\] to the cognitive faculty." Real predicates, by contrast, express the factual relations immanent in the thing. Possibility expresses the relation of the object with all its determinations — that is, the entire *Realität* — to the understanding, to mere thinking. *Wirklichkeit* (existence, *Dasein*) expresses the relation to the empirical use of the understanding, or as Kant also says, to the empirical power of judgment. Necessity expresses the relation of the object to reason in its application to experience. The modal categories thus form a triplet keyed to three faculty-relations: possibility to understanding, actuality to judgment, necessity to reason.

Heidegger restricts himself to determining more precisely the relation of the object to the empirical use of the understanding expressed by *Wirklichkeit*. *Dasein* — actuality, existence — has, according to Kant, "only to do with the question whether such a thing \[as we can think of according to its possibility\] is given to us, so that the perception of it may, if possible, precede the concept." "But the perception that gives the matter for the concept is the sole character of *Wirklichkeit*." "Where therefore perception and what depends on it according to empirical laws extends, there also extends our cognition of the *Dasein* of things." It is *perception* that, in itself, carries the reach to *Dasein*, to *Wirklichkeit*, to existence of things — in our terminology, to the being-on-hand of things. Thus the specific character of absolute position, as Kant delimits it, unveils itself as *perception*. The predicates of *Wirklichkeit*, possibility, necessity — which can be called predicates only improperly — are not real-synthetic; they are, as Kant says, "only subjective." They "add to the concept of a thing (the real) the cognitive faculty." The predicate of *Wirklichkeit* adds to the concept of a thing the *perception*. Kant says, in short: *Wirklichkeit*, existence, *Dasein* \= absolute position \= perception.

But what is this supposed to mean — that to the thing, when it is grasped as existing, the cognitive faculty, perception, is "added"? Heidegger forces the question with characteristic vividness. I think merely a window with all its determinations. I represent such a thing to myself. In mere representation I make a window present to myself. To this represented thing I do *not* add further real predicates — color of the frame, hardness of the glass — but something *subjective*, something taken from the subject: the cognitive faculty, perception. This added perception — or the adding of perception — is supposed to make out the existence of the window? Kant says literally: "Perception... is the sole character of *Wirklichkeit*." How am I supposed to equip something thought — the thing "window" — with a perception? What does it say to add a "subjective cognitive faculty" to an object? How is the *Dasein* of the object supposed to come to expression through this? What is that: a window equipped with a perception, a house furnished with an "absolute position"? Are there such formations? Can even the strongest imagination conceive such a monstrosity (Mißgeburt) — a window equipped with a perception? Heidegger's rhetorical force here is calculated: by literalizing Kant's "addition" of perception to the object, he reveals that Kant cannot mean what he says, and yet he says it.

But perhaps Kant means by this rough talk of "adding my cognitive faculty, perception, to the thing" something else, even though his interpretation of *Dasein*, existence, expressly gives no further information. What does he basically mean, and what can he alone mean? Evidently only this: that the perception belonging to the subject *as its mode of comportment* is added to the thing — meaning: the subject brings itself perceptively to the thing into a comporting that *takes in and lets be encountered* this thing "in and for itself." The thing is set into the *relation of cognition*. In this perception, the existing, the on-hand, gives itself *at itself*. The real proves itself as actual. This phenomenological recasting is decisive: what Kant calls "absolute position" really means *the comporting of Dasein toward beings that lets them show themselves from themselves*. But Kant's vocabulary, with its talk of "adding perception to the thing," cannot adequately express this.

But is the concept of existence clarified through the going-back to perception that takes in something existing? How can Kant in general say — and he says it constantly — *Dasein* \= absolute position \= perception, that perception and absolute position are the sole character of *Wirklichkeit*? The question prepares the transition to subsection (b), where Heidegger will dismantle the ambiguity of "perception" itself.

### **b) Perceiving, Perceived, Perceivedness. The Distinction between Perceivedness and the Being-On-Hand of the On-Hand**

The opening of (b) seizes on the equivocation in Kant's identification. Something like *existence* is surely no perception. Perception is itself something that *is* — a being, a comporting carried out by the existing I, something actual in the actual subject. This actual-in-the-subject — perception — is not *Wirklichkeit*, and this actual-of-the-subject is by no means the *Wirklichkeit of the object*. Perception as the *act of perceiving* (Wahrnehmen) cannot be equated with existence. Perception is not existence but that which perceives an existing, on-hand being and refers to the perceived. Now we also customarily designate what is perceived in perception briefly as "perception." Perhaps Kant understands the expression "perception" in the identification of *Wirklichkeit* and perception in the sense of *the perceived* (Wahrgenommenes) — just as we say "the perception that I had to make there was painful," where I do not mean that the perceiving — the act of seeing — caused me pain, but that what I experienced, the perceived, oppressed me. So we take "perception" here in the meaning not of the perceiving act but of the *perceived* (Wahrgenommenes), and ask: can perception in this meaning be equated with existence, with *Wirklichkeit*? Can existence be equated with the *perceived existing thing*? In that case it would itself be a being, a real. That it is *not* is precisely the indisputable negative content of Kant's thesis. According to this thesis, it is excluded that *Wirklichkeit* be equal to the perceived actual.

From this it follows: existence is *not* equal to perception — neither in the sense of perceiving nor in the sense of the perceived. What then remains of Kant's identification of perception and *Wirklichkeit* (existence)?

Heidegger now makes one more concession to Kant and interprets him in his favor. Existence cannot be equated with the perceived existing-thing — but perhaps it can be equated with *Wahrgenommensein* — the being-perceived — of the perceived, with *Wahrgenommenheit* (perceivedness). Not the existing, on-hand window as this being is the *Dasein*, the being-on-hand — but the being-on-hand of the window is expressed in the moment of *being-perceived*, according to which the thing encounters us as perceived, as *discovered* (entdeckt), and is so accessible to us as on-hand on the basis of perceiving. "Perception" in Kant's discourse would then mean as much as *Wahrgenommenheit* — perceivedness, *Entdecktheit* (discoveredness) in perceiving. Kant himself says nothing about this, just as he gives no unambiguous information about whether he understands perception in the sense of perceiving as act or in the sense of the perceived as object of the act. So one thing comes out indisputably for now: Kant's discussion of the concept of *Dasein*, existence, *Wirklichkeit* as perception is *in every case unclear*, and to that extent capable of a higher degree of distinctness than Kant supposed — *if* it can be decided (and must be decided) whether perception here is to be understood as perceiving, as the perceived, or as perceivedness of the perceived; or whether all three meanings *in their unity* are meant, and what this then means. The threefold distinction — *Wahrnehmen* (perceiving act), *Wahrgenommenes* (perceived object), *Wahrgenommenheit* (perceivedness as the manner of disclosure) — is one of Heidegger's most important phenomenological clarifications and will become crucial for what follows.

The same lack of clarity that obtains regarding the concept of "perception" we find in the more generally framed interpretation Kant gives of being and *Dasein* when he equates being with position and *Dasein* with absolute position. Kant says, in the cited passage of the *Beweisgrund*: "The concept of position or *Setzung* is... one and the same with that of being as such." We ask: does position, *Setzung*, mean as much as positing as comporting of the subject (act), or is what is meant by "position" the posited, the object — or even the *Gesetztheit* (set-ness) of the posited object? Kant leaves this in the dark. The threefold structure — act, object, set-ness — is parallel to the threefold of perceiving, perceived, perceivedness.

Let us now let this lack of clarity — uncomfortable in the case of such fundamental concepts as *Dasein* and existence — pass. Let us take the interpretation of perception or position most favorable to Kant and identify *Dasein* and existence with *Wahrgenommenheit* (perceivedness) or *absolute Gesetztheit* (absolute set-ness), and correspondingly being as such with *Gesetztheit* (set-ness) as such. We then ask: is something existing *through* being-perceived? Does perceivedness of a being, of an existing thing, make out its existence? Are existence, *Wirklichkeit*, and perceivedness *einerlei*, one and the same? Heidegger's answer is forceful: the window does not, after all, *acquire* existence by my perceiving it; on the contrary, I can only perceive it *if* it exists and *because* it exists. *Perceivedness in every case presupposes perceivability*, and perceivability for its part already demands the existence of the perceivable (or perceived) being. Perception or absolute position is *at best* the *mode of access* to the existing, to the on-hand — the manner of its *Entdeckung* (discovery). But *Entdecktheit* is *not* the being-on-hand of the on-hand, the existence of the existing. Existence belongs to the on-hand, to the existing, *without* its being discovered. Only therefore is it discoverable. Likewise, position in the sense of set-ness is not the being of beings and identical with it, but at most the *How* of the grasped-ness of something set. The structure of Heidegger's correction is decisive: Kant has confused the *condition of access* (perception) with the *being of what is accessed* (existence). The condition of access is grounded in the existence, not the other way around. This *priority of being over disclosure* will become the entire problem of the following section.

Thus the preliminary analysis of Kant's interpretation of *Dasein* and existence yields a doubled result. This interpretation is, first, not only unclear and to that extent in need of higher distinctness; second, even on the most favorable construal — being \= perceivedness — it is *questionable* (fragwürdig). The next section (§9) will draw the consequences and demand a more fundamental reformulation of the problem.

## **§ 9\. Demonstration of the Necessity of a More Fundamental Formulation of the Problem-Content of the Thesis and of its More Radical Grounding**

The transition paragraph between §8 and §9 sets the program of the entire section. Should we remain with this negative critical finding? A merely negative carping would be an unworthy enterprise vis-à-vis Kant and at the same time an unfruitful business with respect to the goal we are striving toward. We want to attain a positive clarification of the concepts of *Dasein*, existence, and being as such — and indeed in such a way that we do not simply oppose to Kant our own (that is, a foreign) opinion. Rather, we want to follow Kant's own starting-point — the interpretation of being and existence — further in its own *Blickrichtung* (direction-of-gaze). In the end Kant moves quite well in the *right direction* in his attempt to clarify *Dasein* and existence. Only he does not see the *horizon* out of which and within which he wants to carry out the clarification clearly enough, and that because he has not previously assured himself of this horizon adequately and explicitly readied it for his elucidation. What follows from this is what §9 is meant to expound. The methodology is exemplary of Heidegger's reading of canonical figures: not to refute Kant but to push Kant in the direction Kant himself was moving, by making explicit what Kant tacitly relies on.

### **a) The Insufficiency of Psychology as a Positive Science for the Ontological Clarification of Perception**

The opening paragraph of §9a poses the question: is it chance and merely a whim of Kant's that, in attempting to clarify being, *Dasein*, *Wirklichkeit*, existence, he reaches back to such things as *Setzung* and perception? Where does he direct his gaze in this recourse? Whence does he draw the clarifying marks of the concept of *Dasein*, existence? Whence comes such a thing as position? What is necessarily co-thought therein as what makes possible such a thing as position? Has Kant in general adequately delimited these *conditions of possibility* of position itself, and thereby clarified the essence of position and brought what is clarified through it — being, *Wirklichkeit* — itself into the light? The four-fold question structure pinpoints exactly where Kant has stopped short: at the *phenomena* (position, perception) without their *enabling conditions*.

We saw, Heidegger continues, *Wahrgenommenheit* (perceivedness, *Entdecktheit* of the on-hand) is not the same as the *Vorhandenheit* of the on-hand. But in every discovery of an on-hand thing, this is discovered *as* on-hand — that is, *in its Vorhandenheit*. Accordingly, in the perceivedness or discoveredness of an on-hand thing, *Vorhandenheit is somehow co-disclosed, co-opened-up*. Being is indeed not identical with set-ness, but set-ness is the *How* in which the positing of beings assures itself of *the being of these posited beings*. Perhaps with adequate analysis the being or *Wirklichkeit* and its sense discovered in perceivedness and set-ness can be clarified through them. If, then, we succeed in adequately clarifying *all* the essential structures of the discovering of the on-hand — perception, absolute positing — we must also be able on this path *at least to come upon* such things as existence, *Dasein*, *Vorhandenheit*. The question arises: how is an adequate determination of the phenomena of perception and position to be won that Kant draws on for clarifying *Wirklichkeit* and existence? We have shown that the concepts with whose help Kant tries to clarify the concepts of being, *Dasein*, themselves need clarification — first, because the concepts of perception and position are *vieldeutig*, multivalent (it is undecided in which meaning Kant understands them or what is meant by them), and second, because even on the most favorable interpretation it is *questionable* whether being can in general be interpreted as position, *Dasein* as perception. These phenomena, perception and position, themselves need clarification; the question is how this is to be accomplished. Evidently *in the going-back to what makes perception, positing, such cognitive faculties possible* — to what underlies perception and positing, to what determines them *as comportments* of the being to which they belong.

The next paragraph identifies the apparent direction of regress: all thinking, all positing is for Kant an "I think." The I and its states, its comportments, the *psychisches* (psychic) — as one generally says — need a prior clarification. The ground of the deficiency of Kant's conceptual elucidation regarding existence, *Dasein*, seems to lie openly on the surface: Kant works with a still very rough psychology. Had he — so one might be tempted to suppose — had the possibility that exists today of investigating such a thing as perception exactly, and of standing on the ground of facts instead of moving in empty acuity and dualistic conceptual constructions, then another insight into the essence of *Dasein* and existence would have grown for him.

But how does it stand with this call for a scientific psychology standing on the ground of facts as the foundation of Kant's problem — and that means: of every philosophical problem? Heidegger must briefly discuss whether psychology in principle — not merely in this or that direction of its work — is in a position to provide the ground for Kant's problem and to procure the means for its solution. The strategy is decisive: Heidegger will not contest psychology's progress in factual research but the *kind of science* psychology fundamentally is.

The next paragraph characterizes contemporary psychology. Psychology positions itself — which it rightly claims as its advantage — on the ground of facts. As exact inductive research of facts it has its guiding image in mathematical physics and chemistry. It is a *positive science* of a determinate being — a science that even in its historical development, especially in the nineteenth century, took mathematical physics as its model of scientificity. Today's psychology says in all its directions (which differ almost only terminologically — Gestalt psychology, developmental psychology, thought psychology, eidetics): we have today moved beyond the naturalism of the previous century and previous decades; *life* is for us the object of psychology, no longer merely sensations, tactile impressions, and memory-performances; we investigate life in its full actuality, and when we investigate it we awaken liveliness in ourselves; our science of life is at the same time the proper *philosophy*, since it shapes life itself and is a view of life and worldview; this investigation of life settles in the realm of facts, it builds from below and does not move in the airy space of customary philosophy. Heidegger registers no objection in principle to a positive science of life-phenomena, to a biological anthropology — which, like every positive science, has its own right and significance. That today's psychology in this anthropological orientation, in addition more or less explicitly and programmatically gives itself a *philosophical* significance — because it believes it works for the formation of a living view of life and for the so-called life-nearness of science, and therefore calls biological anthropology "philosophical anthropology" — is an irrelevant side-phenomenon often found precisely in the positive, above all the natural, sciences. We need only recall Haeckel or the attempts of the present (e.g., with the help of relativity theory) to ground and proclaim a worldview or a philosophical standpoint. The polemic is precisely targeted: the inflation of empirical psychology into a worldview-philosophy is one of the symptoms of the very confusion §2 had diagnosed.

The next paragraph poses the first of two fundamental questions about psychology. When today's psychology says we have moved beyond the naturalism of the previous decades, it would be a misunderstanding to believe psychology has *moved itself* beyond naturalism. Where psychology today stands fundamentally in all its directions with the emphasis on the anthropological problem, *Dilthey* stood absolutely unambiguously already more than three decades ago — only that in his time the supposedly scientific psychology (the predecessor of today's) opposed and rejected him in the strongest terms as unscientific. (Cf. Ebbinghaus's critique of Dilthey, Heidegger notes parenthetically.) Psychology has not brought itself to where it now stands through itself, on the basis of its results, but through a more or less consciously executed fundamental *reorientation* onto the whole of life-phenomena. This reorientation it could not avoid in the long run, because it was demanded for decades by Dilthey and by phenomenology. The reorientation is necessary if psychology is not to become philosophy but to *come to itself as positive science*. These new formulations of questions in today's psychology — of incalculable significance — must of course lead in the positive psychological science of life to new results vis-à-vis the old formulations. For nature, both physical and psychic, answers in experiment only what one questions it about. The result of positive research can only ever *confirm* the fundamental questioning in which it moves. It cannot, however, *ground* the fundamental questioning itself or determine its sense. This is one of Heidegger's most important methodological theses about the limits of positive science: empirical results can confirm but cannot found their own framework.

With this we come upon the second fundamental question concerning psychology. When today's psychology extends its research field to that which in its wholeness Aristotle already assigned to it — the whole of life-phenomena — this expansion of the field is only the *completion* of the field that belongs to psychology, that is, only a previous deficiency is removed. Psychology remains what it is; it first really becomes what it can be: a science of a determinate region of beings, of life. It remains *positive science*. As such, however, it needs — like every positive science — a prior delimitation of the *being-constitution* of the being it makes its theme. The being-constitution of its region — which psychology, like every other positive science (physics, chemistry, biology in the narrower sense, but also philology, art history) *tacitly presupposes* — is itself, according to its very sense, *inaccessible to positive science*, since *being is no being* and accordingly demands a fundamentally different mode of grasping. All positive positing of beings includes an a priori cognition and an a priori understanding of the being of this being, even though positive experience of beings knows nothing of this understanding and is unable to bring what is understood in it to its concept. The being-constitution of beings is accessible only to a *totally different* science — to philosophy as science of being. All positive sciences of beings can only, as Plato once says, *dream* of beings, that is, of their thematic object — they are not *awake* (wach) to what makes the being into the being it is, to being. But nevertheless being is, in a certain way (dream-like, *traumhaft*), co-given to them. Plato touches this distinction in connection with the relation of geometry to philosophy.

Heidegger then expounds Plato's dream-metaphor. Geometry is a science that according to its method of cognition seems to coincide with philosophy. It is no science of experience in the sense of physics or botany but *a priori* cognition. It is therefore no accident that modern philosophy strove to pose and solve its problems *more geometrico*, according to the mathematical method. Kant himself emphasizes that a positive science is only so far a science as it contains mathematics. Nevertheless Plato says: although geometry is a priori cognition, it differs *fundamentally* from philosophy, which is also a priori cognition and has the *Apriori* as its theme. Geometry has as its object a determinate being of determinate What-content — *pure space* — which is indeed not on hand like a physical material thing, nor existing like a living being, life, but *is* in the mode of *Bestehen* (subsisting, holding-good). Plato is cited (Republic VII, 533b6 ff.): the other *technai*, modes of dealing with beings — those of which we said they each thematically grasp a piece of beings as such, i.e., the sciences of beings, geometry and those sciences following on it making use of it — *dream of beings*; they are unable to *see* beings as something *intuited in waking*, *idein*, *idea* — that is, to grasp the *being* of this being. They are unable to do this as long as they make use of *presuppositions* about beings, about their being-constitution, and leave these presuppositions *akinētous* — unmoved — that is, do not traverse them in philosophical cognition, in *dialectic*. To this they are fundamentally unable, since they cannot demonstrate what beings *in themselves* are. They cannot give an account of what a being *as* being is. The concept of being and of the being-constitution of beings is closed to them.

Plato makes a distinction in the manner in which beings, *to on*, are accessible for positive sciences and for philosophy. The *on* is accessible for positive sciences in *dreaming*. The Greeks have a short word for this: *onar*. But not for them is the *on* accessible as something *intuited in waking* (*hupar*). Among the sciences that only *dream* of their object, Plato counts even geometry. So what geometry treats a priori still has a further *Apriori* underlying it, for which geometry itself is *not awake* — not by chance but in accordance with its scientific character cannot be awake — just as little as arithmetic can understand and clarify in its proper essence the principle of contradiction, of which it constantly makes use. I cannot elucidate the principle of contradiction arithmetically or in any like manner. If even a priori sciences like geometry, which never and nowhere treat empirical facts, still presuppose something inaccessible to them — the being-constitution of their thematic field — then this holds all the more for all factual sciences, hence also for psychology as science of life, or as one now often says following Dilthey: anthropology, the science of the living human being. Every psychology only *dreams* of the human being and human Dasein, because it must necessarily make presuppositions about the being-constitution of human Dasein and its mode of being, which we name *existence*. These ontological presuppositions remain forever closed to psychology as ontic science. Psychology must have them given to it by philosophy as ontology. But the positive sciences — and this is the remarkable thing — *arrive at their results precisely in these dreams*. They do not need to become philosophically awake, and when they do, they themselves never become philosophy.

Heidegger then makes a striking historical-cultural diagnosis. The history of all positive sciences shows that they only momentarily awake from dreams and open their eyes for the being of the being they investigate. We stand today in such a situation. The basic concepts of the positive sciences are coming into motion (geraten in Bewegung). One demands their revision in returning to the originary sources from which they spring. More precisely, we *were* just in such a moment. Whoever today listens more closely and senses the actual movements of the sciences over the external noise and busyness of the scientific industry must see that *they are already dreaming again* — which is not meant as a reproach to science but as the recognition that the sciences are returning to their fitting and customary condition. It is too uncomfortable to sit on a powder keg and know that one's basic concepts are worn-out opinions. One has had enough of asking about basic concepts; one wants to have peace. Philosophy as science of the "inverted world" is *unheimlich* — uncanny — to common understanding. Therefore one furnishes the concept of philosophy not according to its idea but according to the prevailing needs and possibilities of common understanding, to which, as Kant says, nothing imposes itself as much as facts. The polemic against the early-twentieth-century revival of dogmatic metaphysics and against the comfort of positive science is sharp.

The conclusion of §9a follows: these reflections on the relation of positive sciences to philosophy, following Plato's word, should make clear: even if Kant had had an exact psychology of perception and of cognition, it would not have advanced the task of clarifying the concepts of *Dasein* and existence *in the least*. Not because the psychology of his time was not exact and empirical enough did Kant's clarification of the questioned concepts get nowhere, but because it was *not adequately a priori grounded* — because the *ontology of the Dasein of the human being* was lacking. The deficiency of Kant's interpretation of *Dasein* and existence as perception and position — to be discussed still more precisely — psychology can *never* remedy, because psychology itself must be helped. Anthropology in the sense of psychology as a positive science as the foundation of philosophy (e.g., of logic) is fundamentally still more absurd than if someone tried to ground geometry with the help of chemistry and physics of bodily things. From this science — whatever the state of its development — we can expect *no help* for the clarification of a philosophical problem. It hardly needs noting, Heidegger adds, that this does not mean psychology is *no* science; on the contrary, the fundamental determination of the scientific character of psychology as a *positive*, that is, non-philosophical science speaks *for* psychology, in order to draw it out of the common confusion.

When Kant interprets *Dasein*, existence, *Vorhandenheit* as perception, the phenomenon "perception" is itself not to be brought to clarity through psychology. Psychology must rather already *know* what perception in general is if it does not wish to investigate factual perception-processes and their genesis blindly and gropingly.

### **b) The Being-Constitution of Perception. Intentionality and Transcendence**

The opening of §9b returns to the systematic question: from what Kant leaves unclarified at the phenomena "perception" and "position," and from what he allows to dissolve in the demonstrated multivalence, we now try to extract what kind of investigation of what connections is needed in advance to provide the firm ground, the clear horizon, and the secure access for the task of an interpretation of *Dasein*, existence, *Vorhandenheit*, *Wirklichkeit*, being as such.

Kant's thesis — being is not a real predicate — is in its *negative* content untouchable. Kant fundamentally wants to say with it: *being is no being*. Against this, Kant's positive interpretation — *Dasein* as absolute position (perception), being as position as such — proved itself both *undeutlich* (unclear) and *vieldeutig* (multivalent) and at the same time, on the most fitting reading, *fragwürdig* (questionable). We ask now: what does Kant in fact leave undetermined when he uses perception and position in the indicated multivalence? What lies in the dark when perceiving, the perceived, and the perceivedness of the perceived are not distinguished but nevertheless are determined as belonging unitarily to perception? Nothing less than the *being-constitution* of perception as such — its *ontological essence* — and likewise the being-constitution of positing. The multivalent or unclear use of the terms "perception" and "Setzung" in Kant is the *index* that he leaves the ontological essence of positing and perception in general undetermined. But in this lies further: ultimately the comportments of the I (of *Dasein* in our terminology) are *as such* ontologically unclarified. The *appropriate explicit ontology of Dasein* — of the being that we ourselves are — lies in disarray (im Argen). But not only this: it has not even been recognized that the adequate working-out of this ontology is the presupposition for posing the problem whose solution Kant takes on with the elucidation of the concept of being.

Heidegger then sets the limits of what he will undertake here. On the basic concept of an ontology of Dasein he will not enter; this will occupy the second and third parts of the lecture course. On the discussion of its function as the foundation of philosophical research in general he will not enter either, and even less is it possible here to develop and present the ontology of Dasein even in its main features. (He notes parenthetically that he has just presented an attempt in the first half of *Sein und Zeit*.) Rather we now try, conversely, through a further-going analysis of Kant's problem and Kant's solution, to *lead into the territory of the ontology of Dasein as the foundation of ontology in general*. The procedure of GA 24 is therefore precisely the inverse of the procedure of *Sein und Zeit*: rather than analyzing Dasein first and then approaching the question of being, the lecture course approaches the ontology of Dasein through the problems of the traditional theses about being.

Kant interprets existence — Heidegger will now say *Vorhandenheit*, since he claims "Dasein" for the human being — as perception. The threefold meaning (perceiving, perceived, perceivedness of the perceived) must be retained. But have we gained anything for the clarification of the concept of existence by expressly noting the multivalence and holding fast to the three meanings? Have we gotten further in the understanding of the phenomenon meant by this expression through the separation of the three meanings? Through the enumeration of what a word in its multivalence can mean, one wins no knowledge of the matter. Certainly not. But these meaning-distinctions of the term "perception" have ultimately their *ground in the matter meant by them, in the phenomenon of perception itself*. Not only the meaning-distinctions as expressly known, but precisely also the *imprecise* use of the multivalent word probably goes back to the peculiarity of the matter meant. Presumably the multivalence of the expression "perception" is not accidental but precisely attests that the phenomenon meant by it offers — already to vulgar experience and understanding — the support for grasping it now as perceiving (a perceiving comportment), now as perceived (in the sense of what the perceiving comportment comports itself toward), now as perceivedness (in the sense of the being-perceived of what is perceived in the perceiving comportment). It could be that the phenomenon meant by "perception" furnishes the *ground and support* for the multivalence because it itself, in its own structure, is not simple but *multivalent*. Presumably what is meant separately in the three meanings belongs originally to the unitary structure of what we are to understand as perception. Perhaps in the individual meanings and the grasping of the matter guided by them, this matter is in each case envisioned in a different respect. The methodological principle here is exemplary: linguistic ambiguity is, at the most fundamental level, a clue to the structure of the thing itself.

This is indeed so. What we briefly call perception, more explicitly stated, is: *perceiving self-directing toward the perceived*, in such a way that the perceived is itself understood as perceived in its perceivedness. This determination seems to be no especially profound wisdom: perception is perceiving, to which a perceived in its perceivedness belongs. Is that not an empty tautology — a table is a table? The determination, though provisional, is *more than a tautology*. We say with it: perception and the perceived in its perceivedness *belong together*. We say with the discourse of perceiving self-directing-toward that the belonging-together of the three moments of perception are characteristics of this *self-directing-toward*. This self-directing-toward makes up, as it were, the *framework* (Gerüst) of the entire phenomenon "perception."

But that perceiving directs itself at a perceived — or, generally and formally spoken, relates to it — is, after all, too self-evident to require special note. Kant says the same when he speaks of the thing (the perceived) coming into relation with the cognitive faculty (the perceiving), when he speaks of a *subjective synthesis*. Moreover, this explicitly noted relation of perceiving to the perceived also accrues to other comportments: mere representing relates to the represented, thinking thinks something thought, judgment determines something judged, loving relates to a loved. These are, one would think, *unsurpassable trivialities* one should be ashamed to pronounce. Nevertheless, we will not refrain from explicitly fixing this determination: comportments comport themselves *toward* something; they are directed at this whither-to, formally spoken: related. But what shall we do with this determination of the relation of comportments to that toward which they comport themselves? Is this still philosophy? Whether this is philosophy or not we leave open. We even concede it is not yet philosophy. Nor does it concern us what we do with the determination of the alleged trivialities — whether thereby we penetrate the secrets of world and Dasein or not. *The one thing that concerns us is that this trivial determination and what is meant therein not slip away from us — that we perhaps still bring it closer.* Perhaps then the alleged triviality turns into complete riddle-character. Perhaps this insignificance becomes one of the most exciting problems for those who can *philosophize*, that is, for those who have learned to understand that *the self-evident is the true and only theme of philosophy*. This is a programmatic formulation of Heidegger's understanding of phenomenological method as the de-trivialization of the apparently obvious.

The comportments have the structure of *Sichrichten-auf*, of being-directed-toward. *Phenomenology designates this structure, following a Scholastic term, as intentionality.* Scholasticism speaks of the *intentio* of the will, of the *voluntas* — that is, it speaks of intentionality only regarding the will. It is far from assigning *intentio* to the other comportments of the subject, or even from grasping the sense of this structure fundamentally. It is therefore both a historical and a substantive error if one says — as is mostly done today — that the doctrine of intentionality is a doctrine of Scholasticism. But even if this were so, it would be no reason to reject it but only to ask whether it holds up in itself. But Scholasticism does not know the doctrine of intentionality. Rather, Franz Brentano, in his *Psychologie vom empirischen Standpunkt* (1874), under strong influence of Scholasticism (above all Thomas and Suárez), emphasized intentionality more sharply and said that with respect to this structure — the manner of self-directing-toward something — the totality of psychic lived-experiences could and must be classified. (The title *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* means something very different from today's "empirical psychology," Heidegger warns.) By Brentano, Husserl is influenced, who for the first time in the *Logical Investigations* clarified the essence of intentionality and continues this clarification in the *Ideas*. Nevertheless it must be said: this enigmatic phenomenon of intentionality is far from being philosophically adequately grasped. Research concentrates precisely on seeing this phenomenon more clearly. The historical reconstruction is carefully calibrated: intentionality is post-Scholastic, opened by Brentano, philosophically founded by Husserl, but still to be worked out.

In remembrance of what we said about perception itself, the concept of intentionality can first be clarified thus: every comportment is a *Verhalten-zu* (comporting-toward); perception is a *Wahrnehmen-von* (perceiving-of). This comporting-toward we designate in the narrower sense as the *intendere* or the *intentio*. Every comporting-toward and every being-directed-toward has its specific *Wozu* of the comportment and *Worauf* of the being-directed. This Wozu-of-comportment and Worauf-of-being-directed belonging to the *intentio* we designate as the *intentum*. Intentionality embraces both moments — the *intentio* and the *intentum* — in their unity, which is still dark. Both moments are different in every comportment; the difference of the *intentio* or of the *intentum* constitutes precisely the difference of the modes of comportment. They are different with respect to their own respective intentionality. The Latin formula — intentio/intentum — is one of Heidegger's most economical pieces of conceptual machinery and will recur constantly in what follows.

What is now needed is to follow up this structure of Dasein's comportments with special regard to perception and to ask how this structure of intentionality itself looks and, above all, *how it is ontologically grounded in the basic constitution of Dasein*. First it is necessary to bring intentionality as a structure of Dasein's comportments still closer to us — that is, to guard it against ready-at-hand and constantly pressing-in misinterpretations. We do not so much have in mind the misinterpretations with which contemporary philosophy overwhelms intentionality, misinterpretations that all spring from preconceived epistemological or metaphysical standpoints. We leave determinate epistemologies — determinate philosophical theories in general — entirely aside. We must make the attempt to see the phenomenon of intentionality simply and without prejudice. But even if we extricate ourselves from the prejudices that spring from philosophical theories, we are not yet safe from all misinterpretations. On the contrary, the most dangerous and persistent prejudices regarding the understanding of intentionality are not the explicit ones in the form of philosophical theories, but the *implicit ones* — those that spring from the natural comprehension and interpretation of things through the everyday understanding of Dasein. These are precisely the least noticeable and the hardest to reject. Heidegger orients himself again at the intentional character of perception in trying to make a misinterpretation of intentionality conspicuous.

The first misinterpretation begins from the natural reading of "I perceive the window over there." Perception has intentional character — that is, the perceiving (the *intentio*) relates to the perceived (the *intentum*). How will one naturally characterize this relation? The object of perception is the window there. The relation of the perception of the window evidently expresses the relation in which the window on hand there stands to me, the human being on hand here — the subject. With this perception of the window now on hand, accordingly a *relation on hand* between two beings is created — between the on-hand object and the on-hand subject. The relation of perception is an *on-hand relation between two on-hand things*. If I remove one relatum, say the subject, then the relation itself is no longer on hand. If I let the other relatum (the object, the on-hand window) disappear or think it as disappeared, then evidently with it the relation between me and the on-hand object disappears, indeed the very *possibility* of the relation, since the relation now has no support-point in the on-hand object. The intentional relation can, so it seems, only be on hand *as* relation if both relata are on hand, and the relation lasts only so long as these relata are themselves on hand. Put otherwise: the psychic subject needs, in order for a possible relation between it and another to exist, the on-handness of a physical object. Were there no physical things, the psychic subject would have to be on hand for itself, isolated, without this intentional relation. The intentional relation accrues to the subject by grace of the on-handness of the object, and vice versa. This all *seems* self-evident. Heidegger reproduces the natural ontology with great care so that its overthrow is conspicuous.

Nevertheless, in this characterization of intentionality — as an on-hand relation between two on-hand things, a psychic subject and a physical object — the essence as well as the *mode of being* of intentionality is missed from the ground up. The miss lies in this: the interpretation takes the intentional relation for something that *first accrues* to the subject on the basis of the emerging of the on-handness of an object. The opinion is hidden in this: *in itself, as isolated psychic subject, this subject is without intentionality*. Over against this, it must be seen that the intentional relation does not first arise through the accrual of an object to a subject — somewhat as a distance between two on-hand bodies first arises and is on hand when to one on-hand body another accrues. The intentional relation to the object does not first fall to the subject with and through the on-handness of the object; rather, *the subject is intentionally structured in itself*. As subject, it is directed at... Heidegger then gives the now-famous *hallucination argument*: suppose someone is seized by a hallucination — hallucinating, he sees here in this hall that elephants are moving in it. He perceives these objects, although they are not on hand. He perceives them, he is perceptively directed at them. We have here a being-directed at objects without these being on hand. They are, we say (we the others), only *putatively* given to him as on hand. But these objects can be only putatively given to the hallucinator because his perceiving (in the mode of hallucination, as such) is such that *in this perceiving something can be encountered* — because perceiving is in itself a comporting-toward, a relation to the object, whether actual or only putatively on hand. *Only because hallucinating perceiving as perception has in itself the character of being-directed-toward can the hallucinator putatively mean something.* Putatively grasping something I can only if I, *as grasping*, mean something in general. Then *meaning* can take on the modification of putativity. The intentional relation does not first arise through the actual on-handness of objects but lies in perceiving itself, whether it is free of deception or deceives itself. *Perceiving must be perception-of-something so that I can deceive myself about something.* The hallucination case is the crucial test: the directedness is *prior to* the actual presence of the object, hence cannot be constituted by it.

So it becomes clear: the discourse of the *relation* of perceiving to an object is *zweideutig*, two-sensed. It can mean: perceiving as something psychic in the on-hand subject stands in a relation to an on-hand object, which relation is itself on hand on the basis of these two on-hand things. This relation stands and falls accordingly with the on-handness of the relata. *Or* the speech "relation of perception to an object" means: perceiving is *in itself, in its structure*, constituted by this relation — whether the *Wozu* it comports itself toward is on hand as object or not. The second meaning of the discourse of the relation of perception to an object better hits the peculiarity of intentionality. The expression "relation of perception" does not mean a relation into which perception, as the one relatum-point, *first enters* — a relation that falls to the in-itself relation-free perception; rather it means a relation *that perceiving as such itself is*. This relation, which we mean with intentionality, is the *a priori relation-character* of what we designate with self-comporting (Sichverhalten). The structural correction is precise: intentionality is not a relation added to a self-subsistent relatum but the inner *structure* of what we mean by self-comporting.

Intentionality is, as structure of comportments, itself a structure of the *self-comporting subject*. It is in the mode of being of the self-comporting subject *as* the relation-character of this relation. It belongs to the *essence* of comportments, so that the talk of "intentional comportment" is already a pleonasm and amounts to my saying "a spatial triangle." Conversely, so long as intentionality as such is not seen, comportments are *confusedly* thought — as when I represent a triangle to myself without the corresponding space-idea that underlies it and makes it possible. The analogy is striking: intentionality is to comportment as space is to the triangle.

With this we have warded off a misinterpretation of intentionality familiar to common understanding, but at the same time we have suggested a new misinterpretation, to which almost exclusively non-phenomenological philosophy falls victim. We want also to discuss this second misinterpretation, without entering more closely into determinate theories.

The result of the previous clarification was: intentionality is not an objective on-hand relation between two on-hand things, but as relation-character of comportment is a *determination of the subject*. Comportments are those of the I. One usually also calls them *Erlebnisse* (lived-experiences) of the subject. The lived-experiences are intentional and accordingly belong to the I, or as one says learnedly, they are *immanent to the subject*, they belong to the *subjective sphere*. But the subject and its lived-experiences are what is for the subject (the I) first alone and undoubtedly certainly given — according to a general methodological conviction of modern philosophy since Descartes. The question arises: how can this I, with its intentional lived-experiences, get out from its lived-experience-sphere and take up a relation to the on-hand world? How can the I *transcend* its own sphere and the intentional lived-experiences enclosed in it — and in what does this transcendence consist? More precisely it must be asked: what does the intentional structure of lived-experiences accomplish for the philosophical clarification of transcendence? For intentionality designates a relation of the subject to the object. But we have heard intentionality is a structure of lived-experiences and thereby belongs to the subjective sphere. So even intentional self-directing seems to remain *within* the subjective sphere and, taken in itself, accomplishes nothing for the clarification of transcendence. How do we get from intentional lived-experiences inside, in the subject, *out* to the things as objects? In itself — so one says — intentional lived-experiences, as belonging to the subjective sphere, relate only to what is *immanent* to this sphere. Perceptions, as something psychic, are directed at *sensations*, representational images, memory-residues, and determinations that thinking (likewise immanent to the subject) adds to what is initially subjectively given. With this the supposedly central philosophical problem must first be posed: how do lived-experiences and what they direct themselves at as intentional — the subjective of sensations, representations — relate to the *objective*?

This formulation of the question also *seems* plausible and necessary, since we ourselves said: the lived-experiences that should have the character of intentionality belong to the subjective sphere. The further question seems unavoidable: how do the intentional lived-experiences belonging to the subjective sphere relate to the *transcendent* objects? As plausible as this formulation of the question appears, and as widespread as it may be — *even within phenomenology* and the directions of new epistemological realism close to it (e.g., the conception of N. Hartmann) — *so elementarily does this interpretation of intentionality fail to recognize this phenomenon*. It fails to recognize it because theory is to it the first, before fulfilling the demand to open one's eyes and take the phenomena, against all firmly rooted theory and in defiance of it, as *they give themselves* — that is, to *orient theory by the phenomena* and not, conversely, to do violence to the phenomena through a preconceived theory. This is the phenomenological principle in its sharpest form: zu den Sachen selbst is here turned against not just Neo-Kantianism but against intentionality-theorists who keep the Cartesian framework in place.

Where lies the source of this second misinterpretation of intentionality to be clarified? Not, as in the first, in the character of the *intentio*, but in the character of the *intentum* — of that toward which the comportment (in our case perception) is directed. One says: intentionality is a character of lived-experiences. These belong to the sphere of the subject. What is more natural and logical than now to conclude: consequently, what the immanent lived-experiences direct themselves at must itself also be *subjective*. But as natural and logical as this conclusion and as critical and cautious as this characterization of the intentional lived-experiences and of what they are directed at may *look*, it is a theory in which one closes one's eyes to the phenomena and does not let them themselves speak.

Heidegger then makes his decisive phenomenological appeal. Let us take a *natural perception* without all theory, without all preconceived opinion about the relation of subject to object and the like, and question this concrete perception in which we live — e.g., the perception of the window. At what does it direct itself in accord with the directional sense of its *intentio*? At what is perceiving directed according to its own apprehending-sense by which it is guided? In everyday comportment — e.g., walking around in this hall, looking around in the environing-world — I perceive wall and window. At what am I directed in this perception? At sensations? Or, if I avoid what is perceived, do I avoid representational images and guard myself from *leaping out* through these representational images and sensations into the courtyard of the university building? Heidegger's question is rhetorical: nobody actually behaves as if they were directed at sensations.

It is pure theory to say I am initially directed at sensations. Perception is directed *according to its own directional sense at the on-hand being itself*. It means precisely *this* as the on-hand and knows nothing whatever about sensations that it apprehends. This holds also when I move within a perceptual illusion. If, deceiving myself in the dark, I take a tree for a person, one may *not* say that this perception is directed at a tree but takes it for a person — that the person, however, is a mere representation, and so I am in this deception directed at a representation. On the contrary, *it is precisely the sense of the deception* that I, taking the tree for a person, apprehend what I perceive (and believe to perceive) *as an on-hand person*. In this perceptual illusion the person *himself* is given to me, and not, say, a representation of the person.

What perception is directed at according to its sense is the *perceived itself*. This is meant. What lies in this exhibition unblinded by theories? Nothing less than that the formulation of the question — how can the subjective intentional lived-experiences for their part relate to an objectively on-hand thing — is *from the ground up wrong*. I *cannot* and *may* not ask: how does the *inner* intentional lived-experience get to an outside? I cannot and may not so ask because the intentional comportment as such *comports itself toward the on-hand*. I do not first need to ask how the immanent intentional lived-experience acquires *transcendent validity*, but rather *it is precisely intentionality and nothing else in which transcendence consists*. With this, intentionality and transcendence are not yet adequately clarified, but the formulation of the question that corresponds to the proper factual content of what is questioned has been won, because it is *drawn from* the matter itself. The customary conception of intentionality misrecognizes what — in the case of perception — perceiving is directed at. With this it also misrecognizes the structure of self-directing-toward (the *intentio*). The misinterpretation lies in a *false subjectivization* of intentionality. One posits an I, a subject, and lets its so-called sphere belong intentional lived-experiences. The I is here something with a sphere into which its intentional lived-experiences are, as it were, *encapsulated*. Now it shows itself for us that intentional comportments themselves make up the *transcending*. From this it follows that intentionality may not be misinterpreted on the basis of an arbitrary concept of subject and I and of subjective sphere, and may not be taken as the occasion for a *false problem* of transcendence; rather, conversely, *on the basis of the unprejudicedly seen character of intentionality and its transcendence, the subject is first to be determined in its essence*. The inversion is total: the standard order, in which the subject is given first and intentionality is found in it, must be reversed. Because the customary separation of a subject with its immanent and an object with its transcendent sphere — because the difference of an inside and outside in general — is *constructive* and constantly gives occasion for further constructions, *we henceforth no longer speak of a subject, of a subjective sphere*, but understand the being to which intentional comportments belong as *Dasein*, and indeed in such a way that with the help of correctly understood intentional comportment we now try to characterize the *being* of Dasein, *one of its basic constitutions*, adequately. That Dasein's comportments are intentional means: the mode of being of ourselves, of Dasein, is in its essence such that this being, insofar as it is, in each case always *holds itself alongside an on-hand thing* (je schon bei einem Vorhandenen sich aufhält). The idea of a subject that has intentional lived-experiences only in its sphere and is not yet *outside* but enclosed in its capsule is a *Unbegriff* — a non-concept — that misrecognizes the ontological basic structure of the being we ourselves are. If, as remarked earlier, we briefly name the mode of being of Dasein "*Existenz*," it must be said: *Dasein exists and is never on hand like a thing*. A distinguishing character between the existing and the on hand lies precisely in *intentionality*. "Dasein exists" means, among other things: it is such that, being, it comports itself toward the on hand — toward the on hand not as something subjective. A window, a chair, in general any on-hand thing in the widest sense *never exists*, because it cannot comport itself toward the on hand in the manner of intentional self-directing. The on hand is merely also (among other things) on hand. The contrast — exists versus is on hand — is the most concise formulation of Heidegger's central ontological distinction between Dasein and non-Dasein-like beings.

Heidegger pauses to note that this is only a first run to protect the phenomenon of intentionality from the grossest misunderstandings and to bring it into view approximately. This is the presupposition for making intentionality itself into a problem, as he will try in the second part of the lecture course.

The next paragraph summarizes the two corrections. First, against the false *objectivization* of intentionality: intentionality is not an on-hand relation between on-hand things — subject and object — but a structure that *makes up the relation-character of Dasein's comportment* as such. Second, against the false *subjectivization*: the intentional structure of comportments is not something immanent to the so-called subject and first needing transcendence, but the intentional constitution of Dasein's comportments is precisely *the ontological condition of the possibility of any transcendence at all*. Transcendence, transcending, belongs to the essence of the being that exists (on the ground of transcendence) as intentional — that is, that exists in the mode of self-holding-alongside the on-hand. Intentionality is the *ratio cognoscendi* of transcendence; transcendence is the *ratio essendi* of intentionality in its various modes. The classical scholastic formula is here brilliantly redeployed: we know transcendence through intentionality, but intentionality itself *is* on the ground of transcendence.

From these two determinations it follows: intentionality is neither something objective on hand like an object nor subjective in the sense of something occurring within a so-called subject whose mode of being remains wholly undetermined. Intentionality is neither objective nor subjective in the customary sense, but *both at once in a much more originary sense*, insofar as intentionality (belonging to Dasein's existence) makes possible that this being — Dasein — comports itself *existing* toward the on hand. With the adequate exposition of intentionality, the traditional concept of subject and subjectivity becomes *questionable* — not only what psychology understands by subject but also what psychology itself, as positive science, must tacitly presuppose about the idea and constitution of the subject, and what philosophy itself has hitherto ontologically extremely deficiently determined and left in the dark. Even the *philosophical* traditional concept of the subject is inadequately determined with regard to the basic constitution of intentionality. One cannot from a concept of subject decide anything about intentionality, because *intentionality is the essential, even if not the most originary, structure of the subject itself*.

In view of the named misinterpretations, it is not self-evident what is meant by the trivial discourse: perception relates to a perceived. When today, under the influence of phenomenology, one speaks much of intentionality or introduces another word for it, it is not thereby proved that one has phenomenologically *seen* the so-designated phenomena. That the comportments — representing, judging, thinking, willing — are intentionally structured is not a proposition one can memorize and know in order to draw conclusions from it; rather, it is the *instruction* to make what is meant by it (the structure of comportments) present to oneself, and constantly to assure oneself anew at the phenomena of the legitimacy of this assertion. This is one of Heidegger's most concise formulations of phenomenological method as a doing rather than a knowing.

The misinterpretations are not accidental. They are also not exclusively and primarily grounded in a superficiality of thinking and philosophical confrontation, but have their ground in the *natural conception of things themselves* as it lies essentially in Dasein. According to this, Dasein has the tendency to apprehend all beings — whether on-hand in the sense of nature or such of the mode of being of the subject — initially in the sense of an *on-hand thing* and to understand them in the sense of on-handness. This is the basic tendency of ancient ontology, which to this day has not been overcome, because it belongs to the understanding of being and to the manner of Dasein's understanding of being. Insofar as in this conception of all the given as on-hand, intentionality as a relation *within* on-hand things is not to be found, one must apparently assign it to the subject; if it is nothing objective, then it is something subjective. The subject one again conceives in the same indeterminacy of its being as on-hand, as is shown in Descartes's *cogito sum*. So intentionality remains, whether one conceives it objectively or subjectively, something that is somehow on hand. Over against this, it is precisely *with the help of intentionality and its peculiarity that it is neither something objective nor something subjective* that one must become attentive (stutzig) and ask: must not, on the basis of this neither-objective-nor-subjective phenomenon, the being to which intentionality evidently belongs be conceived *otherwise than hitherto*? The phenomenon of intentionality is thus the lever by which the traditional ontology of the subject must be pried open.

When Kant speaks of a relation of the thing to the cognitive faculty, it now shows itself that this discourse and the formulations of the question springing from it are full of confusion. The thing does not relate to a cognitive faculty *inside* the subject; rather, the cognitive faculty itself and thereby this subject *are* in their being-constitution intentionally structured. The cognitive faculty is not the one terminal-point of the relation between a thing outside and the subject inside; rather, its essence is *self-relating* itself, in such a way that the so-self-relating intentional Dasein, as existing, in each case *already holds itself alongside the things, immediately*. *For Dasein there is no "outside"*, wherefore it is also absurd to speak of an "inside." This is one of Heidegger's most categorical formulations of the post-Cartesian ontology: the inside/outside distinction is *not* something for Dasein to overcome but something Dasein has never lived in.

When we modify Kant's multivalent discourse of perception and seek to give it independence by distinguishing perception-intention from the perceived, we are not simply correcting word-meanings and terminologies but going back to the *ontological essence* of what is meant by perception. Because perception has intentional structure, the named multivalence not only *can* but *must* arise when this structure is not seen. Kant himself must, under the *compulsion of the matter*, insofar as he treats of perception at all, make use of its intentional structure without expressly recognizing it as such. He once speaks of perception reaching somewhere, and that where it reaches actual and on-hand things are encounterable. But perception can only *have some reach* if, according to its own essence, it in general *reaches*, *reaches out toward*, that is, *directs itself at*. Representations essentially relate to a represented, point at such a one — but not so that this referring-structure would first have to be procured for them; they have it from the outset *as* re-presentations. Whether they ever rightfully give what they claim to give is a second question, the discussion of which is, however, *senseless* if the essence of the claim remains in the dark. With this Heidegger has fully positioned intentionality: the question of the legitimacy of the perceptual claim presupposes intentionality, hence intentionality cannot be derived from it.

### **c) Intentionality and Understanding of Being. Discoveredness (Perceivedness) of Beings and Disclosedness of Being**

The opening paragraph of §9c reverts to Kant. We want to hold fast to the direction of Kant's interpretation of *Wirklichkeit*, *Vorhandenheit*, and only more clearly and adequately characterize the *horizon* out of which and in which he carries out the clarification. What have we gained so far with the provisional clarification of the intentional structure of perception? On the structure of *position as such*, Heidegger says, we come back in the discussion of the fourth thesis (the being of the copula). We grant Kant that he wishes to equate *Vorhandenheit* neither with perceiving (the *intentio*) nor with the perceived (the *intentum*), even though he does not name this distinction. There remains accordingly only the possibility of interpreting Kant's equating of *Wirklichkeit* and perception in the sense that perception here means *Wahrgenommenheit*, perceivedness. It proved questionable whether the *Wirklichkeit* of an actuality (the *Vorhandenheit* of an on-hand thing) may be identified with its perceivedness. On the other hand, however, we ourselves had to consider that in the perceivedness (being-perceived) of the perceived and thus discovered actual, evidently its *Wirklichkeit* must be co-unveiled, and in a certain sense in the perceivedness of a perceived on-hand thing its *Vorhandensein* lies enclosed — that through the analysis of perceivedness of the perceived, one must somehow be able to advance to the on-handness of the on-hand. But with this it is already said that perceivedness is *not to be equated with* on-handness; rather, the former is only a necessary, though not sufficient, *condition of access* to the latter. This connection requires that we attempt a characterization of perceivedness as such.

Heidegger then poses the structural question. How does this character of perceivedness of a perceived relate to what we have so far said about intentional constitution as such? Perceivedness is the perceivedness *of* a perceived. How does it belong to the perceived? Can we, through the analysis of the perceivedness of the actual, advance to the *sense of the actuality* of this actual? Orienting ourselves on the intentionality of perception, we must say: the perceivedness belonging to a perceived evidently falls into the *intentum*, into that at which perception directs itself. We must first follow up further what the *intentum* of perception is. We have already said: it lies in the intentional directional-sense of perceiving to *mean the perceived as on-hand in itself*. The intentional directional sense of perceiving itself — whether it deceives itself or not — aims at the on-hand as on-hand. Perceptively I am directed at the window over there as this particular tool-thing. With this on-hand thing — in the broadest sense — it has a determinate *Bewandtnis* (involvement). It serves for the lighting and at the same time the protection of the hall. From its serviceability (Dienlichkeit), from what it serves, its constitution is prefigured — that is, all that belongs to its determinate *Realität* in the Kantian sense, to its *Sachheit*. We can in the everyday way naïvely describe this on-hand thing perceptively, make pre-scientific but also positive-scientific assertions about this object. The window is open, doesn't close tightly, sits well in the wall; the frame is of such-and-such a color, has such-and-such extension. What we so find on this on-hand thing are on the one hand determinations that belong to it as a *tool* (Gebrauchsding, *Zeug*), and on the other hand also determinations such as hardness, weight, extension that belong to the window not qua window but as pure *material thing*. We can cover over the tool-characters that first leap into view in natural dealings with such things as windows and that make up its tool-character, and consider the window only as on-hand thing. In both cases, however — whether we consider and describe the window as tool or as pure natural thing — we already understand in a certain way what *tool* and *thing* mean. We understand in natural dealings with tools (instruments, measuring-tools, vehicles) something like *Zeughaftigkeit* — tool-being — and we understand in coming upon material things something like *Dinglichkeit* — thinghood.

But we are *looking* for the perceivedness of the perceived. Among all these thing-determinations that make up the tool-character of the perceived, but also among the determinations belonging to the general thing-character of the on-hand, *we do not find its perceivedness, which it nevertheless has*. We say: the on-hand is the perceived. Therefore *perceivedness is also no "real predicate"* (in Kant's sense). How does this belong to the on-hand? The on-hand does *not* undergo any change by my perceiving it. It experiences no increase and no decrease of what it is as this on-hand. It is not damaged and rendered unusable by perceiving. On the contrary, it lies in the very sense of the perceiving grasping to *discover* the perceived so that it *shows itself in itself*. So perceivedness is nothing *objective* at the object. But perhaps, one will conclude, it is something *subjective* — not belonging to the perceived (the *intentum*) but to perceiving (the *intentio*)?

Yet we have already, in the analysis of intentionality, become wary of the right of this customary distinction between subject and object, subjective and objective. Perceiving as intentional falls just as little into a subjective sphere as it precisely *transcends* this (if one even wants to speak of such). Perceivedness belongs perhaps to the *intentional comportment of Dasein* — that is, it is *neither subjective nor objective*. Nevertheless we must again and again hold fast: the perceived being, the on-hand, *is perceived*, has the character of perceivedness. A remarkable and *enigmatic formation* is this perceivedness, belonging in a certain sense to the object (the perceived) and yet nothing objective, belonging to Dasein and its intentional existence and yet nothing subjective. The methodological response is striking: again and again the methodological maxim of phenomenology must be impressed: *not to flee the enigmatic character of the phenomena prematurely* or to remove it by the violent stroke of a wild theory, but *to heighten the enigmatic character*. Only thus does it become graspable and conceptually conceivable — that is, understandable and so concrete that *from the enigmatic matter itself the instructions for the resolution of the phenomenon spring forth*. With regard to perceivedness — but also, as will still show itself, with regard to corresponding other characters — the problem arises: how can something in a certain way *belong to the on-hand* without being on-hand, and at the same time as this belong to Dasein without meaning something subjective? This problem Heidegger will not yet solve here, but only sharpen, in order to show in the second part that the clarification of the possibility of such an enigmatic phenomenon *lies in the essence of time*. The path forward is announced: temporality is the horizon for understanding even such structural enigmas as perceivedness.

One thing is clear: perceivedness of an on-hand thing is *not on-hand at this thing itself*, but belongs to Dasein — which does not mean: belonging to the subject and its immanent sphere. Perceivedness belongs to perceiving intentional comportment. This makes possible the *encountering* of the on-hand *at itself*. Perceiving *discovers* the on-hand and lets it be encountered in the mode of a determinate discovering. Perception *takes from the on-hand its concealedness* (Verdecktheit) and *releases it* (gibt es frei) so that it can show itself *in itself*. This is the sense of every natural looking-around and every natural orienting-oneself about something — and indeed because in perceiving itself, in accord with its intentional sense, *this mode of uncovering* lies. The formula — *freigebendes Begegnenlassen* — is Heidegger's gloss on what genuine perception accomplishes.

With the reference that perception relates to a perceived, perception is *not yet adequately delimited against mere representing*, *Vergegenwärtigen* (making-present). This too relates to something — to a being — in a determinate manner, and can even, like perception, relate to an on-hand thing. So I can now make present to myself the train station in Marburg. In doing so I do *not* relate to a *representation* and do not mean a represented, but the station as one on hand *there*. Nevertheless, in this pure making-present the on hand is apprehended and given *differently* than in immediate perception. These essential differences of intentionality and of the *intentum* do not concern us here.

Perceiving is *freigebendes Begegnenlassen* of the on-hand. Transcending is a *discovering* (Entdecken). *Dasein exists as discovering.* The discoveredness (Entdecktheit) of the on-hand is what makes possible its *release* as an encountering one. *Wahrgenommenheit* — the specific release of a being in perceiving — is a mode of *Entdecktheit* in general. Discoveredness is also the determination of the release of something in *production* or in *judgment about* — that is, perceivedness is a *species* of the *genus* discoveredness, which has further species (such as the discoveredness operative in fabrication or in predication).

Heidegger then turns to the structural condition of any discovery. What belongs to a discovering of beings — in our case to the perceiving discovering of the on-hand? The mode of discovering and the mode of discoveredness of the on-hand must evidently be *determined by* the being to be discovered and its mode of being. I cannot perceive geometrical relations in the sense of natural, sensible perception. But how is the mode of discovering supposed to be, as it were, *normed and prefigured* by the being to be discovered and its mode of being, if not in such a way that the being itself is *already* discovered, so that the mode of grasping can direct itself by it? On the other hand, this discovering is supposed to *measure itself by* the being to be discovered. The mode of *possible discoverability* of the on-hand in perceiving must already be *prefigured in perceiving itself* — that is, the perceiving discovery of the on-hand must *in advance* already understand something like *Vorhandenheit*. In the *intentio* of perceiving, *understanding of Vorhandenheit must in advance already lie*. Is this merely an a priori demand that we must place, since otherwise the perceiving discovery of the on-hand would remain unintelligible? Or can it be *shown* that in the intentionality of perception (in the perceiving discovery) something like *understanding of Vorhandenheit* lies? This can not only be shown — we have already shown it; more cautiously, we have already made use of this understanding of Vorhandenheit belonging to perception's intentionality without having expressly characterized this structure. The argument is *transcendental* in a precise sense: the possibility of perceiving the on-hand presupposes a prior understanding of *Vorhandenheit* as such — that is, of the being-mode of the on-hand.

The next paragraph reconstructs the argument. With the first characterization of the *intentum* — what perception directs itself at — against subjectivistic misinterpretations (that perceiving directs itself only at the subjective, i.e., at sensations), it was a matter of showing that perception is directed at the on-hand itself. On this occasion we said that we need, to see this, only to *question the apprehending tendency* lying in perception itself, its directional sense. According to its directional sense, perceiving *intends the on-hand in its Vorhandenheit*. It belongs to the directional sense — that is, *the intentio is aligned toward the discovery of the on-hand in its Vorhandenheit*. In this *itself*, an *understanding of Vorhandenheit* already lies — even if only *pre-conceptually* (vorbegrifflich). In this understanding, what *Vorhandenheit* means is *unveiled*, *opened up* — we say *erschlossen*, disclosed. We speak of the *Erschlossenheit* given in the understanding of *Vorhandenheit*. This understanding of *Vorhandenheit* lies as pre-conceptual in advance (vorgängig) in the *intentio* of perceiving discovery as such. This "in advance" does not mean: in order to perceive the on-hand, to discover it, I would have to make clear to myself in advance the *sense* of *Vorhandenheit*. The *Vorgängigkeit* (priority) of the understanding of *Vorhandenheit* belonging to perceiving discovery is not in advance in the order of clock-time we measure. Rather it says, conversely: this understanding of *Vorhandenheit* (in the Kantian sense, of *Wirklichkeit*) is so prior — that is, so belonging to the essence of perceiving comportment — that I do not first have to *expressly carry it out*; rather, it lies (as we will see) in the basic constitution of Dasein itself that, existing, it *already* understands the mode of being of the on-hand toward which it comports itself existing — *quite apart* from how far this on-hand is discovered and whether it is adequately or appropriately discovered or not. To the intentionality of perception belong not only *intentio* and *intentum* but, furthermore, the *understanding of the mode of being of what is intended in the intentum*. The threefold structure is now complete: intentio, intentum, *understanding of being*.

How this prior pre-conceptual understanding of *Vorhandenheit* (*Wirklichkeit*) lies in the discovery of the on-hand — what this "lying" says and how it is possible — will occupy us later. For now it is only a matter of seeing that *the discovering comportment toward the on-hand holds itself in an understanding of Vorhandenheit*, and that to this comportment — that is, to Dasein's existence — *the disclosedness of Vorhandenheit* belongs. This disclosedness is the *condition of the possibility of the discoverability of the on-hand*. Discoverability — that is, perceivability of the on-hand — *presupposes the disclosedness of Vorhandenheit*. Perceivedness is *grounded with respect to its possibility in the understanding of Vorhandenheit*. Only when we bring back the perceivedness of the perceived to its foundations in this way — that is, *analyze the understanding of Vorhandenheit itself*, belonging essentially to the full intentionality of perception — do we place ourselves in the position of clarifying the sense of the so-understood *Vorhandenheit* (in Kantian terms, the sense of *Dasein* and existence). This is the structural argument for the necessity of fundamental ontology: *what Kant wanted to clarify presupposes the analysis of the Dasein that understands Vorhandenheit*.

This understanding of being, then, is evidently what Kant — without seeing it clearly — *recurs to* when he says: *Dasein*, *Wirklichkeit* \= perception. Without our giving an answer to the question of how *Wirklichkeit* is to be interpreted, we must keep present to ourselves that *against* Kant's interpretation (*Wirklichkeit* \= perception), a *wealth of structures and structural moments* offers itself of what Kant fundamentally recurs to. First we come upon intentionality. To this belong not only *intentio* and *intentum* but equally originarily a *mode of discoveredness* of the *intentum* discovered in the *intentio*. But to the being perceived in perception belongs not only that it is *discovered*, the discoveredness of beings, but also that *the mode of being* of the discovered being is *understood*, that is, *disclosed*. We therefore distinguish not only terminologically but for *substantive reasons* between the *Entdecktheit of beings* and the *Erschlossenheit of their being*. *Beings can only be discovered* — be it on the path of perception or some other mode of access — *if the being of beings is already disclosed*, if I *understand* it. Only then can I ask whether it is actual or not, and can I in some way *set about establishing* the actuality of beings. This terminological distinction — *Entdecktheit* for beings, *Erschlossenheit* for being — corresponds to the ontological difference itself and runs throughout the lecture course.

It must succeed in *exhibiting more precisely the connection between the discoveredness of beings and the disclosedness of their being*, and showing how the disclosedness (unveiledness) of being *founds*, that is, *gives the ground, the foundation* for the possibility of the discoveredness of beings. Otherwise put: it must succeed in *conceptually grasping the distinction between discoveredness and disclosedness* as possible and necessary, but likewise in conceiving the *possible unity* of both. In this lies at the same time the possibility of grasping the distinction between *the being discovered in discoveredness* and *the being disclosed in disclosedness* — that is, of fixing the distinction between being and beings, the *ontological difference*. *We come in the pursuit of Kant's problem to the question of the ontological difference.* Only on the path of the solution of this fundamental ontological problem will it be possible *not only positively to ground* Kant's thesis ("being is not a real predicate") but at the same time *positively to supplement* it through a radical interpretation of being as such as *Vorhandenheit* (*Wirklichkeit*, *Dasein*, existence). The trajectory from Kant's thesis to the ontological difference — the first of the four basic problems announced in §4 — is now complete.

We see now that the possibility of bringing out the ontological difference evidently *hangs together with the necessity of investigating intentionality* — that is, the mode of access to beings — where it is not said that the mode of access to *every* being is perception in the Kantian sense.

Heidegger then characterizes Kant's position in the history of philosophy. Kant does not drive the elucidation of *Wirklichkeit*, existence, to the center when he equates *Wirklichkeit* with perception. He remains *standing at the outermost edge of the problem-field*, and so that this edge even disappears for him in indistinctness. Nevertheless, the *direction* he strikes — through the recourse to the *subject in the widest sense* — is *the only possible and right one*. It is the direction of interpretation of being, *Wirklichkeit*, *Dasein*, existence that did not first appear in modern philosophy since Descartes through its express orientation of philosophical problematic toward the subject. The direction toward the subject — or rather toward what is basically meant by it, *our Dasein* — is also taken by the not-yet-subjectivistic-in-the-modern-sense ontological questioning of antiquity, that of Plato and Aristotle. But this does not mean one may interpret the philosophical basic tendency of Plato and Aristotle in a Kantian sense (as the Marburg School did years ago). The Greeks go, in the attempt to clarify being, in the same direction as Kant when they go back to the *logos*. The *logos* has the peculiarity of *making manifest*, *discovering* or *disclosing* something — which the Greeks no more than modern philosophy distinguished. The *logos* is, as basic comportment of the *psychē*, an *alētheuein* — the manifestation that is peculiar to the *psychē* (in the widest sense) or to the *nous* — terms one understands badly when one thoughtlessly translates them with "soul" and "spirit" and orients oneself by the corresponding modern concepts. The *psychē* speaks with itself, says Plato, about being; it speaks with itself through being, otherness, sameness, motion, rest, and the like — that is, it *already understands with itself* such things as being, *Wirklichkeit*, existence. The *logos psychēs* is the *horizon* into which every procedure of clarifying such things as being and *Wirklichkeit* enters. All philosophy — however it conceives the "subject" and places it at the center of philosophical investigation — *recurs in the clarification of ontological basic phenomena to soul, spirit, consciousness, subject, I*. Ancient as well as medieval ontology is not, as common ignorance supposes, a purely objective ontology *with the exclusion* of consciousness; rather, the peculiarity is precisely that *consciousness and I are taken as being in the same sense as the objective*. This is shown in the fact that ancient philosophy orients its ontology toward the *logos*, and one could with a certain right say that ancient ontology is a *logic of being*. This is correct insofar as the *logos* is the *phenomenon that is to clarify what being means*. But the "logic" of being does not mean that ontological problems would be reduced to logical ones in the sense of school-logic. The recourse to the I, to the soul, to consciousness, to spirit, and to Dasein is *necessary on substantive grounds*. Heidegger's reading of the history of philosophy here is striking: ancient ontology is not less subject-oriented than modern; it just operates with a different conception of the subject (the *logos psychēs*) than modern philosophy has worked out.

The next paragraph reformulates this unanimity through another formulation. Being, *Wirklichkeit*, existence belong to the most general concepts that the I, as it were, *brings along*. Therefore one called and calls these concepts *innate ideas* (*ideae innatae*). They lie from the outset in human Dasein. Dasein, on the basis of its being-constitution, brings with it a *seeing* — *idein* — an *understanding of being*, *Wirklichkeit*, *Existenz*. Leibniz says in many places — though still much more roughly and ambiguously than Kant — that we grasp only in *reflection on ourselves* what being, substance, identity, duration, change, cause, effect are. The doctrine of the innateness of ideas more or less clearly dominates the whole of philosophy. It is, however, more a way out and a *removal* of the problem than a solution. One falls back too easily on a being and a property of this being — *Eingeborenheit* (innateness) — which one does not further clarify.

Innateness, Heidegger continues, may not be understood (however unclearly conceived) in the physiological-biological sense; rather, it is meant to say that *being and existence are understood earlier than beings*. But this does *not* mean that being, existence, and *Wirklichkeit* are what the individual *first* grasps in its biological development — that children first understand what existence is. Rather, this multivalent expression "innateness" merely indicates the *Früher*, the prior, *the Apriori* — which since Descartes up to Hegel has been identified with the *subjective*. The problem of clarifying being can be drawn out of this blind alley — that is, can first properly be posed as a problem — only when one asks: *What does this innateness mean? How is it possible on the basis of Dasein's being-constitution? How is it to be delimited?* Innateness is no physiological-biological fact, but its sense lies in the direction that *being, existence, is earlier than beings*. It must be conceived in *philosophical-ontological sense*. One may therefore also not believe that these concepts and principles are innate because *all human beings recognize the validity of these propositions*. The agreement of human beings on the validity of the principle of non-contradiction is merely a *sign* of innateness, not the *ground*. The going-back to general agreement and consensus is not yet a *philosophical grounding* of the logical or ontological axioms. The classical doctrine of innate ideas is critiqued from a transcendental-phenomenological angle: it correctly noted that some structures are a priori but failed to ask how this a priority is possible on the ground of Dasein's being.

Heidegger then anticipates the next chapter. We will see in the phenomenological consideration of the second thesis — to every being belong a What and a manner-of-being — that the same horizon opens there: the attempt, from the going-back to the Dasein of the human being, to clarify the being-concepts. It will indeed also show itself that this regress, precisely regarding this problem, is *not so explicitly formulated* in ancient and medieval ontology as in Kant. *Factically*, however, it is nevertheless present.

The closing paragraphs of §9 (and thereby of Chapter One) gather the results.

It has become clear in several ways: the critical discussion of Kant's thesis leads to the necessity of an *explicit ontology of Dasein*. For only on the basis of bringing out the ontological basic constitution of Dasein do we place ourselves in the position of adequately understanding the phenomenon that is assigned to the idea of being — *the understanding of being* — which underlies all comportment toward beings and guides it. Only when we understand the ontological basic constitution of Dasein can we make clear to ourselves how in it *Verständnis von Sein* (understanding of being) is possible. But it has also become clear that the ontology of Dasein is the *latent goal* and the constant more or less clear *demand* of the entire development of Western philosophy. This, however, can be seen and demonstrated only when this demand is itself *expressly posed* and *fulfilled in its basic features*. The discussion of Kant's thesis led in particular to a *fundamental ontological problem*: the question of the distinction between being and beings — the problem of the *ontological difference*.

In the discussion of Kant's thesis, Heidegger notes, we have touched on problems at every step without expressly noting them as such. Thus, to discuss Kant's thesis completely, it would have been necessary not only to analyze the equation of *Dasein* (*Wirklichkeit*) with absolute position but correspondingly also the equation of *being as such* with *position as such* — that is, to show that position, *Setzung*, too has an *intentional structure*. We will come back to this in connection with the discussion of the fourth thesis, where we treat being in the sense of the *"is" of the copula*, which Kant interprets as *respectus logicus*, i.e., as the positing of being as such. The being that Kant takes as one with position as such he understands as the "is" that is posited as the *combination of subject and predicate* in the proposition. For its analysis, the demonstration of the structure of the *positing character* of the proposition is necessary. The fourth thesis is thus already announced.

The preliminary clarification of intentionality further led us to the *difference in the being-constitution of objectively existing beings and that of the subjective or of Dasein, which exists*. Evidently this distinction of the being that we ourselves are and the being that we are not — formally, in Fichtean parlance, of the I and the Not-I — is no accidental distinction but one that must somehow impose itself even on common consciousness and around which philosophy has occupied itself from its beginnings. We will discuss this in the third thesis, so that the connection of the first thesis with the fourth and the third becomes already clear. The interlinking of all four theses is thus signaled.

In the clarification of the content of Kant's thesis we started from the concept of *Realität*, *Sachheit*, from which existence as nonreal character is to be distinguished. Nevertheless, it is to be considered that *Realität*, too, is just as little something *real*, as *Existenz* is something *existing* — which is expressed in Kant by the fact that *Realität*, like *Existenz*, is for him a *category*. *Realität* is an ontological determination that accrues to every being — whether actual or only possible — insofar as every being is *something*, has a *Sachgehalt*. It is necessary not only to exclude *existence* as something non-real from the real determinations of a thing but equally to determine the *ontological sense of Realität as such* and to ask how the connection between *Realität* and *Existenz* is to be conceived, and how its possibility can be demonstrated. This is a problem that lies, as it were, *hidden* in Kant's thesis. It is nothing other than the content of the *second thesis*, to whose discussion we now proceed. To be kept in view is that the four theses *hang together among themselves*. The factual content of the one problem includes that of the others. The four theses formulate only from outside, and still covered over, the *systematic unity* of the basic ontological problems toward which we are feeling our way through the preparatory discussion of the theses. With this transition, Chapter One closes, having shown that Kant's thesis on being can be radicalized only by the recourse to the analysis of Dasein's understanding of being — and that this recourse leads inevitably to the ontological difference, which is the first of the four basic problems announced in §4. The systematic unity Heidegger announced at the outset is now visible, even if it has been formulated only "from outside" and still in covered form.

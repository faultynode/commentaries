# Heidegger, "Hegel's Concept of Experience" (GA 5: 115–208): A Commentary

The commentary follows the Gesamtausgabe pagination of *Holzwege* (GA 5), given in parentheses at the head of each unit; the marginal numbers of the first edition are not used. After a short opening the essay reprints the whole of Hegel's Introduction to the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (sixteen sections, plus one sentence Hegel sets apart), then works through those sixteen sections (Abschnitte) in order. The commentary treats every paragraph of Heidegger's text as a unit, and treats Hegel's reprinted sections as units in the place where they stand, keeping them to Hegel's own argument so that Heidegger's re-reading can be measured against it. Heidegger's text has no internal headings; the H3 headings over his treatment of each of Hegel's sections are added here and name the topic of each. Cross-references use GA pages. Marginal notes from Heidegger's copy of the first edition (1950) are folded in where they bear on the sense.

A fixed vocabulary is kept throughout. *Anwesen* is presencing, *Anwesenheit* presence, *das Seiende* beings or what is, *Seiendheit* beingness. *Vorstellen* is representing; *Darstellung* is exposition (Hegel's "presentation" of appearing knowledge), so that "presentation" can be reserved for Heidegger's *Präsentation*. *Umkehrung* is reversal, *Zutat* our contribution, *Zusehen* looking-on, *Prüfung* testing, *Maßstab* standard, *Gestalt* shape, *Bildung* formation, *Gewalt* force. *Subjektität* is subjectity, Heidegger's term for the being of the subject, deliberately distinct from subjectivity. *Parusie* is kept as parousia.

Several misreadings beset this text. First, "experience" here is not a mode of cognition, still less empirical observation or the method of the *Phenomenology*; Heidegger's thesis is that Hegel uses the word to name the being of beings in the age of subjectity. Second, "parousia" is not a borrowed eschatology or a mystical claim of union; it is Heidegger's Greek name for presence (*parousia* as a cognate of *ousia*), applied to the way the Hegelian absolute is already present "with us." Third, Heidegger's word "being" must be kept apart from Hegel's: for Hegel "being" (*Sein*) names the still-untrue immediacy of objects, whereas Heidegger applies the word precisely to what Hegel calls actuality, appearing, and absoluteness (he flags this himself at 154–55). Fourth, "Dasein" in this essay means Hegel's existence or determinate presence (*Dasein des Geistes*), not the being-there of *Being and Time*. Fifth, "natural consciousness" is not sense-perception or common sense; it is the stance that represents beings without attending to their being, and it survives in every shape of spirit, absolute metaphysics and modern technology included. Sixth, "skepticism" is not doubt; Heidegger takes *skepsis* back to seeing, a looking that has already caught sight of being. Seventh, "our contribution" and "reversal" are not subjective additions or constructions laid on the phenomena; they are the letting-appear that the phenomena require. Eighth, the essay is not an endorsement of Hegel. It reads the absolute's "will to be with us" as the completion of metaphysics as will, and a marginal note on the title says the whole is "thought, unspoken, from Ereignis" (the event of appropriation). Heidegger's own idiom (presencing, unconcealment, the twofold of being and beings) runs underneath the exposition and surfaces at set points.

## Hegel's Concept of Experience

(115) Heidegger opens with the title Hegel set over the work in 1807: "Science of the *Experience* of Consciousness," with the middle word in bold type. Experience names what "the phenomenology" is. The essay's question is therefore not how Hegel's method works but what the word "experience" says when Hegel stresses it this way. The answer is to come from "the piece" (*das Stück*) that begins the work after the Preface. Heidegger avoids the name "Introduction" from the first line, and he will justify the refusal at the end (205): there is no introduction to the *Phenomenology* because there can be none. The marginal note on the title, "thought, unspoken, from Ereignis," tells the reader that the commentary on Hegel is also the work of another thinking that does not speak in its own name here. The typographic centrality of "experience" also anticipates a thesis Heidegger states near the end (197): experience mediates consciousness and science, as the word mediates the title.

(115–16) Hegel begins from a "natural representation": before philosophy turns to the thing itself, the actual cognition of what truly is, it must first come to an understanding about cognition, conceived either as an instrument for seizing the absolute or as a medium through which we see it. Two worries seem justified: that we might choose the wrong kind of cognition, and that a faculty of limited kind and scope might grasp clouds of error instead of the heaven of truth. The worry hardens into the conviction that the whole enterprise is absurd. An instrument reshapes what it is applied to; a medium gives us the thing as refracted, not as it is in itself. Subtracting the instrument's contribution only returns us to where we started. If the instrument merely brought the absolute nearer without altering it, like a lime-twig catching a bird, the absolute would mock the ruse, "if it were not, and did not will to be, with us in and for itself already." If we subtract the law of refraction, nothing remains, since cognition is not the refraction but the ray itself by which truth touches us. Hegel's target is the critical program running from Locke to Kant and Reinhold. His argument is that the instrument model defeats itself, not that critique is idle. The subordinate clause about the absolute being already with us will become the axis of Heidegger's entire reading (130–31, 204).

(117) Hegel then turns the distrust round. If fear of error makes us distrust a science that simply sets to work, why not distrust this distrust, and suspect that the fear of erring is already the error? The fear presupposes much as true without testing it: that cognition is an instrument or medium, that we differ from our cognition, and above all that the absolute stands on one side and cognition on the other, separate from it yet still something real. Cognition outside the absolute would then be outside truth and yet true. The supposed fear of error reveals itself as fear of truth. Hegel does not refute critique from a higher principle; he exposes the uncritical premise on which critique rests.

(117) Hegel draws the consequence from a single thesis: the absolute alone is true, or the true alone is absolute. One can try to escape by distinguishing a cognition that fails to know the absolute but is still capable of "other truth." Hegel calls this a murky distinction between absolute truth and truth of some other kind. He adds that "the absolute," "cognition," and the rest are words that presuppose a meaning which has yet to be won. Both points are seeds: the identity of the true and the absolute is what Heidegger will unfold as "absolvence" (136), and the demand that science first give these words their meaning returns at 139.

(117–19) Hegel next considers simply rejecting such representations as contingent and arbitrary, even as fraud, since they pretend that the meaning of "absolute," "cognition," "objective," and "subjective" is generally known and so spare themselves the real work of giving the concept. Science might even spare itself the trouble of noticing them, since they are an empty appearance of knowledge that vanishes when science comes on the scene. Then comes the turn: in coming on the scene, science is itself an appearance; its emergence is not yet science carried out and unfolded in its truth. Science must free itself from this semblance by turning against it. It cannot merely assure us that the other knowledge is nothing, since the untrue knowledge assures us just as dryly that science is nothing. Nor can it appeal to a presentiment of itself within untrue knowledge, since that would again appeal to a mere fact of being and to its own worst mode of existing. "For this reason the exposition of appearing knowledge is to be undertaken here." The paragraph thus derives the necessity of the whole *Phenomenology* from the problem of science's own appearing.

(119) Hegel then says that because the exposition has only appearing knowledge for its object, it seems not to be free science moving in its own shape. From this standpoint it can be taken as the way of natural consciousness pressing toward true knowledge, or as the way of the soul passing through the series of its shapes as stations set out by its nature, purifying itself into spirit through the complete experience of itself. Hegel's verbs are hedged: the exposition "seems," it "can be taken." Heidegger will build his reading of this passage on these hedges (143), insisting that the pilgrim's-progress reading is how the work looks to natural consciousness, not what it is.

(119–20) Hegel now states that natural consciousness will prove to be only the concept of knowledge, not real knowledge. Because it takes itself immediately to be real knowledge, the way has a negative significance for it: what is the realization of the concept counts for it as loss of itself. The way can therefore be called the way of doubt, or more properly of despair (*Verzweiflung*). It is not ordinary doubt, a shaking of this or that supposed truth followed by a return to it, but conscious insight into the untruth of appearing knowledge. This self-accomplishing skepticism is not the resolve to accept nothing on authority and to follow only one's own conviction; holding opinions on one's own conviction rather than another's changes nothing in their content and differs only by its vanity. The series of shapes is "the detailed history of the formation of consciousness itself to science." Skepticism aimed at the whole range of appearing consciousness first makes spirit capable of testing what truth is, by bringing about despair over natural representations, thoughts, and opinions, whether one's own or others'. Hegel's targets are Descartes' methodical doubt and the Enlightenment's self-thinking. Despair (*Ver-zweiflung*) intensifies doubt (*Zweifel*) to the point where consciousness no longer returns to its starting point.

(120–21) Hegel says next that the completeness of the forms of unreal consciousness will follow from the necessity of the progression itself. The exposition of untrue consciousness is not merely negative. That one-sided view belongs to natural consciousness and is itself one of the shapes that will appear on the way: the skepticism that sees in every result only pure nothing and abstracts from the fact that this nothing is determinately the nothing of that from which it results. Taken as such, the nothing has a content. Abstract skepticism can only wait for something new to throw into the same empty abyss; determinate negation immediately yields a new form, so that the progression through the complete series of shapes arises of itself. Here Hegel introduces determinate negation as the engine of the work. Heidegger will read the phrase "the nothing of that from which it comes" as a sign that the result comes from the origin, not the destination (157).

(121–22) Hegel then says the goal is set for knowledge as necessarily as the series of the progression: it lies where knowledge no longer needs to go beyond itself, where it finds itself and concept corresponds to object, object to concept. The progression is therefore unstoppable and finds satisfaction at no earlier station. What is limited to natural life cannot go beyond its immediate existence by itself; it is driven beyond by another, and this being torn out is its death. Consciousness, however, is for itself its own concept, and is therefore immediately the going-beyond of the limited, and, since the limited belongs to it, beyond itself. With the individual the beyond is posited for it. Consciousness suffers this force at its own hands, spoiling its own limited satisfaction. Fear of truth may try to hold on to what is threatened: by thoughtless inertia, which thought disturbs; by a sentimentality that assures us everything is good in its kind, which reason contradicts precisely because it is a kind; or by a vanity that dissolves every thought and finds only "the dry I," a satisfaction that must be left to itself because it flees the universal. The sentence "consciousness is for itself its concept" becomes Heidegger's "first proposition on consciousness" (161).

(122–23) Hegel turns to method. If the exposition is conceived as science's conduct toward appearing knowledge, an investigation and testing of the reality of cognition, it seems to need a presupposition as its standard. Testing consists in applying an accepted standard and deciding, from equality or inequality with it, whether the tested is right or wrong; the standard is taken as the essence or the in-itself. But here, where science is only now emerging, neither science nor anything else has justified itself as the essence or the in-itself, and without that no testing seems possible. The passage restates the opening critical demand at a higher level: the problem is no longer the instrument but the criterion.

(123) Hegel promises that this contradiction and its removal will emerge more definitely if we first recall the abstract determinations of knowledge and truth as they occur in consciousness. Consciousness distinguishes something from itself and at the same time relates to it; something is "for it." The determinate side of this relating, the being of something for a consciousness, is knowledge. From this being-for-another we distinguish being-in-itself: what is related to knowledge is also distinguished from it and posited as being outside the relation. This side is called truth. What these determinations really amount to does not concern us yet, since appearing knowledge is our object and its determinations are taken as they immediately offer themselves. Hegel deliberately borrows the received definitions (truth as the in-itself, knowledge as being-for) in order to make them undo themselves.

(123) Hegel sets out the difficulty. If we investigate the truth of knowledge, we seem to investigate what knowledge is in itself. But in the investigation knowledge is our object, for us; its in-itself would be its being-for-us, and what we asserted as its essence would be only our knowledge of it. The standard would fall within us, and what was compared with it would have no need to acknowledge it. The criterion problem thus recurs as the problem of the investigator's own standpoint.

(123–24) Hegel removes the separation by pointing to the nature of the object investigated. Consciousness provides its standard in itself (*an ihm selbst*), and the investigation becomes a comparison of consciousness with itself, since the distinction just made falls within it. Within consciousness something is for another; at the same time this other is for it not only in relation but also outside it, in itself: the moment of truth. What consciousness declares within itself to be the in-itself is the standard it sets up for measuring its knowledge. If we call knowledge the concept and the true the object, testing means seeing whether the concept corresponds to the object; if we call the in-itself of the object the concept and the object as it is for another the object, testing means seeing whether the object corresponds to its concept. The two are the same. The essential point is that both moments fall within the knowledge investigated, so that we need bring no standards of our own; by leaving out our notions and thoughts we consider the matter as it is in and for itself. This is the "second proposition" on consciousness for Heidegger (169), and the maxim of "pure looking-on" begins here.

(124–25) Hegel adds that we are also spared the work of comparing and testing, since consciousness tests itself and pure looking-on is all that remains for us. Consciousness is consciousness of the object and of itself, of what is true for it and of its knowledge of that; since both are for it, consciousness is their comparison. It may seem that consciousness cannot get behind the object as it is in itself and so cannot test its knowledge against it. But the very fact that it knows an object already contains the difference between the in-itself and knowledge. If the two fail to correspond, consciousness seems obliged to change its knowledge; but in changing its knowledge the object itself changes, for the object essentially belonged to that knowledge. What was the in-itself for it proves to have been in-itself only for it. The test is a test not only of knowledge but of its standard. This is Hegel's decisive break with the correspondence model: the criterion is historical, revised by the very act that applies it. Heidegger's "third proposition" (173) comes from this paragraph.

(125–26) Hegel names the result: "This dialectical movement which consciousness exercises on itself, on its knowledge as well as on its object, insofar as the new true object arises for it out of this, is properly what is called experience." Hegel singles out one moment. Consciousness knows something; this object is the in-itself, but it is also the in-itself for consciousness, and this makes the true ambiguous. Consciousness now has two objects, the first in-itself and the being-for-it of this in-itself. The second looks at first like mere reflection of consciousness into itself, a representing of its knowledge of the first. But the first object changes; it becomes an in-itself only for consciousness, and so the being-for-it of the in-itself becomes the true, the essence, the new object. This new object contains the nothingness of the first; it is the experience made concerning it. The word "dialectical" appears here and nowhere else in the piece, and Heidegger will make much of that (183).

(126–27) Hegel admits that this account of experience seems to disagree with the usual one. Ordinarily we discover the untruth of our first concept on another object that we happen upon externally, so that only the pure apprehension of what is in and for itself falls to us. On Hegel's account the new object arises through a reversal of consciousness itself. "This way of considering the matter is our contribution," by which the series of experiences is raised to a scientific progression, and it is not for the consciousness we observe. It is the same circumstance already met in relation to skepticism: each result must be grasped as the nothing of what it results from. The first object sinks to a knowledge of it, the in-itself becomes the being-for-consciousness of the in-itself, and this is the new object, with which a new shape of consciousness appears. This necessity, the origination of the new object, goes on "behind the back" of consciousness; it brings into the movement a moment of being-in-itself or being-for-us. The content is for consciousness; we grasp only its formal aspect, its pure arising. For consciousness what has arisen is only an object; for us it is also movement and becoming. The paragraph introduces the two notions Heidegger will put at the center of the essay: the reversal and "our contribution."

(127) Hegel then sets a single sentence apart: "Through this necessity the way to science is itself already science, and by its content therefore science of the experience of consciousness." The italicized words reassemble the work's original title. Heidegger will read this as the summary of the two preceding sections on experience and as the point where the whole piece gathers into the title (193).

(127–28) Hegel's closing section ends the piece. The experience consciousness makes of itself can by its concept comprise nothing less than the whole system of consciousness, the entire realm of the truth of spirit, so that its moments appear not as abstract moments but as they are for consciousness, that is, as shapes of consciousness. Driving itself toward its true existence, consciousness will reach a point where it sheds the semblance of being burdened with something alien that is only for it and as an other, where appearance becomes equal to essence and its exposition coincides with the science of spirit proper. Finally, grasping this essence of its own, it will indicate the nature of absolute knowledge itself. The "science of spirit proper" will be read by Heidegger as the *Science of Logic*, and the coincidence of appearance and essence as the completed parousia (196–97).

### Knowing as Instrument or Medium, and the Parousia of the Absolute

(128) Heidegger begins by saying that Hegel's opening section names the matter of philosophy, and he names it in Aristotle's words: philosophy "beholds what is present as present, and so beholds what already holds sway in it of itself" (*Metaphysics* Γ 1, 1003a21, on beings as beings and what belongs to them as such). He glosses "holding sway" (*hyparchein*) as coming forth into unconcealment, so that philosophy beholds the present in its presencing. Contemplation (*theoria*) looks at what is present only with regard to its look; it contains no brooding profundity but is "the sobering of all knowing." Hegel says the same thing in the language of his thinking when he calls philosophy "the actual cognition of what truly is." In between, what truly is has shown itself as the actual, whose actuality is spirit, and the essence of spirit lies in self-consciousness. The whole essay is staked on this opening equation. Hegel's phrase is not a new definition of philosophy but the Aristotelian definition after a historical transformation of what "being" means. The Aristotle citation returns at 193–94 to frame the close.

(128–29) Heidegger cites Hegel's lectures on the history of modern philosophy: with Descartes "we come for the first time to the philosophy of the new world"; this philosophy knows that it comes independently from reason and that self-consciousness is an essential moment of the true; "here we are at home," and like a sailor after long wandering on a stormy sea we can cry "Land!"; in this new period the principle is thinking that proceeds from itself. The quotation supplies the governing image of the next pages: modern philosophy as the occupation and surveying of a "land," the self-certainty of thinking.

(129) Heidegger expands the image. Thinking seeks an absolute foundation (*fundamentum absolutum*) in the unshakable certainty of what it thinks. The land in which philosophy has since made itself at home is the unconditioned self-certainty of knowing. It is conquered and surveyed only step by step, and philosophy takes full possession of it only when the absolute foundation is thought as the absolute itself. For Hegel the absolute is spirit: what is present to itself in the certainty of unconditioned self-knowing. The actual cognition of beings as beings is now the absolute cognition of the absolute in its absoluteness. Heidegger thereby places Hegel at the end of a single history that begins with Descartes; Hegel does not leave the Cartesian land but completes its survey. This is the frame of "The Age of the World Picture" (GA 5: 75–113), where representation and certainty are developed at length.

(129–30) The same modern philosophy, living in the land of self-consciousness, demands of itself, "in keeping with the land's climate," that it first become certain of its principle. It wants to come to an understanding beforehand about the knowing with which it knows absolutely. Knowing then appears, unnoticed, as a means whose proper use knowing must worry about. Descartes takes care of choosing, among the ways of representing, the only one fit for absolute knowing; Kant takes care of measuring the chosen knowing by its nature and limits. But once knowing is treated as a means for seizing the absolute, the conviction must arise that every means, being relative, is unfit for the absolute. Whether the means is an instrument we handle or a medium we undergo, the project becomes absurd. Heidegger's point is that the critical demand is not an external accident but grows out of the land itself: certainty requires that certainty first be secured, and this requirement turns knowing into a tool. Hegel's opening thus diagnoses a tendency built into modern subjectity, with Descartes and Kant as its two stages.

(130) One might remedy the defect, that the means does not mediate, by testing the means and separating what it alters in the absolute from what it leaves unchanged. But if we subtract the alteration, we no longer use the means, and it mediates nothing of the unaltered absolute either. More fundamentally, the test does not know what it is doing. To measure knowing by its adequacy to the absolute, it must already have known the absolute as absolute; otherwise all critical limit-drawing falls into the void. The test also cares more for the instrument than for knowing the absolute. Why all this critical activity if knowing wants from the start to wriggle out of its immediate relation to the absolute in order to settle the business of critique first? Heidegger then corrects Hegel's image: the absolute does not mock the critical effort either, for to mock it the absolute would have to share its presupposition, that knowing is a means and that the absolute lies far enough off to need capturing. "But then the absolute would not be the absolute." The critique of critique is circular in the precise sense that every criterion of adequacy presupposes access to what it is meant to secure access to. Heidegger's correction of the lime-twig image removes even the residual distance that mockery implies.

(130–31) Only in passing, hidden in a subordinate clause, Hegel says the decisive thing: the absolute is already with us in and for itself and wills to be with us. Heidegger names this being-with-us *parousia*, the Greek word for presence that, coming from *para* (beside, with) and *ousia*, says "presencing with." It is in itself the way the light of truth, the absolute itself, shines on us. Knowing the absolute stands in this ray, reflects it, and so is in essence the ray itself, not a medium the ray must pass through. The first step for such knowing is simply to receive and take up the absolute in its being-with-us. If philosophy takes seriously what it is as knowing of the absolute, it is already actual knowing. Hegel's opening section looks as if it tries to satisfy natural representation's critical demand for a test of knowing; in truth it points to the absolute's parousia and so directs us explicitly into the relation in which we already stand. Does Hegel thereby abandon the critical achievements of modern philosophy and fall back into arbitrary assertion? By no means; he only prepares the test. Its first step is to give up the ordinary representation of knowing as a means. If knowing cannot be a means, testing cannot consist in assessing its fitness to mediate; perhaps it suffices to look and see what knowing is. Not only what is tested but testing itself shows another essence. The critical question is not dismissed but transformed, and the transformed test will turn out to be consciousness itself (173). "Parousia" will also carry a second sense, the second coming of Christ, which Heidegger lets resonate at the end (202–03).

### Fear of Error as Fear of Truth: Philosophy Become Science

(131–33) This section touches the core of the critique to which science subjects all previous philosophical critique of knowing. From here on Hegel no longer says "philosophy" but "science." The change of name records an event: modern philosophy has completed its essence by taking full possession of the land it first entered, the self-certainty of representing with respect to itself and what it represents. To possess this land fully means to know self-certainty in its unconditioned essence and to be in this knowing as knowing pure and simple. (A marginal note glosses this "be" as "that is, know.") Philosophy is now unconditioned knowing within the knowing of self-certainty, and so "the science." The name does not mean that philosophy has taken the existing sciences as its model. Within absolute metaphysics it draws its sense from the self-certainty of the subject, which is now what truly lies before, the *subiectum* or *hypokeimenon* that philosophy has always had to know as what is present. Philosophy has become science because it remains philosophy: its task is still to behold beings as beings. Since Leibniz, however, beings appear such that every being as being is a thinking thing (*res cogitans*) and in that sense subject. This is due not to Leibniz's opinion but to the being of beings. The subject is not the egoistic subjective; it essentially unfolds in the representing relation to the object, and as this relation it is already the representing relation to itself. Representing presents the object by re-presenting it to the subject, and in this re-presentation the subject presents itself as such. Presentation is a mode of presence (*parousia*), and so the being of beings of the subject's kind. Self-certainty, as self-knowing conditioned only by itself and therefore unconditioned, is the beingness (*ousia*) of the subject. Heidegger names the subject-being of the subject, that is, of the subject-object relation, "subjectity" (*Subjektität*). It consists in unconditioned self-knowing. The subject so to speak deals only with its own constitution, knowing (Heidegger plays on *beschaffen*, constituted, and *sich zu schaffen machen*, to be busy with). Subjectity as absolute self-certainty is "the science." Three moves fix the essay's ontology here. The word "science" is ontological, not methodological. The Greek *hypokeimenon* (what underlies) becomes the Latin *subiectum* and then the self-representing ego, so that subjectity is a transformation of *ousia*, not its abandonment. And representation (*Repräsentation*) is a mode of presence, which allows Heidegger to read modern metaphysics in the Greek vocabulary of presencing throughout. The coinage "subjectity" separates the structural being of the subject from any psychological subjectivity; Heidegger develops it in the Nietzsche volumes ("Metaphysics as History of Being," GA 6.2).

(133) Unconditioned self-knowing, as the subjectity of the subject, is the absoluteness of the absolute. Philosophy is absolute knowing; it is science because it wills the will of the absolute, that is, wills the absolute in its absoluteness, and so wills to behold beings as beings, and so wills its own essence. In "philosophy is the science" the "is" does not attach a predicate; it says that philosophy exists as absolute knowing only by belonging to the absoluteness of the absolute and carrying it out in its own way. Philosophy is not science by striving to make its procedure exact and its results compelling, which would assimilate it to scientific research, something beneath it in essence and rank. The motif of will enters here and will grow until it names the essence of experience itself (190, 203–04). The polemic against "scientific" philosophy is aimed at Husserl's ideal of rigorous science as much as at neo-Kantianism.

(134) Philosophy is the science by staying with its work as absolute knowing. Hegel chooses his words: he calls the critical analysis's worries "such scruples" (*dergleichen Bedenklichkeiten*). He does not say science may go to work heedlessly and throw testing to the wind. Absolute knowing is more heedful (*bedenkender*) than the scrupulous critique could ever be. Critique fears error, but it could err only within a relation it has already, without scruple, assumed to be true: knowing taken as a means is itself the going-astray. The apparently testing fear of error is itself the error. How so? Heidegger hears in *Bedenklichkeit* (scrupulousness) and *bedenken* (to consider, to think about) the difference between a fearful hedging and genuine thoughtfulness. The critical attitude is accused not of too much rigor but of too little thought.

(134) As soon as knowing is taken as a means, whether instrument or medium (and Heidegger asks in parenthesis how long and why it has been so taken), it counts as something occurring by itself between the absolute and the knowers. Knowing stands apart from the absolute and from us who handle it, and the absolute and the knowers end up wholly separated on two sides. But what is an absolute that stands on one side, or on any side at all? In no case the absolute. The argument is ontological, not epistemological: "absolute" means unbound (*ab-solutum*), and an absolute with something beside it is a contradiction in terms. The parenthetical question points beyond Hegel to the history in which knowing became an instrument.

(134–35) At the same time the dissecting critique takes knowing as something real, even the first and authoritative reality. It appeals to something true, which for it means something certain, whose certainty is supposed to hold apart from the unconditioned self-certainty of everything certain. This created being, the *ego cogito* as the certain being (*ens certum*) supposedly certain without the absolute, must then be secured after the fact, as already in Descartes, through the back door of a proof of God. Critique wants to reach something absolute while doing without the absolute. It even seems to honor the absolute by setting it out of reach and so, apparently, as high as possible. In fact it underestimates the absolute and drags it down into the narrowness of its scruples and means. It tries to drive the absolute out of its parousia, as if absoluteness could be introduced later. The supposedly critical fear of hasty error is the uncritical evasion of the truth that already abides. If, by contrast, science accepts its own essence expressly, it has already tested itself; part of this test is knowing that science, as absolute knowing, stands in the parousia of the absolute. All this rests on what the next section says. Heidegger reads Hegel's "fear of truth" as a precise diagnosis of the Cartesian structure: the cogito's certainty is first secured without God and then needs God as guarantor, which shows that its independence was illusory. Kant's unknowable thing-in-itself is the "elevation" that is really a demotion. The phrase "the truth that already abides" anticipates the end, where there is no introduction because we are already within.

### The Absolute Alone Is True: Certainty and Absolvence

(135) This section says: the absolute alone is true; the true alone is absolute. Hegel posits these sentences without grounds. They cannot be grounded, because no grounding reaches their ground; grounding, as the wish to ground, constantly moves away from it. They are ungrounded but not arbitrary; they posit what first grounds. In them speaks the will of the absolute that wills to be with us already in and for itself. Heidegger distinguishes the groundless from the arbitrary, a distinction he works out in *The Principle of Reason* (GA 10): the first principles of a metaphysics are not conclusions but the opening in which conclusions become possible.

(135–36) Since modern philosophy set foot on its land, truth has held sway as certainty. The true is what is known in unconditioned self-knowing. Formerly truth counted as the agreement of representing with beings, a character of representing; as certainty, truth is representing itself insofar as it delivers itself to itself and makes sure of itself as re-presentation. Knownness that has secured its knowing before and with itself has withdrawn from every isolated representing of objects; it no longer clings to objects in order to have the true in clinging to them. Knowing detaches itself (*absolvere*) from the relation to objects, from finding sufficient security in one-sided object-representation. The detachment does not abolish object-representation; it only stops it from merely hanging on its object. Heidegger names three moments. *Absolvence* is self-certainty's detaching from the objective relation. *Absolving* is the completion of that detachment in every respect. *Absolution* is self-certainty's acquittal of itself, its release into the freedom of its essence from one-sided bondage to objects. Their unity is the absoluteness of the absolute; all three have the character of representation, and in them the parousia of the absolute holds sway. The true, as unconditioned self-certainty, is alone the absolute; the absoluteness of self-representing is alone the true. The etymology of *ab-solvere* (to loosen from) is the method: Heidegger reads "absolute" as a process of loosening, not a static highest being. The triad also explains why the absolute "alone" is true. Once truth is certainty, any truth bound to an object is a truth not yet released, so the unreleased truth cannot be "other truth" alongside the absolute; it is the absolute not yet completed. The religious overtone of "absolution" is deliberate and prepares the theological reading at the end.

(136–37) Yet any explanation leaves these sentences empty and even deepens the misunderstanding, for what they name is the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, which exists only in its exposition. That is why Hegel states them bluntly, despite every appearance of arbitrariness, to ready us for what science as absolute knowing wills. Science wills only what the absolute wills, and the absolute wills to be with us already. This now means: so far as we are knowers, there is with us only the absolutely true. Whoever still says that besides the absolute knowing philosophy claims without testing there is other truth does not know what he is saying; as soon as he names something true he has already represented the absolute. Distinguishing an absolute truth from some other truth means drifting in a murky distinction and making murkiness the principle of critique and the measure for judging science. Only science can win the meaning of the words "absolute," "knowing," "true," "objective," "subjective." This requires that science reach the absolute's parousia with its first step; otherwise it would not be science. It then violates its own essence to engage at all with scruples that stay outside and below the domain of the true. But having freed itself from them, science remains suspected of asserting itself absolutely without showing its credentials, and so of violating most gravely the demand of certainty it claims to fulfill. It must therefore bring itself before the only tribunal that can decide what its test is, and that tribunal can only be the parousia of the absolute. Once again everything depends on making the absoluteness of the absolute clear. Two claims stand in tension and drive the next sections. Absolute knowing cannot be tested from outside. Yet it is answerable to the very standard of certainty it claims to fulfill, so it must show itself. The "tribunal" is not an external court but presence itself, which prepares the reading of science's "appearing" at 139.

### Science's Coming on the Scene: Appearance and Semblance

(137–38) This section points to what the will at work in the parousia demands of us as knowers. Ordinary critique of philosophical knowing takes it, without looking, as a means; this testifies that it neither knows absolute knowing nor can carry it out. The incapacity to perceive and accept the parousia before all else is incapacity for science. Over-eager concern with scruples and testing evades the labor of science, which is to engage in such acceptance. The absolute does not give us the step into the parousia in our sleep. The step is hard not because we must reach the parousia from somewhere outside, but because within the parousia, and out of it, we must bring our relation to it forth and before it. The effort of science is therefore not the knower's toil on himself but arises from science's relation to the parousia. The difficulty is located precisely: we are already inside, and the task is to make the relation we already have explicit. This is the structure of the hermeneutic circle in *Being and Time* (§§ 2, 32), now transposed into the vocabulary of Hegelian presence.

(138–39) The absoluteness of the absolute, absolution absolving itself absolvently, is the labor of unconditioned self-certainty's self-conceiving. It is the toil of pain that endures the diremption as which the in-finite relation fulfills the essence of the absolute. Heidegger quotes an early note of Hegel's: "A darned stocking is better than a torn one; not so with self-consciousness." When Hegel speaks of the labor of the concept he means not the scholar's mental sweat but the absolute's struggling out of itself into the absoluteness of its self-conceiving from unconditioned self-certainty. Yet this labor of the absolute is compatible with the effortlessness of the parousia, since the absolute simply belongs as such to the relation of presencing with us. The effort of science answers to the absolute's effort to bring its presence about and itself to appearance in it. Critical busyness, by contrast, slips past the hardest part of that effort: to consider that the knowing to be tested is absolute knowing, that is, philosophy. It resembles someone who wants to represent an oak without noticing that it is a tree. One might be tempted to call it fraud, since it pretends to have the essential concepts when everything depends on first giving them. Critical worry is not with the matter it constantly talks about; it is an "empty appearance of knowledge." Why should science not spare itself the effort of disputing with it, and simply come on the scene? Here, in the middle of the section, Hegel sets the decisive "But." The labor of the concept is ontologized: it is the absolute's own self-diremption, as the Preface says ("the seriousness, the pain, the patience and labor of the negative"). The stocking aphorism makes diremption the life of self-consciousness, not a defect to be mended. The oak-and-tree image identifies the critics' error as ignoring the genus, here the being, of what they examine, a first form of the ontic blindness that Heidegger names at 175.

(139–40) "But in coming on the scene science is itself an appearance." Science emerges like any other knowledge. It can assure us that it is absolute knowing before which all else must vanish, but by puffing itself up in this way it makes itself common with the empty appearances of knowledge, which can likewise only assure us that they exist; one assurance is as dry as the other and never sets the living sap of actual knowing flowing. Science could instead present itself as the knowledge that untrue knowledge unknowingly seeks, the true dimly sensed within the untrue. But that is again mere assurance, and it would appeal to a mode of occurrence ill befitting absolute knowing; to remain the merely sensed true is far from being the true in and for itself. Heidegger follows Hegel's two blocked exits. The first is dogmatic self-assertion. The second is the Romantic and Jacobian appeal to a "presentiment" (*Ahnung*) of the absolute within ordinary consciousness. Both treat science's being as a mere fact. "Dry" anticipates the "dry I" at 162–63.

(140) What then of science's coming on the scene? It must appear if it emerges, but the question arises what appearing is, in which science alone can appear. Heidegger lists three senses. First, appearing means to emerge alongside others, asserting oneself. Second, it means to occur while pointing to something else that has not yet come forth. Third, it means to be the forecast (*Vorschein*) of something that has not appeared or never will. None of these fits science, since in none of them can it unfold itself as itself and set itself up completely. Nor can science arrive at a single stroke as absolute knowing; it must bring itself forth into its truth and bring that truth forth along with it. In every phase in which science comes forth it does so as absolute, and absolutely. The appearing proper to it can only be that it presents itself in its self-production and so sets itself up as appearing knowledge. Science can emerge only by carrying out the exposition of appearing knowledge, and only in doing so can it emerge what that appearing is in which science truly appears as itself. The triad maps the common senses of appearance: co-occurrence, sign, and Kantian appearance of a thing-in-itself that never appears. Heidegger eliminates them in order to reach a fourth sense, appearing as self-showing presencing, the Greek *phainesthai* of *Being and Time* § 7. The exposition is not science's preface but its mode of being.

(140–41) In its appearing, science presents itself in the fullness of its essence. Empty appearance does not vanish by being rejected or left aside; merely appearing knowledge is not to vanish at all but to enter into its appearing. There it appears as untrue, that is, not-yet-true knowledge within the truth of absolute knowing. The exposition must turn against the semblance of knowledge, but in the reconciling way that makes even mere semblance light up with the pure shining of the ray. If semblance is only rejected as false, it has not even been perceived in its shining. Nor does science's unfolding consist in merely overcoming semblance, for then the true would remain in servitude to the untrue. The appearing of science is necessary because of that shining which even semblance needs in order to be mere semblance. Heidegger exploits the family *Schein* (shine, semblance), *scheinen* (to shine, to seem), and *Erscheinung* (appearance): semblance is possible only through a prior shining. This is his version of the Hegelian claim that the untrue is a moment of the true. It also guards against reading "turning against semblance" as refutation; mere refutation would keep the true dependent on what it refutes.

(141–42) Hegel's sentence "But in coming on the scene science is itself an appearance" is said ambiguously, and with a lofty intention. Science is not only an appearance in the sense in which the empty appearing of untrue knowledge also shows itself. It is already appearance in the unique sense that, as absolute knowing, it is the ray in which the absolute, the light of truth, shines upon us. Appearing from this shining of the ray means presencing in the full radiance of self-presenting re-presentation. Appearing is presencing proper, the parousia of the absolute. In accordance with its absoluteness the absolute is with us of itself. In willing to be with us it is present (Heidegger hyphenates *an-wesend*, "essencing toward"); bringing itself toward itself in this way it is for itself. Only for the sake of the will of the parousia is the exposition of appearing knowledge necessary, and it must remain turned toward the absolute's will. The exposition is itself a willing: not a wishing or striving but the doing itself, insofar as it gathers itself into its essence. The moment we recognize this necessity we must reflect on what this exposition is, so as to know how it is, and so be able to be in its way, that is, to carry it out. Hegel's apparent concession becomes a claim. The ambiguity of "appearance" is the ambiguity of the ray itself, which shines on the untrue as well. The hyphenation *an-wesend* and *an sich… für sich* turns Hegel's in-itself and for-itself into stages of presencing. Defining will as "the doing that gathers itself into its essence" introduces the concept of will that the essay will identify as the hidden essence of experience and of being in modern metaphysics.

### The Exposition Mistaken for an Itinerary

(142) This section begins the reflection. Science must come completely to appearance in exhibiting appearing knowledge and through the course of that exhibition; its emergence consists in showing step by step what it is. On what stage does this happen, if not before the eyes of natural representing? Natural representing follows appearing knowledge through its many appearances and watches how, station by station, it sheds semblance and finally presents itself as true knowledge. On this view the exposition escorts natural representing through the forecourt of knowledge to the door of absolute knowing; it is the way of natural consciousness to science, and since the semblance of the untrue falls away on the way, it is a purification of the soul into spirit, an "itinerary of the mind into God." Heidegger states the received reading at its most persuasive, in order to dismantle it. The phrase *itinerarium mentis in Deum* (Bonaventure's title) marks this reading as a Christian-Neoplatonic ascent schema imposed on Hegel.

(142) What could be more welcome to natural consciousness and more useful for philosophy than a travel account of this path? Since it runs along the appearances, it is a way of experience; empirical method, following the given, deserves preference over mere construction and deduction. The exposition, phenomenology, keeps to the phenomena, goes the way of experience, and guides natural representing step by step into the domain of philosophical science. This extends the received view to its methodological version, where "experience" means sticking to the given and "phenomenology" means description. Heidegger is also glancing at readings of Hegel through Husserlian phenomenology, and he refutes this version outright at 182.

(143–44) That is indeed how the exposition looks to natural representing, which stays referred to whatever it believes it has before it. But can relative opinion ever see absolute knowing? No. What natural consciousness takes for "merely appearing knowledge, which is to lead to the true," is a mere semblance. Yet philosophy to this day takes the *Phenomenology* for an itinerary leading everyday consciousness to philosophical knowledge. What the work so taken seems to be, it is not in its essence. This semblance is not accidental: it follows from the work's essence, pushes in front of it, and covers it. Natural representing, having crept into philosophy, takes appearing knowledge as "merely" appearing, with something non-appearing hidden behind it. But the exposition is not the exposition of merely appearing knowledge as opposed to the true knowledge to which it leads; it is the exposition of appearing knowledge in its appearing. The "only" in Hegel says not that the exposition is not yet science, but that it is not yet science in every respect. The appearing of appearing knowledge is the truth of knowledge, and the exposition is science from the moment it begins. Heidegger then reads the text closely: Hegel says the exposition "seems" not to be science and "can be taken" as the way of natural consciousness; he speaks neither of merely appearing knowledge, nor of an exposition that only develops into science, nor of an itinerary as the only way to understand it. The argument turns on the placement of "only." "Only appearing knowledge" posits a hidden reality behind appearances, which is the two-world schema. "The exposition has only appearing knowledge as its object" means the exposition is restricted to appearing, and appearing is the truth. The thesis that the semblance arises from the essence and conceals it is Heidegger's general model of the history of interpretation.

(144) The exposition does not lead natural representing around a museum of the shapes of consciousness and then release it through a special door into absolute knowing. Rather, with its first step, if not before it, the exposition takes leave of natural consciousness as something by its nature unable to follow it. The exposition is not a path natural consciousness walks; nor is it a path moving gradually away from natural consciousness until it somewhere flows into absolute knowing. And yet it is a way, constantly moving back and forth in a between that holds sway between natural consciousness and science. A paradox stands: a way that is neither travelled by natural consciousness nor a road away from it. The "between" is the clue, and it returns in the analysis of dialectic as the *dia* of a conversation (183, 192).

### Natural Consciousness and Real Knowing: The Way of Despair

(144) This section begins to characterize the way that the exposition is, and to clarify the between in which it moves of necessity as it brings appearing knowledge to shine. It opens with a distinction that reappears from section to section in varied respects, while how these respects belong together, and what grounds their unity, stays hidden. The first task is to see the distinction between natural consciousness and real knowing. Heidegger thereby announces a single distinction that will take several guises (natural and real, ontic and ontological, for it and for us, object and objectness), and he flags the unity of these guises as a question Hegel leaves unasked. His answer, given at 175–77, is the ontological difference.

(144–45) Hegel uses "consciousness" and "knowledge" for the same thing, and each explains the other. Heidegger reads the German *Bewußt-sein* as "being in the state of knowing": knowing delivers and presents, and so determines the "-being" as being-known. In this state there are together the known, the knower, and the knower's representing. Knowing (*Wissen*) means *vidi*, "I have seen": I have taken the view of something, an insight into it. The perfect tense of having seen is the present tense of knowing, in whose presence the seen is present. Seeing is here thought as having-before-oneself in representing (*Vor-stellen*, setting-before), whatever is presented, sensed or thought, willed or felt. Representing sights in advance; it is a beholding of the sight, an *idea*, but in the sense of *perceptio*: it takes up something present as such, goes through it, inspects and secures it. Representing rules in all modes of consciousness; it is neither mere intuition nor conceptual judgment. It gathers in advance into a having-seen (Heidegger cites the Augustinian-Cartesian *co-agitare*, behind *cogitare*). In this gathering what is sighted becomes present. Consciousness (*conscientia*) is the gathering into presence of the kind that belongs to the re-presented. Representing brings the look, the image, into presence: it is imagination (*Einbildung*, literally "bringing-in of the image"). To be conscious means to be present by being delivered from representedness. The rest of the essay presupposes this phenomenology of consciousness, built from three etymologies. *Wissen* comes from the root of Latin *videre*, so that knowing is a perfect of seeing. *Cogitare* is *co-agitare*, a gathering. *Einbildung* is the bringing-in of the image. Together they make consciousness a mode of presence rather than an inner container, which is how Heidegger can apply Greek presence-categories to Descartes and Hegel without anachronism. The reference to imagination recalls the *Kantbook* (GA 3), where the transcendental imagination is the root of representation.

(145–46) "Consciousness" names a being, and the "-being" must not stay an empty sound: it means presencing in the manner of the gathering of what is sighted. By long habit the word also means the being itself that is in this way. The other name for this being is "subject": what already lies before everywhere, the present that accompanies all consciousness, the representer itself in its representing, which sets what it represents toward itself and so back. Representing presents by way of re-presentation. The being of this which comes before everything represented, the being of the subject as the subject-object relation reflected into itself, is subjectity: presence in the manner of re-presentation. To be present in representedness means to present oneself as knowledge in knowing, to appear in the immediate sense of coming forth into unconcealment: presencing, existence (*Dasein*). Consciousness as such is in itself what appears. The immediate existence of consciousness is appearing, in such a way that appearing forms the site of appearing, its stage, through itself. Now the title "exposition of appearing knowledge" becomes clearer. It does not mean exhibiting something that merely seems; it means representing knowledge, which immediately is nothing but what appears, in its appearing. With appearing knowledge the exposition represents existing consciousness as existing, as actual, real knowing. The essential gain is that "appearing" is no longer opposed to being: for consciousness, to be is to appear. That makes the *Phenomenology* an ontology rather than a psychology or a theory of error. "Dasein" here is Hegel's existence, glossed as presencing; it is not *Being and Time*'s Dasein. "Site" (*Stätte*) is a technical word that returns: the place where the absolute appears is formed by its appearing (191, 207).

(146) The actuality of this actual, the subjectity of the subject, is appearing itself. But this being, like all being of all beings in all metaphysics, enters representation only as beings present themselves as beings (*on hēi on*). The *on* is now the being-as-being as perceived (*ens qua ens perceptum*), present in presentation through the *cogitationes* that make up *conscientia*. What is to be exhibited now is the subject as subject, the appearing as appearing. The exposition of appearing knowledge is the ontology of actual consciousness as actual. Heidegger compresses the history of metaphysics into a formula: Aristotle's "being as being" persists, but the "as" has become perception (Leibniz and Berkeley's *percipi* are in view). The genus "ontology" is retained, which is why Heidegger can call Hegel's work "first philosophy" later (195).

(146) The exposition is a way, but not a stretch from pre-philosophical representing to philosophy. Philosophy itself is the way, as the course of exhibitive representing. Its movement must be determined by what the exposition follows: appearing consciousness as such, real knowing, which is the truth of natural knowing. This sharpens the paradox of 144: the way is not travelled toward philosophy but is philosophy's own movement. "Follow" anticipates the thesis that the exposition follows the appearing that comes toward it (152, 164).

(147) Hegel can therefore begin characterizing the exposition only with a sentence that sets off real knowing as such: "Natural consciousness will prove to be only the concept of knowledge, or not real knowledge." The choice to begin with "real knowing" confirms the previous paragraph: the exposition takes its measure from the truth of natural knowing, not from natural knowing.

(147) Natural knowing is set against real knowing, so the natural is not the real and the real not natural. One might think them the same: the natural is what comes from nature, and nature is what is without effort, beings themselves. How can this not be the real, the actual, beings themselves? Hegel uses the distinction for knowing, for consciousness, which is in itself what appears. In the manner of appearing, the subject is present, and with it, in relation to it, the object. The appearing subject is present knowledge, natural consciousness. Yet the exposition will show it to be "only the concept of knowledge." One might suppose Hegel turns nature into a mere concept and that nature must be restored to its rights as the actual. But Hegel does not deny that nature is actual; he shows that it cannot be actuality, the being of beings. Nor does he say nature is only a concept; he says natural consciousness is only the concept of knowledge, and what that means is determined by what "real knowledge" means. Heidegger heads off a materialist or naturalist objection to Hegel (Feuerbach and the Left Hegelians are in view). The ontological difference again does the work: nature is a being, actual, but not actuality. Being is not a being, and so "natural" knowledge, which keeps to beings, is not "real" knowledge, which knows realness.

(147–48) The real is what truly is. Since Descartes the true, the *ens verum*, is the *ens certum*: what knows itself in certainty, present in knowing. But the *ens certum* is truly known only when known as *ens*, when its *esse* is expressly represented and the real is known in its reality. Real knowing is the knowing that everywhere represents beings in their beingness (reality), the appearing in its appearing; knowing the reality of the real is why it is called real knowing. If natural knowing proves not to be real, this means it represents beings everywhere not as such but merely clings to them. When it seeks beings in their truth, it always tries to explain beings by beings. The beings in which consciousness is absorbed (*aufgeht*) are what alone rises up (*aufgeht*) for it, and so count as the natural. Because such representing is absorbed in, and stays surrounded by, the beings that rise up for it, this knowing is natural. Yet even this knowing can be absorbed in beings and take everything for beings only if, without knowing it, it already has the beingness of beings in view in a general way. Natural representing is necessarily this general representing of beingness, but without expressly knowing it. In representing beings it does not heed being and must nevertheless heed it, since without the light of being it could not even lose itself among beings. Natural consciousness is thus only the general and indeterminate representing of beingness, "only the concept of knowledge," not the knowledge that becomes certain of the reality of the real. Two moves carry the argument. Heidegger reads "real knowledge" as knowledge of reality (the *-ität* of realness), turning a Hegelian adjective into the ontological question. And he defines natural consciousness by the double pun on *aufgehen* (to be absorbed in, to rise up as *physis*), which makes "natural" mean: absorbed in what emerges. Natural consciousness is then structurally the pre-ontological understanding of being from *Being and Time* § 4, an implicit understanding of being that makes dealing with beings possible. The phrase "the light of being" is Heidegger's own and points to the clearing.

(148–49) Hegel uses "concept" here in the sense of traditional logic, which fixes natural thinking in its forms and rules: the concept is the representation of something in general, and "only concept" says that this representing does not even grasp its represented as such. But it belongs to natural consciousness not only to be absorbed in represented beings but also to take them for the only true, and so its knowledge for real knowledge. Hence Hegel continues: since it takes itself immediately for real knowledge, this way "has negative significance for it." Wherever real knowing brings the being of beings to light, natural knowing ignores it, since its own true is contested. Natural knowing keeps to its own; everything it meets falls under the statement: it is and remains mine, and as this meant (*Ge-meinte*) it is the being. When Hegel calls representing "opining" (*Meinen*), he hears in the word several senses together: meaning as directly aiming at something; opining in the old sense of *minne* (love), trusting acceptance of what is given; and opining as keeping and asserting something as one's own. This opining is the basic constitution of all representing in which natural consciousness moves, so Hegel can say it is stuck "in the system of opinion." The triple etymology of *meinen* (to mean, Old High German *minna*, and the play on *mein*, mine) is familiar from the sense-certainty chapter, where what is "meant" (*gemeint*) is what is "mine" and cannot be said. Heidegger takes it as the constitution of natural consciousness as such: aiming, trusting, appropriating. "System of opinion" is not a pejorative for doxa but a structural description of an ontic stance.

(149–50) What Hegel calls natural consciousness is not sensory consciousness. Natural knowing lives in all shapes of spirit and lives each in its own way, including absolute knowing, which happens as absolute metaphysics, visible only to a few thinkers. This metaphysics did not collapse before nineteenth- and twentieth-century positivism; rather, the modern technological world in its unconditioned claim is nothing other than natural consciousness, which in the manner of its opining carries out the unconditioned, self-securing producibility of all beings in the relentless objectification of everything. Yet absolute metaphysics is not the cause of what establishes itself as the confirmation of what happens in the essence of technology. The natural in consciousness lies not in the sensory but in what immediately rises up for consciousness and so immediately enters it. In this way natural consciousness also takes up everything nonsensory, the non-sensuous of the rational and logical as well as the supersensory of the spiritual. This is the decisive extension of the concept and one of the essay's political-historical claims. "Natural consciousness" is not a stage that philosophy leaves behind; it persists within absolute metaphysics, and modern technology is natural consciousness made unconditioned. The claim that Hegelianism did not collapse but became world (as technology) anticipates "The Question Concerning Technology" (1953) and the "Overcoming Metaphysics" notes. Heidegger also denies a causal history of ideas: metaphysics is not the cause of technology; both belong to the same sending of being.

(150) When the appearing of appearing knowledge comes forth, everything in knowing depends on this shining. Natural consciousness finds itself placed in another light, without ever being able to see this light as such. In it natural knowing loses its true, since this true now shows itself as the not-yet-true; for the appearing of the appearing, which natural consciousness itself is, is its own truth and reality. The exposition of appearing realizes what was "only the concept of knowledge." It brings the real forth into its reality and makes reality rule within the real. The appearing is neither eliminated nor separated from real knowing; it is preserved in what is truly its own, its reality and truth. Natural consciousness and real knowing are indeed the same (*das Selbe*), insofar as the former, as the not-yet-true, belongs necessarily to the latter as its truth; but for that very reason they are not equal (*das Gleiche*). The distinction between the same and the equal is Heidegger's constant device (see "Identity and Difference"): sameness is belonging-together in difference, equality is indifferent identity. "Realizes" is taken literally, making real, which is why reality is said to come to rule in the real. Natural consciousness cannot see the light it now stands in, a precise anticipation of the "behind its back" at 189.

(150–51) Seen from natural consciousness, the exposition keeps shaking what counts for it as true, and one could call this doubting. But the way of mere doubt, as Descartes' *Meditations* show, is of another kind. It calls many modes of representing into question, but only to remain in the starting position, which learns to doubt and is itself never doubted. The way of doubt only shows that doubting has already secured itself in a certainty that counts as the absolute foundation, whose absoluteness is neither doubted, nor questioned, nor even named in its essence. Hegel's way differs because he knows that absolute knowing can only be if it begins, in whatever way, with absoluteness. That is why natural consciousness first appears in its own landscape for his thinking, whereas Descartes, though entering the land of modern philosophy, the subject as *ego cogito*, does not really see the landscape at all. The image of land and landscape completes the opening metaphor: Descartes discovered the continent, Hegel mapped it. Heidegger's criticism is that methodical doubt presupposes its result, since the doubter's certainty is untouched. Hegel's "path of despair" differs because it starts from absoluteness rather than arriving at it.

(151–52) In the absolute exposition there is no return for natural consciousness to its truth. The way is "the conscious insight into the untruth of appearing knowledge, for which what is most real is what is in truth only the unrealized concept." Natural consciousness finally loses its former truth on the way, but never loses itself; it settles into the new truth in its old manner. From the standpoint of the science of appearing knowledge, the way is for natural consciousness, without its knowing, the way of despair. But natural consciousness never despairs. Despair is the business of the exposition, of absolute knowing. And it too does not despair of itself, but of natural consciousness, insofar as this never wants to realize the mere concept of knowledge it always is, yet never ceases to arrogate to itself the truth of knowledge and pass itself off as the sole measure of knowing. The more completely the exposition traverses the way of despair, the sooner science completes its own appearing. Heidegger relocates despair. The usual reading has natural consciousness despairing on its pilgrimage; Heidegger assigns despair to the philosopher, who despairs of natural consciousness's refusal to learn. That natural consciousness "settles into the new truth in its old manner" restates 149–50: it persists in every shape.

(152–53) The exposition brings itself wholly into the constancy of despair; it is the accomplishing of despair, "self-accomplishing skepticism." Heidegger recovers the original sense of the word: *skepsis* means seeing, looking on, inspecting, examining what and how beings are as beings. Skepsis so understood pursues the being of beings by seeing; its looking has seen the being of beings in advance, and from this sight it views the matter itself. "The thinkers are by nature the skeptics about beings, out of the skepsis into being." Skepsis moves and stands in the light of the ray by which the absoluteness of the absolute, already with us, touches us; its having-seen is the *vidi* that has the reality of the real in view. If reality is the appearing of appearing knowledge, appearing comes to be exhibited only as the exposition follows it and moves as its following. In this movement the appearing of what appears comes toward the exposition, and in this coming, what appears, insofar as it takes itself for the real, goes away. This unified coming and going is the movement that consciousness itself is. Consciousness is in the unity of natural and real knowing, setting itself toward itself according to its knowledge of itself and appearing in this setting; so it is always a shape (*Gestalt*). Skepsis falls within consciousness itself, which unfolds into the skepticism that produces one shape of consciousness out of another in the appearing of what appears. Consciousness is consciousness in the manner of self-accomplishing skepticism, and this skepticism is the history of consciousness itself, which is neither natural consciousness in itself nor real knowing for itself but, before both, their original unity in and for itself. The movement of the coming of appearing and the going of what appears is the happening that brings consciousness, shape by shape, into the sight, the image (*Bild*), of its essence; this is "the history of the formation (*Bildung*) of consciousness itself to science." Hegel does not say "of natural consciousness into philosophical consciousness," since he thinks only of the appearing of appearing consciousness in view of its complete coming-forth, as which consciousness already is science. *Skepsis* is etymologized back to seeing and joined to the *vidi* of 145, so that the skeptic is the one who has seen being. Skepticism is then relocated from the philosopher into consciousness: consciousness is the self-accomplishing skepticism. "Shape" (*Gestalt*) is read through *stellen*, setting, as consciousness's self-positioning. *Bildung* is read through *Bild*, image: formation is consciousness bringing itself into the image of its essence. The "coming and going" pair anticipates the structure of experience as reaching out and arriving (185).

(153–54) Self-accomplishing skepticism is the historicity of the history in which consciousness forms itself out into the appearing of absolute knowing. Skepticism here is no longer the attitude of an individual subject, the subjective resolve to rely on no alien authority and test everything in the subject's own sense. That skepticism appeals to the insight of a self-representing ego but is no skepsis into the being of beings, which does not bend back into the narrow horizon of a limited evidence. Looking out on the appearing of appearing knowledge, it sees into the whole range of appearing knowledge, within which the individual *ego cogito* stays confined. But perhaps this range too, thought more essentially than Hegel could, is only the recollection of the *esse* of the *ens certum* of the *ego cogito*, extended into the reality of absolute knowing. This extension needs a prior skepsis into the breadth of the self-appearing of unconditioned subjectity; yet this going-forward is at the same time the resolute and complete retreat into that truth of beings which takes absolute certainty for being itself. The paragraph turns from exposition to critique. Hegel's skepticism transcends the Cartesian ego, yet the "whole range" it opens is still the Cartesian *esse* enlarged. Hegel's advance is therefore also a retreat: going forward into absoluteness withdraws into certainty as the truth of beings, and certainty is mistaken for being itself. This is the first explicit "but perhaps" in which Heidegger's own thinking steps out from behind the exposition.

(154) At this point a clarification of usage can no longer be avoided. Since settling his terminology, Hegel calls what becomes objective to consciousness in immediate representing "being" (*das Seiende*). This objective is represented one-sidedly, only on the side of its standing-against, without regard to representing and the representer. "Being," as the name for this, names what is not yet truly the true and real; Hegel uses it for reality that, in his sense, is still untrue. He interprets ancient philosophy accordingly: having not yet entered the land of self-consciousness, where the represented object first is as such, it thinks the real only as being. "Being" always counts for Hegel under the restriction "only being," since what truly is, is the actual (*ens actu*), whose actuality consists in the knowing of self-knowing certainty. Only this can claim to "be" all reality. So being returns here, where it was supposed to have vanished; but absolute knowing takes no notice of this. Heidegger documents Hegel's demotion of being to immediacy (the opening of the *Logic*, "being, pure being, without further determination") and his history of philosophy, in which the Greeks stop at being. The ironic point is that the very word Hegel demotes reasserts itself in the copula of "self-conscious-*ness*" and "to *be* all reality." The essay "Hegel and the Greeks" (GA 9) develops the same point.

(154–55) Unlike Hegel, we use the name "being" both for what Hegel, following Kant, calls objectness and objectivity and for what he represents as truly actual and calls the actuality of spirit. We do not interpret the Greeks' *einai* as Hegel does, as the objectness of the immediate representing of a subjectivity that has not yet come to itself, but from the Greek *alētheia*, as presencing out of and into unconcealment. The presence that happens in the re-presentation of consciousness's skepsis is a mode of presence that, like the *ousia* of the Greeks, comes from an as yet unthought essence of a concealed time. The beingness of beings, which from the beginning of Greek thought to Nietzsche's eternal recurrence of the same happened as the truth of beings, is for us only one mode of being, though a decisive one, and being by no means appears necessarily only as the presence of what is present. A marginal note in Heidegger's copy adds that since being is always the being of beings, being belongs within the ontological difference and so itself points to a more originary essence; what metaphysics knows as beingness is only one mode of "Beyng" (*Seyn*), which is the name, taken from metaphysics, for the difference itself. Given Hegel's usage, he should strictly not have named the true actuality of the actual, spirit, with a word still containing "being"; yet this happens throughout, since spirit's essence remains self-conscious-*being*. This usage is not imprecise or inconsistent terminology but is grounded in the concealed way in which being itself reveals and conceals itself. Heidegger here states his own usage and his own thesis: presence is one epoch of being, determined by an unthought time. That is the program of *Being and Time*'s Division Three, "Time and Being." The marginal note goes further toward the later thinking of the difference as such, written *Seyn*. The claim that Hegel's lapse into "being" is a destiny of being, not a terminological slip, applies the doctrine that being's concealment governs the language of metaphysics.

(155) If, by contrast, our reading of Hegel's text calls the appearing of appearing knowledge, and the absoluteness of the absolute, by the word "being," this may look arbitrary at first. But it is neither arbitrary nor a matter of mere terminology, assuming the language of thought may be brought together with a terminology at all, since terminology is by essence an instrument of the sciences. The language of thinking, grown from its destiny, calls what another thinking has thought into the brightness of its own thinking, in order to free the other into its own essence. Heidegger defends his method. A thinking dialogue (*Zwiesprache*) is neither paraphrase nor imposition; it translates the other thinker into a light that frees him into what is his own. The rejection of "terminology" as an instrument of the sciences links back to 133: thinking is not science.

(156) What happens when consciousness's skepsis looks ahead into the appearing of appearing knowledge and brings it to exposition? How does the exposition thereby bring itself to appearance, so that it ceases to be a mere coming-on-the-scene? Only if the exposition is sure that the whole history of the formation of consciousness occurs within it, a formation in which natural consciousness can find the truth of all its shapes. Heidegger closes the section with the question of completeness, which leads directly into Hegel's account of the completeness of the forms. It also ties completeness to the problem raised at 139–40: only a complete exposition escapes being a mere assertion.

### Completeness and Determinate Negation

(156) This section unfolds the question of "the completeness of the forms of unreal consciousness," the shapes of appearing knowledge so far as it has not yet appeared to itself in its appearing and so been set into its reality. The completeness of their coming-forth can only result from the course of this coming, the progression of appearing, which must be necessary, since only necessity guarantees a closure with no gaps of chance. What does the necessity of the progression rest on, and what is the essence of progression? Heidegger converts Hegel's question about completeness into a question about the essence of movement, which Heidegger will answer by the goal (159–60).

(156–57) To answer rightly we must not follow the view natural consciousness has of the exposition. That view is one-sided in principle: natural representing looks only to one side, which for it is not a side but the whole, the side of what is directly encountered. It never looks toward the other side, toward the being of beings. This essential one-sidedness can even appear as a shape of consciousness in the history of its formation: the skepticism that ends all knowing and acting in the result that the supposed knowledge amounts to nothing. As mere compulsive doubt of an unconditioned sophistry, it always has empty nothing as its result. Heidegger identifies Hegel's "abstract skepticism" as natural consciousness raised into a principle; the two "sides" are being and beings. This prepares what follows: being looks like nothing to a consciousness that sees only beings.

(157) How is natural knowing's one-sidedness raised in this shape into a known principle? Natural consciousness everywhere finds only beings, what appears, and judges everything it encounters by this finding. Whatever is not of that kind is decreed not to exist. Being is not of the kind of the finds of a consciousness that finds only beings. So, within its horizon, the appearing of what appears and the reality of the real count as nothing. By its judgment every step of the exposition leads to nothing, and the exposition never gets beyond its first step, which has already led it to nothing. How could it proceed, and where to? Only by letting some other shape of appearing knowledge fall to it from somewhere, finding there the supposed appearing, and falling with it into nothing again. Being is not a being, and so, to a consciousness that counts only beings, it is nothing. This is the thesis of "What Is Metaphysics?" (GA 9), where the nothing is the veil of being, now read into Hegel's critique of abstract skepticism. The image of the empty abyss into which each new content is thrown becomes the procedure of positivism.

(157–58) The view natural consciousness must hold whenever it judges the exposition also speaks, often enough, from objections raised against Hegel's philosophy as supposedly philosophical. Hegel's only defence in this section is that the nothing to which the exposition supposedly leads is not empty but "the nothing of that from which it comes." But appearing comes from what appears. If what results for the exposition results from where the course comes from, and not from where its next step leads, it is no wonder the course stays strange to natural consciousness. All the more must the one-sided view be kept from confusing everything. Heidegger reads "from which it comes" (*woraus es herkommt*) with emphasis on origin: the result of each step is determined by its provenance, not its destination. The movement thus runs backward, toward its ground, which prepares the next section's thesis that the course is drawn from the goal. The objections in view are the familiar charges of empty negativity and nihilism against Hegel's dialectic.

### The Goal, the Unrest of Consciousness, and the First Proposition

(158–59) This section characterizes the movement of the historical course in which the formation of consciousness runs. The progression through the complete series is to arise "of itself," which can only mean: from the way consciousness is in itself a course. So consciousness itself must now come into view, and the section leads to the first of Hegel's three propositions on consciousness. "Formation of consciousness" says that consciousness sets itself into the image of its essence, which is to be science as absolute knowing. This contains a twofold: consciousness appears to itself in its appearing, and at the same time, in the light of its own essence, it arranges itself according to the essential aspects of its shining and so organizes itself as the realm of its shapes. Consciousness itself is neither only natural nor only real consciousness, nor a mere coupling of both; it is their original unity. Natural and real knowing do not lie in consciousness like lifeless components; it is both of them as it appears in and as their original unity. A marginal note gives Heidegger's later gloss: "being equals complete self-appearing; 'absolute idea' is presencing in and for itself, as complete self-presencing." The two are distinguished within consciousness, and the distinction exists as the unrest of the natural against the real and the real against the natural. Consciousness is in itself the unrest of self-distinguishing between natural and real knowing. The movement of the historical course rests on this unrest and has its direction from it; consciousness is neither put into motion afterward nor directed afterward. The section's achievement is to make motion intrinsic: no external motor drives the *Phenomenology*, since consciousness is the unrest of the difference between beings and being. "Unrest" (*Unruhe*) is Hegel's word from his account of the goal; Heidegger gives it the ontological difference as its content. The marginal note identifies Hegel's being with complete self-presencing, confirming that the essay reads the absolute idea as presence perfected.

(159–60) In the history of formation, natural consciousness proves to be "only the concept of knowledge." But this "only" is already enough. Since natural consciousness, in representing beings, unavoidably co-represents their beingness, however inexplicitly, it is beyond itself in itself, and yet not outside itself. It not only takes no notice of the "concept" it always already is; it thinks it can do without it, while in truth the domain of beings in which it dwells, its breadth and the manner of its controllability, is determined solely by what consciousness itself is as knowing the beingness of beings. Natural consciousness conceals from itself the unrest of being-beyond-itself at work in it; it flees and so chains itself to it in its own way. It takes its opining for the true, claims truth for itself, and attests that what it takes for its own is not its own. Its opining constantly betrays the unrest of the relentless being-torn-beyond-itself. The exposition need only engage this unrest to be already in the course of progression. But the relentlessness can be determined only by what the unrest is held to: what tears it onward, the reality of the real, which is only in appearing to itself in its truth. Seen from the direction of the progression, this is the goal. Thought from the unrest of consciousness, the course begins with the goal; it is a movement from the goal, which is not left behind but arrives with the movement in its unfolding. The goal is set for knowing in its own essence as that essence itself, and consciousness in its unrest is the self-setting-ahead of the goal. Heidegger inverts the teleological picture. The goal is not a terminus ahead but the origin from which the movement starts, because the understanding of being that natural consciousness already has is what draws it on. The claim that the domain of beings and their controllability is fixed by the understanding of beingness links the paragraph to the analysis of technology (149–50).

(160) That is why Hegel's section begins its characterization of motion with: "But the goal is set for knowledge as necessarily as the series of the progression." Yet the section does not discuss the goal in the form in which one represents a goal, as what something drives toward. If the language of mechanics were allowed as a makeshift, we could say: the progression is not pushed forward from the current shape into the undetermined but drawn from the already-set goal. The attracting goal brings itself forth into its appearing in the attracting, and brings the course of consciousness in advance into the fullness of its completeness (*Voll-ständigkeit*, "full standing"). Heidegger hyphenates *vorgesteckt* (set before) and *Voll-ständigkeit* to make the goal something already standing in full. The push and pull image is a deliberate concession; the thought is that the goal is present before the course, as the parousia is.

(160–61) Self-accomplishing skepticism has already taken the goal so understood into view and so into the center of consciousness's unrest. Since this center constantly begins the movement, the skepsis governing knowing has already embraced all possible shapes; the range of forms of unreal knowing is complete according to this embrace. The exposition's way of representing all appearing knowledge is nothing but going along with the skepsis at work in consciousness, sustaining in advance the relentlessness by which consciousness, natural knowing, is torn beyond itself into real knowing. Through this tearing-out natural consciousness loses what it takes for its true and its life, so the tearing-out is its death. In this constant death consciousness sacrifices its death, to win from the sacrifice its resurrection to itself. Natural consciousness suffers a force, but the force comes from consciousness itself; it is the rule of unrest within it. This rule is the will of the absolute, which in its absoluteness wills to be with us, who always dwell amidst beings in the manner of natural consciousness. Now the first proposition on consciousness becomes clear: "Consciousness, however, is for itself its concept." It differs from the sentence that opened Hegel's account of natural consciousness, "natural consciousness will prove to be only the concept of knowledge." It speaks of consciousness as such, not natural consciousness, and it stresses "concept." "Concept" now means the self-appearing of consciousness in its truth, whose essence is unconditioned certainty. By this measure something known is not yet conceived if only represented in general; it must be referred back to the knowing that belongs to it and represented together with it in this reference. Only so is the known on all sides in knowing, which has thereby become a comprehensive and unconditioned general representing, conceiving. In relation to this concept, natural consciousness remains "only concept," because as consciousness it has some representation of knownness in general. Only because consciousness is for itself its concept can natural consciousness, belonging to it, persist in being only the concept of knowledge. To understand the first proposition fully we must also weigh the stress on "is." It means that consciousness accomplishes its self-appearing in such a way that in appearing to itself it forms for itself the site of appearing that belongs to its essence; so it finds itself in its concept. Three things are packed in. Completeness is guaranteed by the skepsis that has embraced all shapes in advance, not by an inventory. The death of natural consciousness is a sacrifice that yields resurrection, the first appearance of the Christological figures that dominate the end (202–03). And "concept" has two senses: traditional (a general representation, "only concept") and Hegelian (self-conceiving that refers the known back to knowing). The emphasis on "is" makes the proposition ontological: consciousness *exists* as its concept, forming the site of its own appearing.

(162–63) Because the first proposition makes the truth of consciousness visible, Hegel can now clarify natural consciousness as not-real knowing. He also calls it untrue, which does not mean it is merely the spoil-heap of the false and deceptive. It is the not-yet-true, overwhelmed by the force that tears it into its truth. It feels this force and falls into anxiety for its survival. Hegel, whose rationalism can never be praised or abused enough, speaks at the decisive place, where he names the relation of natural knowing to the being of beings, of "the feeling of this force." This feeling of the force of the will that the absolute is characterizes how natural consciousness is "only the concept of knowledge." But it would be foolish to think Hegel meant that natural anxiety, which makes consciousness evade the being of beings, is as such the organ by which philosophy thinks being, as if philosophy, wherever thinking must refer back to feeling, were handed over to mere feeling instead of being grounded in science. Such superficial opining, which still makes its school today, belongs itself to the vanity of the understanding that delights in the inertia of its thoughtlessness. At the end of the same section that looks out into the truth of knowing with the first proposition, its untruth appears as "the dry I," which finds its only satisfaction in restriction to the beings it encounters. Heidegger notices the place of affect in Hegel's text, "the feeling of this force," and of anxiety (*Angst*) before truth, but he forbids reading it as an anticipation of the attunement-based ontology of *Being and Time*, or as Romantic feeling-philosophy. The jab at "school" opinion likely targets the existentialist reception of anxiety, which made it an organ of insight. Anxiety here is evasion, not disclosure.

(163) "The dry I" names the high-handed conduct of ordinary opining within philosophy. It does not name the isolated ego as opposed to the community of the "we"; it is precisely the subject of the many in their common opining. The dry I lives in the egoism of the "they" (*man*), which flees the anxiety of self-accomplishing skepticism into the dogmatics of opinion, whose principle is to shut its eyes to the exposition and refuse to go along. So the dogmatism of current views must be left to itself. By this decision philosophy does not reject natural consciousness; how could it, since science is the truth of the not-yet-true, and so is that itself, in its truth? Philosophy first discovers natural consciousness in its naturalness and acknowledges it. Philosophy passes it by only when natural consciousness puffs itself up as philosophy, erasing its boundaries and turning its back on philosophy as knowledge of being. Then philosophy passes by only what has already turned away, while in passing by it still engages natural consciousness, and only it, in order to be the course on which the truth of consciousness appears. Heidegger connects Hegel's "dry I" with the "they" of *Being and Time* § 27: the dry I is not the solitary ego but the anonymous public subject of shared opinion. Philosophy's relation to natural consciousness is thus not contempt but recognition; what it refuses is only natural consciousness disguised as philosophy, which Heidegger's polemics against "philosophical" positivism and anthropology elsewhere name.

(163–64) The exposition of appearing knowledge is self-accomplishing skepticism; in accomplishing itself it carries itself out, and it presents itself instead of merely coming on the scene. Its way does not go from natural to real consciousness; consciousness itself, which is this difference of natural and real in every shape, goes from one shape to another, and the progression is determined by the goal, the force of the absolute's will. The exposition follows the appearing that comes toward it. The natural representation of absolute knowing as a means has vanished; knowing can no longer be tested as a means applied to an object; and since the exposition presents itself, testing seems superfluous. So the exposition could begin at once, unless it has already begun. Instead new sections of reflection follow, which shows that the essence of the exposition has not yet been brought near enough, nor our own relation to it reached. How exposition and what is exposed belong together, whether they are even the same without flowing into sameness, remains dark. How can absolute knowing be a way to the absolute, if the absolute is already with us? If we may speak of a way at all, it is only the way the absolute itself goes, insofar as it is this way. Could the exposition be this way? Its essence has become still more enigmatic. Only this is clear: the exposition does not confront the absolute from somewhere, separated from it, as natural consciousness represents knowing. Heidegger's closing paragraph here sums up and sharpens the riddle. The phrase "unless it has already begun" anticipates the end (205–07), where the piece itself is the beginning. The suggestion that the way is the absolute's own way recasts the *Phenomenology* as the self-movement of being rather than a human route.

### The Problem of the Standard

(164–65) This section takes up again the natural representation of knowing, but only to raise anew the question of testing absolute knowing. Since knowing is not a means, testing becomes not obsolete but questionable for the first time. If the exposition brings appearing knowledge forth into its appearing, it sets not-yet-true consciousness into its truth; it measures what appears by its appearing, which is the standard. Where does the exposition get it? So far as science undertakes to test appearing knowledge, it itself acts as the authority and so as the standard. Even if its emergence consists in the exposition carrying itself out, it must bring the standard, already justified, with its first step. Science needs the standard in order to carry itself out, yet the standard can only result from the carrying out, assuming absolute knowing cannot take its standard from elsewhere. If the exposition must measure untrue knowing by its truth, it is forced to unite the incompatible. The impossible stands in its way. How can the obstacle be cleared? Heidegger sharpens Hegel's dilemma: the standard is both presupposed and a result. This is the circularity of every foundational project; Heidegger presents it as a real impasse to be dissolved by looking at consciousness, not argued away.

### Knowledge and Truth as Determinations of Consciousness

(165–66) This section continues in a way that shows Hegel does not settle the contradiction by logical argument. The apparent incompatibility lies not in the exposition's essence but in the inadequate way we, still governed by natural representing, see it. The exposition aims at the appearing of knowing and is itself a knowing; both fall within consciousness. If the question of standard and testing has any foothold, it can only find what it asks for in consciousness itself. Is consciousness as such something like measure and standard, a testing of itself? Consciousness moves more clearly into view, though which basic trait of its essence the reflection aims at is not yet apparent. The method is phenomenological rather than dialectical in the logician's sense: the contradiction dissolves when the matter is seen correctly. This sets the pattern for the sections that follow.

(166) As if nothing had yet been said about consciousness, Hegel begins with two determinations "as they occur in consciousness," knowledge and truth. They are "abstract" because they result from looking at consciousness while looking away from the full essence of its constitution and unity; consciousness is taken as it immediately, and so always one-sidedly, offers itself to natural representing. Heidegger explains Hegel's "abstract": these are natural consciousness's definitions, drawn from the one side it sees. Their insufficiency is intended.

(166) "To be conscious" says that something is in the state of the known. The known is in knowing and is as a knowing; it is that to which consciousness relates in the manner of knowing, and it is insofar as it is "for" consciousness. What so is, is in the manner of "being-for." But being-for is a mode of knowing; in it something is "for it," for consciousness, for which as the known it is nevertheless another. In knowing as being-for something is one and other "for the same." The known is not only represented in general; representing means it as something that is in itself, truly. This being-in-itself of the known is called truth. Truth too is one (something represented) and other (something in itself) "for the same." The two determinations, knowledge and truth, differ as being-for and being-in-itself. Hegel only directs our gaze to them, without saying "what really lies in them." Yet he has thereby pointed, unobtrusively but deliberately, to a distinctive basic trait of consciousness, which the section's first sentences name in passing. Heidegger's gloss makes clear that even "truth," the in-itself, is posited *for* consciousness. The in-itself is a moment within the relation, which already contains the solution that follows (168–72).

(167) In consciousness something is distinguished from and by it. Consciousness, as itself and through itself, is the one relative to an other. What is separated in this difference (object for subject, within the subject) stays related, through the distinguishing, to what distinguishes. Consciousness, representing, sets something off from itself, but sets what is set off toward itself. It is in itself a distinguishing that is none, and as this difference that is no difference it is ambiguous in its essence. This ambiguity is the essence of representing. Because of it, the two determinations, knowledge and truth, being-for and being-in-itself, occur immediately in consciousness, and occur ambiguously. "A distinction that is none" is Hegel's formula for self-consciousness in the Force and Understanding chapter; Heidegger generalizes it to all representing. Ambiguity (*Zweideutigkeit*) is a positive structure, not confusion, and it will turn out to mirror the ambiguity of the Greek *on* (175–76).

(167) What is the exposition, seen from these two determinations, since as representing it remains a mode of consciousness? It represents what appears in its appearing; it examines knowledge for its truth, tests the one by the other. It moves in the distinguishing of the distinction that consciousness is. So the view onto the difference opens a prospect: that the exposition receives its standard and testing character from the medium it moves in. The prospect becomes clearer once it emerges what measuring and testing are directed at, seen from consciousness itself. The exposition is itself consciousness, so the distinction it applies belongs to what it examines. The circle raised at 164–65 starts to look like a self-relation.

### Who Investigates: The "For Us"

(167–68) This section asks directly what the exposition investigates. But the question is direct only if it asks not only what is investigated but who investigates, since what is investigated, being known, is in our knowing for us who investigate. In characterizing science we unexpectedly come into play, and it turns out we are already in play, since what is exposed is "for us." The role of the "for us" cannot be avoided, and its reach extends into a dimension we barely suspect. Heidegger promotes the "we" to a central problem. Its "dimension" is resolved only at the end, where the "with us" of the parousia becomes "not without us" (204–05).

(168) What do we investigate when we test knowing for its truth? Truth is being-in-itself, knowledge is being for a consciousness. Investigating the truth of knowledge, we seek what it is in itself. But our investigation would make knowledge our object; set before us in its being-in-itself, it would have become a being for us. We would grasp not the truth of knowledge but only our knowledge of it, and being-for-us would be the standard. But how would knowledge come to submit to a standard that turns what is to be measured into the measure itself? If the exposition had to proceed as the two determinations suggest, it could only keep turning its own conduct into its opposite. The difficulty is the reflexive version of the critical paradox: the investigator's standpoint swallows the in-itself. This is the Kantian problem of whether we can know knowledge as it is in itself.

### Consciousness Provides Its Own Standard

(168–69) This section frees the exposition from this difficulty by pointing simply to the nature of its object, which is consciousness itself, whose nature is what rises of itself into appearing. Does consciousness by nature have the character of a standard? Then it must offer of itself the possibility of being at once measure and measured: distinguished in this respect and also not distinguished. This came to light at 167. The essential ambiguity of consciousness, the difference of representing that is no difference, points to a twofold in its nature, which contains the possibility of being in essence both measure and measured. If we take the ambiguity not as a lack of univocity but as the mark of its unity, consciousness shows in it the belonging-together of the two determinations first represented as separate, knowledge and truth. Measuring and measure become possible from consciousness's nature. Nature (*Natur*) is glossed as *physis*, what rises of itself into appearing, which binds Hegel's "nature of the object" to Heidegger's account of the natural. Ambiguity is revalued as twofoldness (*Zwiefalt*), the key word of the later essays on Parmenides and Anaximander.

(169–70) Hegel characterizes the object's nature in a second proposition on consciousness. The first, stated at 161, reads: "Consciousness is for itself its concept." The second: "Consciousness provides its standard in itself" (*an ihm selbst*). The idiom is strange to us but familiar to Hegel from the nature of the matter. Why "an ihm selbst" (on or in it itself) and not "an sich selbst" (in itself)? Because it lies with consciousness that there is a standard for it. The standard is not fetched from elsewhere, taken on and held; nor is it applied to consciousness. It lies in it, because the standard-like arises from it, being twofold, measure and measured. Would it not be as good or better to say consciousness gives its standard "an sich selbst"? But what is consciousness in itself? It is in itself when it is with itself, and it is with itself when it is expressly for itself and so in and for itself. To give its standard in itself would strictly mean: consciousness gives itself the standard for itself. Yet consciousness usually does not turn to what it is in truth; on the other hand, truth does not fall to it from elsewhere, since it is already for itself its concept. Therefore it has its standard in it, puts the standard at its own disposal. "An ihm selbst" means a twofold: consciousness has the standard lying in its essence; but what lies so in it, it does not thereby directly give to itself. It gives and at the same time does not give. Heidegger's grammatical precision serves a thesis. "An sich selbst" would mean the standard is already explicitly possessed by consciousness for itself, which would make testing unnecessary. "An ihm selbst" means the standard lies in consciousness without its knowing. The resulting "gives and does not give" is the first of the three ambiguous verbs analyzed at 181–82.

(170–71) Insofar as natural consciousness represents beings in themselves, what is represented is the true, "for it," the immediately representing consciousness. Corresponding to "an ihm selbst," Hegel uses "for it" (*für es*) when he means that consciousness takes what it directly represents for the true. Representing directly, it is absorbed in what is represented and does not refer it back to itself; it has it, but not for itself, only "for it." Yet with the true it represents for it, it has at the same time, "for us" who heed the truth of the true, the truth of the true in it, the standard. In exhibiting appearing knowledge we take appearing as the standard by which to measure the knowing that takes what appears for the true. If we call this true the object and the knowing the concept, testing means seeing whether what natural consciousness takes for its knowing corresponds to what is true. If, conversely, we call the knowing we test the object and the in-itself of the known the concept, testing means seeing whether the object corresponds to its concept. The decisive point is that whenever we represent what appears in its appearing, what we measure and what we measure with fall within consciousness, which supplies both moments of testing in itself. Hence the maxim for us, the expositors: leave aside your notions and opinions about what appears. The basic attitude of absolute knowing is not to assail appearing consciousness with knowledge and argument but to leave all that aside. By leaving it aside we arrive at pure looking-on, which brings appearing into sight; in it we "consider the matter as it is in and for itself." The matter is appearing knowledge as appearing; its matter-ness, the reality of the real, is appearing itself. Three perspectives are fixed: "an ihm selbst" (in consciousness, unknown to it), "für es" (for natural consciousness), and "für uns" (for those who heed the appearing). The phenomenological maxim of letting the matter show itself (cf. *Being and Time* § 7, "to the things themselves") is located in Hegel. The final sentence turns "the matter" into appearing itself, so that pure looking-on is a looking at being.

(171–72) The way Hegel shows that both fall within consciousness looks like a dubious play on words. Consciousness includes knowledge and the true known in it, and it seems to come to the same whether we call the first concept and the second object or the reverse. It does come to the same (*das Selbe*), but it is not the equal (*das Gleiche*), and so not indifferent how we use the names. If the true represented in natural consciousness is called the object, it is the object "for it"; if knowledge is called the object, then knowledge, as appearing, is the object "for us," who consider what appears with regard to its appearing. If the knowing by which natural consciousness represents the known is called the concept, conceiving means representing something as something, the concept in the sense of traditional logic. If, instead, the true represented in consciousness is called the concept, by which knowledge as our object is measured, then the concept is the truth of the true, the appearing in which appearing knowledge is brought to itself. Heidegger saves Hegel's two formulations from triviality by assigning them to two standpoints, "for it" and "for us." The second naming already contains the reversal (188): knowledge becomes our object and the concept becomes the truth of the true.

(172) The apparently arbitrary use of "object" and "concept" is bound in advance by the nature of consciousness stated in the first proposition: "Consciousness is for itself its concept." In what consciousness takes for its true, it will realize a shape of its truth. The true is the object "for it"; truth is the object "for us." Because consciousness is for itself its concept, it gives its standard in itself. In what appears there appears, not "for it" but "for us," the appearing of what appears. Hegel says this in the sentence we now understand better with our added emphasis: "In what consciousness declares within itself as the in-itself or the true, *we* (namely we who know absolutely) have the standard which it itself sets up for measuring its knowledge." The distinction between "the true" (for it) and "truth" (for us) is the textual form of the ontological difference. Heidegger's added stress on "we" restores the role of the absolute knowers that the maxim of pure looking-on seems to erase.

(172–73) Since we have the standard for testing from consciousness itself, no contribution of ours is needed in this respect. But what we have at our disposal, insofar as we ourselves are consciousness, is not yet expressly at our disposal. If the exposition is put under the maxim of pure looking-on, it remains obscure how mere leaving-aside of our views should give us something, and give the standard as such. Granted that the knowledge measured and the standard fall within consciousness, measuring and its execution cannot happen without our contribution. Does not everything essential remain our doing? What about testing itself, without which neither measured nor standard are what they are? Heidegger finds a gap in Hegel's maxim. Leaving aside is itself an act, and the measuring needs an agent. Heidegger's answer is that consciousness tests itself (173), and that "our contribution" is indeed required, though not as an imposition (190).

### Consciousness Tests Itself: Ontic and Ontological Consciousness

(173) This section answers by stating and explaining the third proposition on consciousness, hidden in a subordinate clause. As a main clause it reads: "Consciousness tests itself." Consciousness, so far as it is consciousness, is testing. The basic word of modern metaphysics, consciousness, is thought only when we hear in its "-being" the trait of testing, a testing determined by the consciousness-character of knowing. As with the parousia (130), the decisive thing hides in a subordinate clause. Heidegger now adds testing to the meaning of "-being" in *Bewußt-sein*, alongside presence and gathering: to be conscious is to put oneself to the test.

(173) In testing, the measured and the measure are together; they never come together in consciousness by one being laid against the other. The nature of consciousness is their holding together, which has shown itself in several respects. Natural consciousness is immediate knowledge of an object it takes as true; it is also a knowing of its knowing of the object, even if it does not turn back to that knowing expressly. Consciousness of the object and consciousness of knowledge are the same, for which both are known; both "are for the same." Consciousness is for itself the distinction of the two from each other. By its nature it is the comparison of one with the other, and this comparing is testing. "Consciousness tests itself." Heidegger follows Hegel's argument, adding only the qualification "even if it does not turn back to it expressly." Self-consciousness is implicit in all consciousness, which is why comparison is native to it.

(174) But consciousness is the test only in such a way that it first becomes evident to it whether knowledge corresponds to the object, and so is the object in truth, or whether the object corresponds to what knowledge at bottom knows. Testing is only as this becoming happens to consciousness. It comes over consciousness when it gets behind what that is in truth which counts for it immediately as true, and behind what it then knows for certain when it represents the object in its objectness. Accordingly there is for consciousness, behind the object and behind its immediate representing of it, something still to reach, toward which it must first set out. "Setting out" (*sich aufmachen*) means both opening oneself to and setting off toward. Testing is an event that befalls consciousness, not an operation it performs; this is why Hegel speaks of "becoming." The double sense of *aufmachen* combines disclosure with journey and anticipates the etymology of *Erfahrung* as a journeying (185).

(174) The first proposition showed that natural consciousness is "only the concept of knowledge." It has a general representation of its object as object, and of its knowledge as knowledge. But it does not engage with this "as," since it accepts only what is immediately represented, though always with the help of the "as." Because by its own sense it does not engage with the "as," it never, stubborn as it is, goes behind to what it strangely has before it as its background. So consciousness is the comparison, and again is not. By its nature it is, in representing the object, the distinction between being-in-itself and being-for-it, truth and knowledge. It is not only the distinction that is none; it is at once a comparing of the object with its objectness, of knowledge with its knownness. Consciousness itself is the comparison, which natural consciousness never expressly carries out. The "as" (the *hēi* of *on hēi on*, the "as-structure" of *Being and Time* § 32) is what natural consciousness uses but never thematizes. With it the difference between object and objectness, knowledge and knownness, enters as the real content of Hegel's "comparison." The odd image of a background that lies before one prepares the reversal at 178: what seems behind is in truth in front.

(175) In the nature of consciousness, knowledge and object are dirempted and yet cannot come apart; object and concept are likewise dirempted in the "as" and cannot come apart; and these two pairs are themselves dirempted and cannot come apart. That Hegel distinguishes all this but levels the differences into a general distinguishing, so that they do not come into their own, has its concealed ground in the essence of metaphysics, not in the metaphysical position of Hegel's philosophy. From the same concealed essence it follows that the level to which the differences are flattened is determined by the discreteness of one and other, which presents itself in the distinction of reason (*ratio*). Hegel grasps distinction as the negation of negation. This is Heidegger's central critical observation. Hegel has the differences, including the difference of beings and being in the "as," but treats them all as instances of one formal distinguishing, logical negation. That flattening is not Hegel's personal failing but the fate of metaphysics, which thinks difference as the distinction of the *ratio*. Heidegger's own task is to let the difference between being and beings come into its own; see "The Onto-theo-logical Constitution of Metaphysics," where the difference "as difference" is the unthought of Hegel.

(175–76) With due caution, a difference named "earlier elsewhere" can be applied to Hegel's distinctions. Natural consciousness, which goes straight to the object as a being and to its knowledge as a being, and stays there, could be called ontic consciousness. "Ontic," from the Greek *to on*, means what concerns beings. But the Greek *on* harbors its own essence of beingness (*ousia*), which does not stay the same through its history. If we use the words *on* and "being" thoughtfully, we must heed how their meaning changes and how it is historically fixed in each case. When beings appear as objects, because beingness has been cleared as objectness, and when being is consequently addressed as the non-objective, all this already rests on the ontology by which the *on* became *hypokeimenon*, this became *subiectum*, and its being was determined from the subjectity of consciousness. Since *on* means both "a being" and "being" (participle), the *on* as a being can be gathered (*legein*) toward its being; by its ambiguity it is already so gathered. It is ontological. But with the essence of the *on* this gathering, the *logos*, changes, and with it ontology. Since the *on*, what is present, rose up as *physis*, the presencing of what is present lay for the Greek thinkers in *phainesthai*, the self-showing appearing of the unconcealed. Accordingly the many present things (*ta onta*) were thought as what is simply accepted as present in its appearing, without anything further: accepting (*dechesthai*) means taking it as it comes and letting it rest with what is present. Such accepting does not think on to the presencing of the present; it remains in *doxa*. *Noein*, by contrast, is that perceiving which expressly perceives what is present in its presencing and attends to it accordingly. The "earlier elsewhere" is *Being and Time* and *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics*, where ontic and ontological were introduced. Heidegger then historicizes the pair: "ontology" is not a fixed discipline but changes with the essence of the *on*. The participial ambiguity of *on* (a being and being) is the root of the "as." The Greek pair *doxa* and *noein* (Parmenides) is the original form of the distinction between natural and real knowing. The phrase "the non-objective" (*das Ungegenständliche*) targets contemporary appeals to a non-objectifiable being, which still define being negatively from the object.

(176) The ambiguity of *on* names both what is present and presencing, both at once and neither as such. Corresponding to this essential ambiguity, the *noein* of *einai*, of *eon*, belongs together with the *doxa* of the *dokounta*, the things that seem, which are the *eonta*. What *noein* perceives is not the truly real as opposed to mere semblance. Rather, *doxa* perceives what is present immediately, but not the presencing of what is present, which *noein* perceives. This corrects the Platonist reading of Parmenides as two worlds, truth and illusion. *Doxa* and *noein* are two receptions of one ambiguous *eon*, as natural consciousness and real knowing are two moments of one consciousness. This anticipates the 1952 lecture course *What Is Called Thinking?* and "Moira" (on Parmenides).

(176–77) If we think the essence of metaphysics, as will become necessary, as the emergence of the twofold of what is present and presencing out of the self-concealing ambiguity of *on*, then the beginning of metaphysics coincides with the beginning of Western thought. If, on the other hand, we take the separation of a supersensory from a sensory world as its essence, with the former as truly real and the latter as merely apparent, metaphysics begins with Socrates and Plato. But what began with their thinking is only a specifically directed interpretation of that early twofold in the *on*. With it begins the dis-essence (*Unwesen*) of metaphysics, from which later thinkers misinterpret its genuine beginning to this day. Yet this dis-essence is nothing negative if we consider that already at metaphysics' beginning the difference holding sway in the ambiguity of *on* stays unthought, and that this staying-unthought constitutes the essence of metaphysics. Accordingly the *logos* of the *on* stays ungrounded; but this ungroundedness gives onto-logy the force of its essence. Two definitions of metaphysics are set side by side. The Platonic two-world one is derivative. The deeper one locates metaphysics at the very start, in the emergence of the twofold whose difference stays unthought. *Unwesen* is not a moral word; it names the essential deformation that belongs to an essence. The ungrounded *logos* gives ontology its force, which echoes the groundless propositions at 135.

(177–78) Behind the title "onto-logy" the history of being conceals itself from us. "Ontological" means carrying out the gathering of beings toward their beingness; that being is ontological which by nature stands in this history, enduring it according to the unconcealment of beings. So we can say: consciousness, in its immediate representing of beings, is ontic consciousness; for it the being is the object. But representing the object represents it, however unthought, as object; it has already gathered the object into its objectness and is therefore ontological. Since it does not think objectness as such and yet already represents it, natural consciousness is ontological and yet not yet. We call it pre-ontological. As ontic-pre-ontological, natural consciousness is latently the distinction between the ontically true and ontological truth. Because to be conscious means to be this distinction, consciousness is by nature the comparison of the ontically and the ontologically represented. As comparison it is in testing; its representing is in itself a natural putting-itself-to-the-test. Hegel's "consciousness tests itself" is translated into Heidegger's terms: to be conscious is to be the latent ontological difference, so self-testing is the movement between pre-ontological and ontological understanding. The pre-ontological is the *Being and Time* term; its historicizing (ontology as history of being) is the later one.

(178) Consciousness is therefore never natural consciousness in the sense of being cut off from what its object is in truth and its knowledge in certainty. Natural consciousness rests in its nature and is according to one of its modes, but it is not itself its nature. It is natural for it never to come of itself to its nature, to what constantly goes on behind its back. Yet as naturally pre-ontological it is already set on the way to its truth, while constantly turning back on the way and remaining for it. Ordinary opining does not care to see what is really behind and hidden in what it takes for true; it resists the looking-on by which skepsis examines what is in truth the truth behind the true. One day skepsis might even come to see that what remains a "behind" for philosophy's opining is in truth a "before." Its truth, which natural consciousness never gets behind as its background, is itself, in truth, the foreground of the light within which every kind of knowing and consciousness, as a having-seen, already stands. This is a key moment of Heidegger's own thinking in the essay. Hegel's "behind the back of consciousness" is reinterpreted: being is not behind beings but in front of them, the light in which they are seen. "Foreground of the light" points to the clearing (*Lichtung*), and "one day skepsis might" signals that this insight lies beyond Hegel.

(178–79) But philosophy itself sometimes resists skepsis and sides with the habitual opining of natural consciousness. It admits that objectness belongs to the object as object, but takes objectness as only the non-objective. It reassures ordinary opining that it is basically right, since the non-objective can be represented only in the representations of ordinary consciousness, which are therefore inadequate and a mere play of signs. Such assurances easily convince natural consciousness and even give it the impression that this assuring is critical philosophy, since it is skeptical toward ontology. But this kind of skepsis is only the semblance of skepsis, a flight from thinking into the system of opinion. The target is a philosophy of the "non-objective" whose access to it is through symbols or ciphers. Jaspers' ciphers of transcendence fit the description, as do neo-Kantian views of the non-objectifiable; Heidegger names no one. Such philosophy is "critical" only in appearance and actually confirms natural consciousness, defining being negatively from beings.

(179) When skepsis accomplishes itself as complete skepticism, by contrast, thinking within metaphysics is under way as the expressly performed comparing of ontic and pre-ontological consciousness through ontological consciousness. This does not separate itself from natural consciousness but goes back into the nature of consciousness as the original unity of ontic and pre-ontological representing. When the comparing happens, testing is under way. In this happening, consciousness is its own self-appearing in appearing; it is present to itself; it is. Consciousness is by becoming itself in its truth. The completion of skepsis is the explicit ontological comparison of what natural consciousness latently is. The sequence "it is present to itself; it is" makes testing, presence, and being coincide, closing the argument that self-testing is the being of consciousness.

(179) Becoming takes place as testing, a comparing, goes on (*vor sich geht*). Testing can go only by going ahead of itself (*vor sich her*): skepsis looks ahead and is provident (*sieht vor sich und sieht sich vor*), foreseeing what knowledge and its object are in their truth. Heidegger already indicated (151) that natural consciousness loses its truth on the way of testing. When its supposed true is regarded in view of truth, knowledge proves not to correspond to its object, since it does not let itself be addressed by its objectness. To become adequate to the truth of the object, consciousness must change its previous knowledge; but in changing its knowledge of the object, the object has changed too. The wordplay on *vor sich gehen* (to happen; to go ahead of itself) and *vorsehen* (to foresee, to provide) defines skepsis as an anticipatory seeing. The rest restates Hegel's account of self-testing in ontological terms: knowledge "fails" because it does not heed objectness.

(179–80) Objectness is now the object, and what is now called object can no longer be determined from former opinion about objects. Yet that opinion still holds sway when objectness is given out only from the former object, negatively and ever more negatively, as the non-objective. Philosophy then busies itself with being the glorification of the thoughtless incapacity of ordinary opining. The new object is objectness, being, which cannot be grasped in object-terms. The bitter sentence about philosophy renews the polemic of 178–79.

(180) In the testing comparison that looks ahead into the appearing of appearing knowledge, not only does natural knowledge of the object fail to hold its stand as the supposedly sole true, but the object itself does not keep its stand as the standard. In the testing that consciousness is, neither the tested nor the standard passes the test. Neither stands before what has meanwhile arisen in testing. Heidegger hears *Stand* in *Gegenstand* (object, "what stands against") and *bestehen* (to pass, to persist): object and standard both lose their standing. What "has arisen" (*erstanden*) is the new object, named in the next section.

### Experience as the Being of Beings: The Dialectical Movement

(180) This section begins: "This dialectical movement which consciousness exercises on itself, on its knowledge as well as its object, insofar as the new true object arises for it from this, is properly what is called experience." What does Hegel name with "experience"? He names the being of beings. Beings have meanwhile become subject, and with it object and objective. From of old, being means presencing; the way in which consciousness, what is from knownness, presences is appearing. Consciousness, as the being it is, is appearing knowledge. With "experience" Hegel names what appears as appearing, the *on hēi on*; in the word experience the *hēi* is thought, and from this *hēi* (*qua*, as) beings are thought in their beingness. Experience is no longer the name of a kind of knowing; it is now the word for being, insofar as being is perceived from beings as such. Experience names the subjectity of the subject. It says what "-being" means in *Bewußt-sein*, in such a way that only from this "-being" does it become clear and binding what remains to be thought in "conscious." This is the thesis of the essay. "Experience" names the "as" of being as being, the being of consciousness, subjectity. The "as" of 174 thereby returns as the core of the concept, and the earlier analyses of *Bewußt-sein* (145, 173) are completed: its "-being" is experience.

(180–81) The strange word falls into the reflection as the name of the being of beings because it has become due (*fällig*). Its use departs entirely from ordinary and philosophical usage, but it falls as a fruit of the matter at which Hegel's thinking persists here. Its justification, which is something quite other than a mere manner of speaking, lies in what the preceding sections brought into view about the nature of consciousness. The three propositions on consciousness sketch the structure of that nature. The play on *fallen* and *fällig* (falling, ripe, due) presents the word as matured from the matter, not chosen. Usage is not terminology; it is justified by the thing (cf. 155).

(181) Heidegger sets the three propositions on consciousness out as separate lines: "Consciousness, however, is for itself its concept." "Consciousness provides its standard in itself." "Consciousness tests itself." The second unfolds the first in one respect: "its concept," in which consciousness grasps itself in its truth, is the measure for the course of self-conceiving, and this measure falls within consciousness together with the measured. The third points into the original unity of measured and measure, as which consciousness essentially is, being itself the testing comparison from which both arise, together with the appearing of what appears. The essence of appearing is experience. The word must now keep the meaning it has received from this account of the nature of consciousness. Heidegger arranges the propositions as a structure. The first is the thesis, the second its unfolding, and the third the ground of their unity, which is the event of testing. The appearing that this event brings about is what "experience" names.

(181) Yet with the three propositions something has emerged that should always have been named, since it is unavoidable. Hegel states it only in the section where the decisive word "experience" falls. The verbs of all three propositions are ambiguous: the "is" in the first, the "gives" in the second, the "tests" in the third. Heidegger isolates the verbs, since ambiguity in the verb is ambiguity in the mode of being. The next three units take them one by one.

(181) Consciousness is for itself its concept, and at the same time is not. It is its concept in such a way that the concept becomes for it and it finds itself in the concept. The copula has a temporal structure: consciousness is its concept as a becoming.

(182) Consciousness gives its standard in itself, and at the same time does not. It gives it, insofar as the truth of consciousness comes from itself, arriving in its appearing as absolute certainty. It does not give it, insofar as it withholds the standard again and again, since the standard, as the always untrue object, never holds; consciousness keeps it, so to speak, behind the mountain. The giving is a withholding. The standard is given in advance as the goal and denied at each stage as the current object.

(182) Consciousness tests itself, and yet does not. It tests itself insofar as it is what it is from the comparison of objectness and object. It does not, insofar as natural consciousness stiffens in its opining and passes off its true, untested, as the absolutely true. The third verb's ambiguity maps onto the two moments, real and natural, of the one consciousness.

(182–83) With this ambiguity consciousness betrays the basic trait of its essence: to be already something it at the same time is not yet. Being in the sense of consciousness means dwelling in the not-yet of the already, in such a way that the already is present in the not-yet. Presencing is in itself a self-referral into the already; it sets out on the way toward it and forms the way itself. The being of consciousness consists in moving itself (Heidegger writes *be-wegt*, "makes its way"). The being that Hegel thinks as experience has the basic trait of movement. Hegel begins the sentence that states the essence of experience with "The dialectical movement." It would be the worst misreading of the text to think that Hegel merely characterizes the exposition as a kind of experience, to stress that it must keep to the phenomena and avoid degenerating into construction. The experience to be thought here does not belong to the exposition as a mark of its character; the exposition belongs to the essence of experience, which is the appearing of what appears as such. The exposition belongs to appearing as the movement in which consciousness realizes its reality. The temporal structure ("already" and "not yet") recalls the analysis of care in *Being and Time*, and *be-wegt* ties being to *Weg* (way), answering the riddle of 164: the way is the absolute's own way. The "worst misreading" is the methodological one dismissed at 142, and Heidegger now reverses the relation. The exposition does not have experience as its method; experience has the exposition as one of its moments.

(183) Hegel stresses that the movement is "dialectical," a term used only here in the piece and nowhere explained. We therefore try to understand the dialectical from what has emerged about the nature of consciousness. One might explain it by the unity of thesis, antithesis, and synthesis, or by the negation of negation. But everything thetic has its essence in consciousness, where negativity too is grounded insofar as it is understood from negation, and the essence of consciousness is to be determined only by unfolding its nature. Likewise the problem whether dialectic is a method of knowing or belongs to the objectively real may be set aside. It is a pseudo-problem as long as it is not determined what the reality of the real consists in, how it rests in the being of consciousness, and what this being is. Discussions of dialectic resemble trying to explain the spring of a source from the stagnant water of sewage. The way to the source may still be long, but we must try to point in its direction with Hegel's help. Heidegger rules out both textbook schemas: the triad (a Fichtean formula Hegel himself rejected) and negation of negation, since both presuppose consciousness and so cannot explain it. The method-or-reality debate (Engels, the Marxist and neo-Hegelian discussions) is dismissed as a pseudo-problem because it lacks an ontology of reality. The sewage image is unusually harsh.

(183–84) Consciousness as consciousness is its movement, since it is the comparison between ontic-pre-ontological and ontological knowing. The former lays claim to the latter; the latter claims to be the former's truth. Between (*dia*) the one and the other occurs the speaking of these claims, a *legein*. In this conversation consciousness speaks its truth to itself; the *dialegein* is a *dialegesthai*. But the conversation does not stay in one shape of consciousness; it goes through (*dia*) the whole domain of shapes, and in going through gathers itself into the truth of its essence. The thorough gathering, *dialegein*, is a self-gathering (*dialegesthai*). Heidegger's positive account of dialectic rests on the two senses of *dia* (between and through) and the two senses of *legein* (to speak and to gather). Dialectic is a conversation between natural and real knowing and a gathering through all the shapes. The middle voice *dialegesthai* makes consciousness both speaker and gathered.

(184) Consciousness is consciousness as the conversation between natural and real knowing, a conversation that accomplishes the gathering of its essence through its shapes. Insofar as the formation of consciousness happens both as self-gathering conversation and as self-uttering gathering, its movement is dialectical. A marginal note asks: "In what sense does this also hold for the 'Logic'? The conversation between what?" This is the definition proper. The marginal note registers a limit. In the *Logic* there is no natural consciousness, so it is unclear who the conversation partners would be; the dialogical reading may fit the *Phenomenology* only.

(184) Only from the dialogical character of ontic-ontological consciousness can the thetic character of its representing be extracted. So characterizing dialectic by thesis, antithesis, and synthesis is always correct but always derivative. The same holds for interpreting the dialectical as in-finite negativity, which rests on the self-gathering of the conversational shapes of consciousness toward the absolute concept, as which consciousness is in its accomplished truth. Positing and negating presuppose the originally dialectical appearing of consciousness but never constitute its nature. The dialectical can be neither explained logically from the position and negation of representing nor established ontically as a particular activity within real consciousness. As a mode of appearing it belongs to being, which unfolds as the beingness of beings from presencing. Hegel does not grasp experience dialectically; he thinks the dialectical from the essence of experience, which is the beingness of beings that, as *subiectum*, is determined from subjectity. The order of grounding is fixed: experience (being as appearing) grounds dialectic, which grounds positing and negation. This deliberately inverts the common view of Hegel as a logician of contradiction.

(185) The decisive moment of experience is that the new true object arises for consciousness in it. What matters is the arising of the new object as the arising of truth, not the taking note of an object as something opposite. The object is no longer to be thought as what stands over against representing, but as what arises, against the old object taken as not-yet-true, as the truth of consciousness. Experiencing (*Erfahren*) is the way consciousness, insofar as it is, reaches out (*ausfährt*) toward its concept, as which it is in truth. This reaching out attains, in the appearing true, the appearing of truth, and so arrives in the self-appearing of appearing itself. *Fahren* (to fare, travel) originally means to draw. The carpenter "fares" with a beam in a certain direction; one person "flies" in another's face (*fährt ins Gesicht*), reaching toward them; the herdsman "drives" (*fährt*) the herd up the mountain, guiding it to arrive. Experiencing is a reaching-out that attains and arrives. It is a mode of presencing, of being. Through experience, appearing consciousness, as appearing, comes to presence with itself in its own presencing. Experience gathers consciousness into the gathering of its essence. The etymology of *Er-fahren* gives Heidegger his definition: experience is a traversing that reaches out and arrives, a movement of presencing. The examples (carpenter, face, herdsman) show three aspects of *fahren*: directed movement, reaching toward, and guiding to a goal. The "new object" is reinterpreted as truth arising, not as one more object. Heidegger develops the same etymology in "The Nature of Language" (GA 12), where "to undergo an experience with language" is analyzed through *fahren*.

(185) Experience is the mode of presence of what is present that presences in representing itself. The new object that arises each time in the history of formation is not some true thing or being but the truth of the true, the being of beings, the appearing of what appears: experience. According to the closing sentence of Hegel's section on experience, the new object is nothing other than experience itself. Heidegger reads Hegel's "it is the experience made concerning it" as an identity: the new object *is* experience. Each new object is a stage of being becoming manifest to itself.

(185–86) The *essentia* of the *ens* in its *esse* is presence, which presences as presentation. Since the *ens*, the *subiectum*, has become the *res cogitans*, presentation is in itself representing (*vor-stellend*), that is, re-presentation. What Hegel thinks in "experience" first says what the *res cogitans* is as the *subiectum co-agitans*. Experience is the presentation of the absolute subject that presences in re-presentation and so absolves itself; it is the subjectity of the absolute subject; as the presentation of absolute re-presentation it is the parousia of the absolute; it is the absoluteness of the absolute, its appearing in absolvent self-appearing. Everything depends on thinking experience so named as the being of consciousness. But being means presencing, which shows itself as appearing, now the appearing of knowing. In the being that experience is, representing in the sense of presenting belongs as the character of appearing. Even where Hegel uses "experience" in the ordinary sense of empirical knowledge, he heeds the moment of presencing above all; he understands experience (Preface, Hoffmeister p. 14) as "attention to the present as such." Hegel deliberately says not merely attention to something present but to the present in its presencing. Every thread so far (presence, presentation, re-presentation, *res cogitans* as *co-agitans*, absolvence, parousia) is gathered into one chain of equivalences ending in "experience." The Preface citation shows that even Hegel's ordinary use of "experience" carries the ontological sense, "the present *as such*."

(186–87) Experience concerns what is present in its presencing. But since consciousness is by testing itself, it reaches out toward its presencing in order to arrive in it. It belongs to the appearing of appearing knowledge to re-present itself in its presence, that is, to present itself. Exposition belongs to experience, to its essence, not as a counterpart that might be missing. So experience is thought in its full essence as the beingness of beings in the sense of the absolute subject only when it comes to light how the exposition of appearing knowledge belongs to appearing as such. The section that follows takes the last step into the essence of experience as the existence (*Dasein*) of the absolute. The question for what follows is posed: how exposition, which is "our" business, belongs to the absolute's own appearing. "Dasein of the absolute" is Hegel's sense of existence, its presence-there, which the essay has been calling parousia.

### The Reversal of Consciousness and Our Contribution

(187) This section starts from natural consciousness's notion of experience, which Hegel's concept runs against. Metaphysically thought experience is therefore inaccessible to natural consciousness; it is the beingness of beings and so nowhere to be found as a component among beings. If we have a good experience with a tool in use, we have it on the object to which the tool is applied. If we have a bad experience with a person, we have it on certain occasions and circumstances where he was to prove himself. We make the experience with an object not on it but on another object that we bring in and engage. In ordinary experience (*experiri*, to try) we view the object to be tested from the conditions other objects place it under, and from them we learn how it stands. If we must change our previous representations of it, the change comes to us from the newly introduced objects. The untruth of the old object shows itself on the new one, which we represent directly and set in comparison with the familiar one. In the experience that consciousness itself is, it is the reverse. Heidegger gives the ordinary concept its due: experience as *experiri*, testing by trial, where verdicts on one thing come from others. Its structure is lateral, from object to object, and never reaches objectness.

(187–88) When we represent the objectness of an object, the truth of a true, experience is made on the old object, so that the new one, objectness, arises precisely on the old object. On it and from it the new object rises into its stand. So we must not go away to another directly present object but first expressly go into the old one. Natural consciousness represents what it represents, and its representing, immediately as beings, without heeding the being it already represents. If it is to perceive the being of beings, it must not only stay with beings but go into them so as to go back to what is already in their representedness for it. Insofar as the appearing of what appears comes to the fore, consciousness has in a sense already given up ordinary representing and turned back from what appears to appearing, and so reversed itself. Ontological experience is vertical: it goes deeper into the same thing toward its being. This is the transcendental turn of Kant, renamed as a "going back" within the object, and it defines the reversal (*Umkehrung*) as a turning-back from beings to their being.

(188) In the self-appearing of appearing, a "reversal of consciousness itself" holds sway; it is the basic trait of the experience of consciousness. It is even "our contribution." What presents itself to consciousness in this reversal is not "for consciousness," not for natural consciousness. What presents itself in the reversal is not "for it," the consciousness "that we observe," but "for us," who observe. Who are the "we"? The question deferred since 168 is now posed directly. The reversal and our contribution are identified, and the "we" becomes the question of the human relation to being.

(188–89) The "we" are those who, in the reversal of natural consciousness, leave it in its opining but at the same time look expressly at the appearing of what appears. This seeing that looks expressly at appearing is the looking-on in which skepsis carries itself out, a skepsis that has looked ahead to the absoluteness of the absolute and provided itself with it in advance. What shines in self-accomplishing skepticism shows itself "for us," for those who, thinking toward the beingness of beings, are already provided with being. The reversal at work in skepsis is the passage by which consciousness provides itself with appearing itself. What shows itself to the one so provided belongs by its content to consciousness and is "for it." But the way it shows itself, as appearing, is the look of what appears, its *eidos*, which forms all that appears, sets it into view, and shapes it: the *morphē*, the *forma*. Hegel calls it "the formal." This is never "for it," for directly representing natural consciousness; insofar as the formal is for it, it is always only as object, never as objectness. The formal, the beingness of beings, is "for us," who in the reversal look not straight at what appears but at its appearing. The reversal, a reversal of representing, does not swerve from direct representing onto a side path; within natural representing it goes into that from which direct representing first receives what it perceives as present. The "we" are defined ontologically, not anthropologically: they are those provided with being (*mit dem Sein versehen*), the word play on *versehen* (to provide, to oversee) and *vorsehen* (to foresee) continuing the account of skepsis. Hegel's "the formal" is translated back through *forma* and *morphē* to *eidos*, the Platonic look. The "for us" therefore sees the being of what appears, which is the Greek theme in modern dress. The reversal is not a detour but an entry into what natural representing already relies on.

(189) In the reversal we go into what no natural consciousness gets behind; we see what "goes on behind its back," to which the reversal itself belongs. Through it the appearing of what appears enters the exposition. The reversal first turns and sets experience into the exposition; through it the experience of consciousness is "raised into the scientific progression." The exposition represents the being of beings; it is the science of *on hēi on*. The reversal, in which looking-on turns to what appears as appearing, brings seeing into the course science takes. Skepsis toward the being of beings sets beings back on themselves, so that they show themselves as beings in the "as." The reversal lets the *hēi* happen expressly with respect to the *on*. So the decisive thing in experience, by which consciousness appears to itself in its appearing, lies with the reversal. And this is "our contribution." The reversal is the explicit enactment of the "as," of *hēi*, and so the founding act of first philosophy. The repetition "this is our contribution" leaves the paradox hanging for the next paragraph: the decisive element seems to be added by us.

(190) But did not Hegel spend all the preceding reflection (especially at 169–71) showing that we must leave out our notions and thoughts so that "pure looking-on" remains? Does he not say explicitly (173) that consciousness tests itself and "a contribution from us" becomes superfluous? By leaving out all contributions, what appears is to show itself of itself in its appearing. But leaving-out does not happen by itself. If any letting is a doing, it is leaving-out (*Weglassen*), and this doing is necessarily the doing of a contribution. Only because the skepsis of self-accomplishing skepticism foresees the being of beings can beings appear freely of themselves and let their appearing shine. The contribution of the reversal is the letting-appear of what appears as such. It does not impose anything alien on experience; it only brings forth expressly from experience what lies in it as the being of consciousness, which by the first proposition is for itself its concept. So the contribution can never cancel the pure looking-on that the exposition requires. In and through it, pure looking-on begins, and so remains within the contribution. Heidegger resolves the apparent contradiction between Hegel's "pure looking-on" and "our contribution." Letting (*lassen*) is a doing, and the highest one: letting beings be (*Seinlassen*, as in "On the Essence of Truth," GA 9). Phenomenological description is never passive. Its contribution is the prior understanding of being that frees beings to show themselves, which is the reversal.

(190–91) In the previous section Hegel said experience is the movement consciousness "exercises" on itself. The exercise is the holding sway of the force as which the absolute's will wills that it presence with us in its absoluteness. The will that the absolute is holds sway as experience, the reaching-out and attaining arrival as which appearing appears to itself. As this arrival (presencing), experience marks the essence of will, which conceals itself together with the essence of experience in the essence of being. The experience to be thought here is neither a mode of knowing nor of willing as ordinarily represented. As experience the absolute's will holds sway to be with us, to appear for us as what appears. For us what appears presents itself in its appearing insofar as we perform the contribution of the reversal. The contribution thus wills the absolute's will; it is itself what the absoluteness of the absolute wills. The reversal adds nothing self-seeking from our side to the absolute. It sets us back into our essence, which consists in being present within the absolute's parousia; for us this means to present the parousia. The exposition of experience is willed from the essence of experience as what belongs to it. The contribution brings to light that and how we, in looking-on, are suited (*zugeartet*) to the absoluteness of the absolute. The thesis that experience is the essence of will places Hegel in the line that ends in Nietzsche's will to power (the next essay in *Holzwege*). Will is not a faculty but the name for being as self-gathering presencing. Our contribution is not subjective initiative but what the absolute's will requires. Heidegger's own position on this human role, that man is needed by being, is anticipated here inside a reading of Hegel.

(191–92) Experience is the being of beings. Beings have meanwhile appeared in the character of consciousness and are, as what appears, in re-presentation. If exposition belongs to the essence of experience, if exposition is grounded in the reversal, and if the reversal as our contribution is the enactment of our essential relation to the absoluteness of the absolute, then our essence itself belongs to the absolute's parousia. The reversal is skepsis into absoluteness. It reverses all that appears in its appearing; by providing itself in advance with appearing, it overtakes everything that appears as such, embraces it, and opens the range of the site in which appearing appears to itself. In and through this site the exposition takes its course, constantly going skeptically ahead of itself. In the reversal the exposition has the absoluteness of the absolute before it and so the absolute with it. The reversal opens and bounds the site of the history of the formation of consciousness, and so secures the completeness and progression of experience. Experience goes ahead of itself, comes back to itself in going ahead, unfolds in coming back into the presencing of consciousness, and becomes constant as presencing. The absolved constant presence of consciousness is the being of the absolute. Through the reversal appearing consciousness shows itself in its appearing and only there. What appears externalizes itself into its appearing (*entäußert sich*) and so goes out into the outermost of its being. But it does not thereby leave itself and its essence, nor does the absolute fall through externalization into the emptiness of its weakness. Externalization is rather the holding-in of the fullness of appearing from the strength of the will as which the absolute's parousia holds sway. The externalization of the absolute is its in-wardizing (*Er-innerung*) into the course of the appearing of its absoluteness. It is so little an estrangement into abstraction that through it appearing becomes at home in what appears as such. Two Hegelian terms from the end of the *Phenomenology*, externalization (*Entäußerung*) and recollection (*Er-innerung*, "inwardizing"), are anticipated here. The circularity of experience (going ahead, coming back) yields "constant presence" (*ständige Anwesenheit*), Heidegger's formula for the metaphysical sense of being as permanence. The denial that externalization is alienation answers the young Marx's use of *Entfremdung*.

(192) It is quite another question whether and how far subjectity is a distinct destiny of being, in which the unconcealment of being (not the truth of beings) withdraws and thereby determines an epoch of its own. Within subjectity every being as such becomes an object; everything is a being from and in being made constant (*Beständigung*). If, within the age of subjectity, in which the essence of technology is grounded, nature is set over against consciousness as being, this nature is only beings as the object of modern technological objectification, which attacks the stock of things and of human beings indiscriminately. Heidegger steps outside the exposition. Subjectity is an epoch (*epochē*, a holding-back) of being, in which being's own unconcealment withdraws. "Being made constant" anticipates standing-reserve (*Bestand*) in "The Question Concerning Technology." The remark on nature as counterweight to consciousness targets naturalistic critiques of idealism (including Schelling-inspired ones), which remain inside the objectifying epoch.

(192–93) The reversal first and expressly opens the between (*dia*) within which the conversation between natural consciousness and absolute knowing comes to language. As skepsis into absoluteness it opens at the same time the complete domain through which (*dia*) consciousness gathers its history into accomplished truth and so forms it. The reversal clears the twofold *dia* of the twofold *legesthai*; it forms beforehand the essential play-space for the dialectical character of the movement in which experience accomplishes itself as the being of consciousness. The reversal is the condition of dialectic: it clears (*lichtet*, the verb of the clearing) the space in which the two senses of *dia* and *legesthai* (183–84) can operate.

(193) The reversal of consciousness is the enactment of skepsis, which sees insofar as it has already provided itself with absoluteness and so is provided by it. The having-seen (*vidi*) of skepsis is the knowing of absoluteness. The reversal is the essential center of the knowing as which the exposition unfolds. So the exposition is the course of consciousness itself toward self-appearing in appearing; it is "the way to science." As this way it is itself science, since the way it moves in is movement in the sense of experience. The force holding sway in and as this experience is the absolute's will, which wills itself in its parousia; in this will the way has its necessity. Heidegger prepares Hegel's set-apart sentence by explaining "through this necessity": the necessity is the absolute's will, and the way is science because it is experience.

(193) Hegel sums up the result of the two sections on experience in a sentence he sets apart, the only one, from the running text, thereby gathering all previous sections into the decisive thought. Typographic isolation is read as a hermeneutic signal: the sentence is the hinge of the piece.

(193) The sentence reads: "Through this necessity the way to science is itself already science, and by its content therefore science of the experience of consciousness." Its weight lies in the italicized words, taken up next.

(193–94) The emphasized words together give the title Hegel first set over the work: Science of the Experience of Consciousness. Literarily, the preceding sections are an interpretation of this title. Experience is the appearing of appearing knowledge as appearing; the science of the experience of consciousness exhibits what appears as appearing. What appears is the *on*, the being in the sense of consciousness. The skepsis of the exposition "beholds what is present (in appearing) as present (so), and so beholds what already holds sway in it (in what appears, in its appearing) of itself." The Aristotle formula from 128 returns with parenthetical glosses that translate it into the vocabulary of consciousness. The circle from the opening (128) is closing: Hegel's science of experience is Aristotle's science of being as being in its modern shape.

(194) The exposition provides itself with the force of the will as which the absolute wills its presence (parousia). Aristotle calls the beholding of beings as beings *epistēmē tis*, a certain way in which our seeing and perceiving stand by (*dabei-steht*) what is present as such. As a way of standing by what is constantly present, *epistēmē* is itself a kind of human presencing with the unconcealed present. We mislead ourselves if we translate *epistēmē* as "science" and leave it to chance what we mean by that word. The translation is legitimate only if we understand knowing as having-seen and think having-seen from the seeing that stands before the look of what is present as present and looks at presence itself. From knowledge so thought, Aristotle's *epistēmē tis* keeps, not by chance, its essential relation to what Hegel calls "the science," whose knowing has changed with the change in the presencing of what is present. If "science" is understood only in this sense, the sciences usually so called are science only secondarily. They are at bottom philosophy, but in such a way that they leave their own ground and set up house in what philosophy has opened for them: the domain of *technē*. Heidegger glosses *epistēmē* through *ephistasthai* (to stand over, to stand by), making knowledge a human mode of presence. The positive sciences are placed in the domain of *technē*, the realm of production that has become technology, which ties back to 149–50 and 192.

(195) Aristotle calls this science first philosophy. But it considers not only beings in their beingness but also the being that corresponds purely to beingness, the highest being. This being, the divine (*to theion*), is also called "being" in a strange ambiguity. First philosophy is as ontology also the theology of what truly is; it would more precisely be called "theiology." The science of beings as such is in itself onto-theological. This is Heidegger's first published formulation of the onto-theological constitution of metaphysics, later the title of a 1957 lecture. The coinage "theiology" (from *theion*, the divine, rather than *theos*, god) separates metaphysical theology from the theology of faith.

(195) Accordingly Hegel calls the exposition of appearing knowledge not "the" science but "science of the experience of consciousness," only a part of the science. That is why "First Part" stands over the title. The science of the experience of consciousness points in itself to the other part. The other part is no more subordinate in rank than theology is to ontology within first philosophy, nor superior, nor coordinate; each in its way is the same. Talk of first and second parts remains external, but not accidental, since from Plato and Aristotle to Nietzsche the ground of the unity of the onto-theological essence of metaphysics stays so hidden that it is not even asked about. Instead ontology and theology waver, depending on the respect, as to which is the very first and proper science within first philosophy. For Hegel the science of the experience of consciousness, the ontology of what truly is in its existence (*Dasein*), refers to the other part as "science proper." The structure of Hegel's system mirrors the structure of metaphysics. The *Phenomenology* is the ontology and the *Logic* the theiology, whose unity remains unthought. The wavering between metaphysics as general (ontology) and as special (theology) runs through the tradition, from Aristotle's *Metaphysics* E 1 to the scholastic division of *metaphysica generalis* and *specialis*.

### Science, Phenomenology, and the Parousia of the Absolute

(195–96) This last section of the piece opens the view onto this connection. It shows itself only if we keep in view that experience is the beingness of beings that presence as consciousness in its shapes. For the Greek thinkers, ever since the *on* rose as *physis*, the presence of what is present, the *ousia* of the *on*, was already *phainesthai*, self-showing appearing. Accordingly the many present things were thought as what is simply taken up and accepted in its appearing, the *dokounta*; *doxa* takes the present immediately, whereas *noein* takes it as present and attends to it in its presencing. Because the *on* means ambiguously both what is present and the presencing, it stands by essential necessity and equiprimordially in relation to *noein* and *doxa*. Heidegger repeats almost verbatim the account at 176 in order to make the Greek beginning the measure for Hegel's end: appearing has been the meaning of presence from the start.

(196–97) The being of what is known in certainty also has the basic trait of presencing; it is as appearing. But in the presencing of knowing, of the *subiectum* as *res cogitans*, appearing is no longer the self-showing of the *idea* as *eidos* but of the *idea* as *perceptio*; it is presencing in the manner of presentation in the domain of representation. The appearing of appearing knowledge is the immediate presencing of consciousness, and this presencing is as experience. With experience the absolute, spirit, arrives in the unfolded "whole realm of its truth." But the moments of its truth are the shapes of consciousness, which in the course of experience have laid aside everything that seemed true only for natural consciousness, insofar as it was in its history only for it. When experience is accomplished, the appearing of what appears has reached pure shining, as which the absolute is present absolutely with itself and is essence itself. From this pure shining holds sway the force that exercises the movement of experience on consciousness. The force of the absolute at work in experience "drives consciousness on to its true existence," where existence (*Existenz*) means presencing in the manner of self-appearing. At this point the pure appearing of the absolute coincides with its essence. The change from *idea* as *eidos* (the look) to *idea* as *perceptio* (representation) compresses the Greek-to-modern transition (cf. "The Age of the World Picture"). The coincidence of appearance and essence in Hegel's closing section is read as the completed parousia.

(197) The parousia is the presence in which the absolute is with us and at the same time, as this, with itself. So at this point the exposition of appearing also coincides with "the science of spirit proper." The science of appearing knowledge leads and falls into science proper, which exhibits how the absolute presences to itself in its absoluteness. Science proper is the *Science of Logic*. The name is taken from the tradition, where logic counts as the knowledge of the concept. But the concept, as which consciousness is for itself its concept, now names the absolute's absolute self-conceiving in its own absolute grip on itself (*Ergriffenheit*). The logic of this concept is the ontological theiology of the absolute. It exhibits not the absolute's parousia, as the science of experience does, but absoluteness in its parousia to itself. The *Logic* is identified as the theiology. The pun on *Begriff* (concept) and *Ergriffenheit* (being gripped) suggests that the absolute concept is seized by itself. The parousia has two directions, "with us" (the *Phenomenology*) and "with itself" (the *Logic*), and these are the two parts of the system.

(197–98) In the title "Science of the Experience of Consciousness" the word experience stands, emphasized, in the middle, mediating consciousness and science, as the matter itself requires. Experience, as the being of consciousness, is in itself the reversal by which consciousness presents itself in its appearing; in exhibiting, experience is science. But natural representing understands the title directly and only to mean that science has experience as its object, and that this in turn is experience of consciousness. Yet the title heads the work that carries out the reversal of consciousness by exhibiting it, and the reversal reverses natural consciousness. So the title remains un-understood while it is read in natural consciousness's habit. The two genitives "of experience" and "of consciousness" are not objective but subjective genitives. Consciousness, not science, is the subject that is in the manner of experience; and experience is the subject of science. On the other hand the objective genitive keeps its sense, but only because the subjective genitive holds. Strictly, neither has priority. Both name the subject-object relation of the absolute subject in its subjectity. In view of this relation, which has its essence in experience, we must think the title each time through the middle of the mediating word both backward and forward. The grammar of the title becomes ontology. Read as subjective genitives, the title says that consciousness experiences and experience knows; the work is not a science *about* experience but science as experience's own self-exhibition. Reading "backward and forward" through the middle word enacts the reversal on the title itself.

(198) In both senses the genitives name the relation the reversal uses without ever thinking it expressly: the relation of being to beings as the relation of beings to being. The dialectical movement settles in the site that the reversal opens, but that as the open of this relation is precisely concealed. The skeptical dialogue between natural and absolute consciousness looks through this site in anticipation of the absoluteness of the absolute. Dialectical skepsis is the essence of speculative philosophy. The genitives in the title are neither merely subjective, nor merely objective, nor a coupling of both; they belong to the dialectical-speculative genitive, which appears in the title only because it governs in advance the language in which the experience of consciousness comes to speech when it accomplishes its exhibition. This is the critical crux of the whole essay. Hegel uses the relation of being and beings (the "as," the reversal, the subjective-objective genitive) without thinking it as such; the site the reversal opens is at once opened and concealed. Heidegger's own question, the difference itself, lives in this concealment. "Dialectical-speculative genitive" names Hegel's grammar of mutual mediation; Heidegger's later analysis of the genitive in "the truth of being" belongs to a different, non-dialectical register.

(198–99) Set off by a typographic break, the next paragraph turns to the history of the title. The first-chosen title "Science of the Experience of Consciousness" was dropped while the work was being printed, but the piece that explains it remained. The title was replaced by "Science of the Phenomenology of Spirit," so the remaining piece, which nowhere speaks of a phenomenology of spirit, becomes the proper interpretation of the new title. This also appears in the overall title under which the work came out in 1807: "System of Science. First Part, the Phenomenology of Spirit." When the work reappeared unchanged shortly after Hegel's death, as the second volume of the collected works (1832), the title was only "Phenomenology of Spirit." Behind the inconspicuous loss of the article "the" lies a decisive change in Hegel's thinking and in his manner of communicating it. The change concerns the system, begins soon after the publication, and was probably occasioned and reinforced by his move to teaching at the Nuremberg Gymnasium; the schoolroom then shaped his later university teaching too. The philological facts (the replaced title, the article dropped in 1832) are put to work. "The" phenomenology names an event, the absolute's parousia; "phenomenology" without the article names a discipline. Heidegger's hypothesis about Nuremberg is that pedagogy turned Hegel's system into a school system, which the *Encyclopedia* confirms (202).

(199–200) The overall title "System of Science" is, at the time of the first edition, dialectically-speculatively ambiguous. It does not mean the sciences arranged in groups in a thought-up order, nor philosophy as science presented in its coherence. It means that science is in itself the absolute organization of the absoluteness of the absolute. The subjectity of the subject is such that, knowing itself, it arranges itself into the completeness of its structure. This self-arranging is the mode of being in which subjectity is. "System" is the coming-together (*syn-histanai*) of the absolute, which gathers itself into its absoluteness and through this standing-together is made constant in its own presence. Science is the subject of the system, not its object, but in such a way that, belonging to subjectity, it helps make up the absoluteness of the absolute. Around 1807 science is for Hegel the onto-theiological knowledge of what truly is as being. It unfolds its whole twofold, into the "Science of the Phenomenology of Spirit" and the "Science of Logic." At that time the *Logic* is absolute theiology, not ontology; ontology had unfolded as the "Science of the Experience of Consciousness." The *Phenomenology* is "first science," the *Logic* the proper science, within first philosophy as the truth of beings as such, which is the essence of metaphysics. But Hegel, no more than Kant before or the late Schelling after him, mastered the long-entrenched power of the didactic systematics of school metaphysics. Nietzsche rails against this systematics only because his thinking must remain within the essential, onto-theiological system of metaphysics. "System" is read from *systasis* as the absolute's own self-assembly, not as an arrangement of doctrines; Heidegger's 1936 Schelling course (GA 42) develops this. The ascription of ontology to the *Phenomenology* and theiology to the *Logic* is a provocative reversal of the usual view that the *Logic* is Hegel's ontology. The remark on Nietzsche, that his hostility to system is itself systematic, anticipates the next essay.

(200) Why did Hegel give up the first title? We do not know, but we can conjecture. Did he shrink from the word "experience," which he himself had placed emphatically in the middle? The word is now the name of the being of beings; for Kant it named the only possible theoretical knowledge of beings. Did it seem too daring to make the original sense of "to experience" sound again, which Hegel presumably heard with his thinking ear: experiencing as a reaching-out arrival, and this as the mode of presencing, of *einai*, of being? Did it seem too daring to raise this old tone into the fundamental tone of the work's language, even where the word does not occur? At all essential points of its course, in the transitions, it does occur. In the last main part, which exhibits the appearing of consciousness as spirit, it recedes. On the other hand, the Preface written after the work's completion still speaks of the "system of the experience of spirit." Heidegger offers a conjecture, not a finding. The Kantian sense of "experience" (the limit of theoretical cognition) made Hegel's ontological use too provocative. The evidence that the word marks the transitions between shapes and survives in the Preface supports the essay's thesis that experience is the work's ground-tone.

(201) Still, the heading disappears, and with it the word "consciousness" disappears from the title, although consciousness as self-consciousness constitutes the essential domain of the absolute's absoluteness, and although consciousness is the land of modern metaphysics, which has now taken possession of itself and fully surveyed itself as "System of Science." The land image from 128–29 returns at the moment of the land's completion, when its name vanishes from the title. The paradox is pointed: the land is fully possessed, and its name drops away.

(201–02) The heading yields to "Science of the Phenomenology of Spirit," built in exact correspondence; its genitives too must be thought dialectically-speculatively. "Phenomenology," a name already current in school philosophy, replaces "experience." The essence of experience is the essence of phenomenology. The *phainesthai*, the self-appearing of the absolute subject called "spirit," gathers itself in the manner of the conversation between ontic and ontological consciousness. The "-logy" in phenomenology is the *legesthai* in the sense of the ambiguous *dialegesthai* that characterizes the movement as which the experience of consciousness is its being. Phenomenology is the self-gathering of the conversation of spirit's dialogue with its parousia. "Phenomenology" is here the name for the existence (*Dasein*) of spirit; spirit is its subject, not its object. The word means neither a philosophical discipline nor a particular kind of research that describes the given. But because the absolute's self-gathering into its parousia requires exhibition by essence, the determination of being science already belongs to the essence of phenomenology, not as a representing of spirit but as the existence, the presence of spirit. So the shortened title "The Phenomenology of Spirit," rightly thought, does not fall into vagueness; it forces thinking into the last possible gathering. "The Phenomenology of Spirit" means: the parousia of the absolute in its holding sway. A decade after its appearance, in the school system of the *Encyclopedia* (1817), "phenomenology" has fallen to a narrowly bounded part of the philosophy of spirit; as in the eighteenth century (Lambert), it is again the name of a discipline, standing between anthropology and psychology. *Phainesthai* and *legesthai* are read back into "phenomenology," as in *Being and Time* § 7, but here they name the absolute's self-appearing and self-gathering rather than a method. Heidegger's denial that phenomenology is "research that describes the given" distances this Hegelian phenomenology from Husserl's, and implicitly from his own early methodological concept.

(202) What then is the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, if it is the experience of consciousness? It is self-accomplishing skepticism. Experience is the conversation between natural consciousness and absolute knowing. Natural consciousness is spirit as it historically exists in each case in its time. But this spirit is no ideology; as subjectity it is the actuality of the actual. The historical spirits remain each time inwardized (*er-innert*), in themselves, to themselves. Absolute knowing, however, is the exhibition of the appearing of existing spirit; it accomplishes "the organization" of the constitution of being of the realm of spirits. The course of the conversation gathers itself into the site that it first reaches by going (*ergeht*), so as to pass through it, settle in it, and, having arrived, be present in it. The arriving course of the conversation is the way of despair, on which consciousness each time loses its not-yet-true and sacrifices it to the appearing of truth. In the completion of the conversation of "self-accomplishing skepticism" the word is spoken: "It is accomplished" (*es ist vollbracht*, John 19:30). It is spoken at the site of the way on which consciousness dies its own death, into which it is torn by the force of the absolute. At the end of the work Hegel calls the *Phenomenology of Spirit* "the Golgotha of absolute spirit." The denial that spirit is "ideology" rejects the Marxist reading of historical shapes of consciousness as superstructure. Christ's last word, "it is accomplished," echoes "self-accomplishing (*sich vollbringend*) skepticism." Hegel's closing image of Golgotha (*Schädelstätte*, "place of skulls") becomes the site at which the way ends.

(202–03) The Science of the Phenomenology of Spirit is the theology of the absolute with respect to its parousia in the dialectical-speculative Good Friday. Here the absolute dies. God is dead. This says anything but "there is no God." The *Science of Logic*, however, is the science of the absolute originally present to itself, in its self-knowing as the absolute concept; it is the theology of the absoluteness of the absolute before creation. Both theologies are ontology, are worldly; they think the worldliness of the world, where world means beings as a whole, whose basic trait is subjectity. The world so understood determines its beings as present in the representation that represents the absolute. But the science of absolute knowing is not the worldly theology of the world because it secularizes Christian and ecclesial theology, but because it belongs to the essence of ontology. This is older than any Christian theology, which must itself first be actual for a process of secularization to begin with it. The theology of the absolute is the knowledge of beings as beings that, with the Greek thinkers, brings its onto-theological essence to light and follows it without ever following it into its ground. In the language of absolute science it emerges that Christian theology, in what it knows and how, is metaphysics. Three claims stand out. "God is dead" is Hegel's "speculative Good Friday" (from *Faith and Knowledge*), and the phrase links forward to the Nietzsche essay that follows in *Holzwege*. The *Logic* is "God before creation," Hegel's own phrase for the *Logic*'s content. Against the secularization thesis (Löwith's *From Hegel to Nietzsche*, 1941, is a likely target), Heidegger argues that the worldly theology of the absolute is not derived from Christianity, since onto-theology is Greek and older; Christian theology is itself metaphysics.

(203) The sentence "the experience of consciousness is self-accomplishing skepticism" and the sentence "the phenomenology is the Golgotha of absolute spirit" join the completion of the work with its beginning. But the essential thing about the *Phenomenology* is not the work as a thinker's achievement but the work as the actuality of consciousness itself. Because phenomenology is experience, the beingness of beings, it is the gathering of self-appearing toward appearing from the shining of the absolute. The Introduction's sentence on skepticism and the last sentence of the work (Golgotha) are made to say the same. The work is not a book about consciousness but the actuality of consciousness, which matches the reading of the genitives at 197–98.

(203–04) This gathering self-collecting (*Sich-zusammen-nehmen*) is the unspoken essence of will. The will wills itself in the parousia of the absolute with us. "The phenomenology" is itself the being according to which the absolute is with us in and for itself. This being wills, insofar as will is its essence. It remains to be thought how being comes to this essence. The equation of being and will is stated outright, and then held open with a question that points beyond Hegel to the history in which being becomes will (Schelling, Nietzsche). The "gathering" in *legein* and in *Wille* (as the "doing that gathers itself into its essence," 141) is now the same.

(204) "Being with us" belongs to the absoluteness of the absolute. Without this "with us" the absolute would be the solitary that could not appear to itself in what appears, could not rise into its unconcealment, and without this rising (*physis*) would not be alive (*zōē*). Experience is the movement of the conversation between natural and absolute knowing; it is, from the unifying unity as which it gathers, both. It is the nature of natural consciousness, historical in the contingency of its appearing shapes, and it is the self-conceiving of these shapes in the organization of their appearing. So the work concludes in the sentence: "both together, comprehended history, form the recollection and the Golgotha of absolute spirit, the actuality, truth, and certainty of its throne, without which it would be lifeless and alone." In its absoluteness the absolute needs the throne as the height on which it settles without lowering itself. The final sentence of the *Phenomenology* is read as the fulfilment of the subordinate clause at the start of the Introduction. The absolute needs to be "with us" as its life, its *physis* and *zōē*. "Throne" is height without condescension, and the absolute's need of us is made explicit next.

(204) The parousia of the absolute happens as phenomenology. Experience is the being according to which the absolute wills to be with us. Because the exhibition that essentially belongs to experience has nothing to exhibit but phenomenology in the sense of the parousia, what the work ends in is already named at the end of the opening section, with which it begins: the parousia. That the absolute is already with us in and for itself, and wills to be, is mentioned only inconspicuously in a subordinate clause; in the completion of the work the subordinate clause has become the sole main clause. The "with us" has unveiled itself as the "not without us." The essay's interpretive arc closes: the subordinate clause of 116 is the work's main thesis. The shift from "with us" to "not without us" makes the human being necessary to the absolute's presence, the Hegelian prototype of Heidegger's own claim that being needs (*braucht*) man.

(204–05) In the "with us" at the beginning, the essence of the "us" is still unthought; in the "not without us" at the end of the work it has been determined. We are those who skeptically heed (*beachten*) the being of beings expressly and so properly respect (*achten*) it. The "we" from 168 and 188 is answered: those who attend to being. The play *beachten / achten* (to heed, to esteem) gives the human role an ethical tone without leaving the ontological register.

(205) The ring has closed. The last word of the work echoes back into its beginning. For the sixteen sections of the piece usually called the Introduction to the *Phenomenology* are already its proper beginning. The circle (Hegel's figure of the system as a circle of circles) is applied to the work: end and beginning coincide, and so the "Introduction" is not an antechamber.

(205–06) The heading "Introduction" does not appear in the original edition of 1807. Only in the table of contents added later is the piece following the Preface listed under "Introduction," presumably from the embarrassment the constraint of a table of contents brought with it. In substance the piece is no introduction, which is presumably why the far longer Preface was written for preparation only after the work was finished. The piece of sixteen sections is no introduction because it cannot be one, and it cannot be because there is no introduction to the *Phenomenology*, and there is none because there can be none. The *Phenomenology* is the parousia of the absolute, the parousia is the being of beings, and for man there is no introduction to the being of beings, because man's essence, in the escort (*Geleit*) of being, is this escort itself. Insofar as the absolute's "being with us" holds sway, we are already in the parousia and can never be introduced into it from anywhere else. But how are we in it? According to the habit of natural consciousness, to which everything appears as though all that is present were side by side. The absolute too usually appears to it as something beside the rest; even what is above ordinary beings is, for natural consciousness, opposite and above it, a "beside" in the upward direction, beside which we ourselves then are. Following the pull of its representing, natural consciousness dwells with beings and does not turn to being, by which it is nevertheless drawn in advance, even for that very pull toward beings. Yet when made aware of being, natural consciousness assures us that being is something abstract. It passes off as something drawn off (*Abgezogenes*) that by which consciousness is drawn (*gezogen*) into its own essence. No greater inversion of its essence is possible for natural consciousness than this opinion. The philological fact (no heading in 1807) supports a philosophical thesis: there is no introduction (*Ein-leitung*, leading-in) into being because the human being is already led (*Geleit*, escort) by it. The play on *abstrakt* (*abgezogen*, drawn off) and *angezogen* (drawn toward) diagnoses the "abstractness" of being as the deepest perversion of natural consciousness, the forgetting of being in its everyday form.

(206) Measured against this inversion, the perversities in which natural consciousness roams grow pale, such that it tries to remove one perversity by organizing another, without thinking of the real inversion. So the abiding need is that consciousness turn back from this not-turning-to the being of beings and turn toward the appearing of what appears. Natural consciousness cannot be introduced to where it already is. But in the reversal it is not to leave its dwelling amidst beings either; it is to take it over expressly in its truth. The reversal (*Umkehrung*) answers the inversion (*Verkehrung*). It is not an escape from beings into a beyond but an appropriation of our dwelling among beings, which links this ending to the "dwelling" thought of the 1950s. "Organizing" one perversity against another is the technological response to technology's ills.

(206) Literarily, we can take the sixteen sections as the explanation of the title that was then dropped. Thought from the matter, however, it is not a book title but the work itself, and not even the work but what it exhibits: experience, phenomenology as what essentially unfolds in the parousia of the absolute. And again not so that we take note of it, but so that we ourselves be in the experience that is part of our own being, and be in the old, traditional sense of being: present with what is present. Each level of reading is surpassed: from title to work to matter to our own being in it. "Being" in the old sense, "present with" (*anwesend bei*), returns to *par-ousia*: our being is to be present with the present.

(206–07) The piece of sixteen sections sends natural consciousness back (*Zurückweisung*) into appropriating its dwelling. This happens through the reversal of consciousness, through which consciousness enters experience, as which the parousia of the absolute truly happens. Natural consciousness can be brought back from its habitual representing and directed into experience only if the sending-back constantly links up with the representations natural consciousness immediately forms of what meets it with the claim of absolute knowing. This linking-up with the opinion of natural representing marks the style of the sections of the piece and their coherence. Heidegger explains the Introduction's peculiar style, its constant starting from opinions (instrument, medium, doubt, testing): the piece meets natural consciousness where it is, which is the only way to send it back into what it already is. *Zurückweisung* means both "rejection" and "referring back," and both senses are intended.

(207) The piece with which the proper body of the work begins is the beginning of the skepsis that pervades self-accomplishing skepticism. To begin the skepsis means to carry out the having-seen into the absoluteness of the absolute and to hold to it. The piece is the indispensable occasion to induce natural consciousness to release in itself the knowledge in which it already is, insofar as it is itself its concept. Only if we have already carried out the reversal of consciousness, in which spirit's appearing turns toward us, does what appears presence as appearing "for us." "For us" does not mean "relative to us," the ordinary representers; it means "in itself," appearing from the absoluteness of the absolute into the pure site of its appearing. This is the essay's final guard against relativism. Hegel's "for us" is not a perspective of finite subjects but the standpoint of the in-itself, the site opened by the reversal. The piece is the beginning of the work, not a propaedeutic.

(207–08) Only if the piece induces us into the reversal as the proper beginning of the exposition can the exposition of the experience of consciousness begin. It begins absolutely with the absoluteness of the absolute, with the utmost force of the will of the parousia, with the utmost externalization of the absolute in its appearing. To look ahead into this appearing we must take what appears as it appears and keep our opinions and thoughts away from it. But this meeting and leaving-aside is a doing that draws its security and endurance solely from the contribution of the reversal. Our contribution is to go toward the appearing of appearing consciousness, which in the parousia has already come toward us, skeptically, that is, with seeing eyes, in order to be in the course as which experience is the phenomenology of the absolute. The paradox of the beginning is resolved. Sense-certainty, the poorest shape, is the absolute's utmost externalization, and to receive it purely requires the full contribution of the reversal. The image of going toward what has already come toward us restates the parousia as encounter.

(208) The exposition begins by letting "sense-certainty" appear absolutely. Heidegger quotes the opening of the first chapter: "The knowledge which is at first or immediately our object can be none other than that which is itself immediate knowledge, knowledge of the immediate or of what is. We must behave just as immediately or receptively, and so change nothing in it as it offers itself and keep conceiving away from apprehending." The quotation demonstrates the maxim of pure looking-on (171) and the receptive "contribution" (190) at work in the text's first step. The words "we" and "receptively" carry the weight.

(208) When the exposition of sense-certainty's appearing is complete, there has arisen, from what sense-certainty takes for the being and the true, its being as the new object, the truth of certainty, which certainty is self-knowing self-consciousness. Heidegger quotes the opening of the chapter "The Truth of the Certainty of Oneself": "In the previous modes of certainty the true is for consciousness something other than itself. But the concept of this true vanishes in the experience of it; as the object was immediately in itself, the being of sense-certainty, the concrete thing of perception, the force of the understanding, it proves rather not to be in truth, but this in-itself turns out to be a way in which it is only for an other; the concept of it sublates itself in the actual object, or the first immediate representation in experience, and certainty was lost in truth." The essay ends without comment, letting Hegel's text show what the commentary has argued: the word "experience" marks the transition to self-consciousness, and the passage enacts the schema Hegel gave for experience, in which the in-itself proves to be only for an other. The first three shapes (sense-certainty, perception, understanding) yield, as their "new object," the being of consciousness itself, self-consciousness, which Heidegger has called subjectity. Ending on Hegel's words rather than his own fits the essay's account of its method: the language of thinking calls another's thought into the open in order to free it into its own essence (155).

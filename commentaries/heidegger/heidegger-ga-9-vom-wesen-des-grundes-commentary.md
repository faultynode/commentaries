# Commentary on Heidegger, "On the Essence of Ground" (GA 9: 123–175)

Page references are to GA 9 (*Wegmarken*). The treatise appeared in 1929 in the Festschrift for Husserl's seventieth birthday; it grows out of the second main part of the Marburg Leibniz course of summer 1928 (GA 26, §§ 10–14), which should be kept in view throughout. Dasein is retained untranslated; everything else is rendered in English, with the German in parentheses where the term carries weight. Ground (*Grund*) covers what English splits into reason, cause, basis and foundation; the principle of reason (*Satz vom Grund*) is Leibniz's *principium rationis sufficientis*. The marginal notes from Heidegger's own copies (1929, 1931, 1949, 1967) are folded in only where they bear on the 1929 sense, and are marked as later self-readings, never read back into the text.

Several misreadings recur in the reception and are guarded against throughout. Transcendence here is not the transcendence of an object beyond consciousness or of God beyond the world; it is Dasein's surpassing of beings toward world. World is neither the totality of beings nor the community of persons, and it is not the equipmental context of *Being and Time* §§ 15–18. Freedom is not a faculty of the will, nor spontaneity as a kind of causality; the "will" of Part III is no act of willing. Grounding is not causal explanation, and the three ways of grounding are neither species of a genus nor a revision of Aristotle's four causes. "Dasein exists for the sake of itself" asserts no egoism. The abyss (*Ab-grund*) of freedom does not make particular choices arbitrary. Ontological truth is not correspondence, and the ontological difference is not a distinction the understanding draws. Finally, the treatise is not an anthropocentrism or a subjectivism of world-constitution, a charge Heidegger anticipates in note 59.

## Preface to the Third Edition (1949)

(123) Heidegger dates the treatise to 1928 and pairs it with the inaugural lecture "What Is Metaphysics?" written at the same time: the lecture thinks the nothing, the treatise names the ontological difference. He presents the two as complementary approaches to one matter, deliberately kept apart. Written after the turn, the preface reads the 1929 text as a step toward the question of being rather than a chapter of fundamental ontology. A note added in 1967 points to the self-critique in *The Principle of Reason* (1957, pp. 82 ff.), where Heidegger hears the principle no longer as a statement about beings ("nothing is without ground") but as a saying about being ("being and ground: the same"). The later lecture course is the essay's own sequel and correction.

(123) The nothing is the "not" of beings, and so being as experienced from the side of beings. The ontological difference is the "not" between beings and being. Heidegger rules out two reductions at once. Being, as the not of beings, is no *nihil negativum*, no sheer absence; the difference, as the not between beings and being, is no product of a distinction drawn by the understanding (*ens rationis*). The second guard matters for reading the 1929 text, which locates the difference in Dasein's "capacity to distinguish" (135) and so invites exactly the subjectivizing reading the preface now forbids.

(123) The nihilating not of the nothing and the nihilating not of the difference are not identical, but they are "the same": they belong together in the essential unfolding of the being of beings. Heidegger relies on the distinction between empty identity and the sameness of what belongs together, developed later in *Identity and Difference* (1957). A note of 1949 marks the "of" in "being of beings" as the decisive genitive: the difference lives in that grammatical relation. Both texts, he concedes, try to bring this sameness closer to determination without being equal to it.

(123) Heidegger ends by asking whether those who claim to reflect might finally take up a matter that has waited two decades. The complaint targets a reception that read the lecture as nihilism and the treatise as a philosophy of the free subject, and so never asked what joins the nothing to the difference.

## On the Essence of Ground

(124–126) Heidegger opens with Aristotle's summary of the senses of *archē* in *Metaphysics* Δ 1: what all beginnings share is being "the first from which" something is, comes to be, or is known. This yields the three variants of what we call ground: ground of what-being, of that-being, and of being-true. Aristotle also seeks what these "grounds" have in common, and Δ 2 adds the fourfold division of *aition* into underlying matter, essence, source of change, and that-for-the-sake-of-which, a division that has governed the later history of metaphysics and logic. Though all causes count as beginnings, the inner connection between the threefold and the fourfold division, and the principle behind either, stay obscure. Heidegger doubts that the essence of ground can be found by abstracting what the "kinds" of ground have in common, yet he credits the impulse toward a unified clarification. Aristotle himself was not content with the four causes as a mere collection: *Physics* B analyses them, and *Metaphysics* A 3–7 surveys his predecessors and concludes that none of them named a further cause. This framing anticipates the result of Part III (170–171), where the essence of ground turns out to be no genus but a threefold "strewing" of grounding, and where Aristotle's list is said to blur transcendental grounds with ontic causes. A 1929 marginal note adds the later verdict that *archē*, as "first from which", already presupposes being as the presencing of what is constant.

Heidegger then sketches the modern history. Leibniz made the problem familiar as the principle of sufficient reason; Crusius gave it its first monograph (1743), Schopenhauer its last (*On the Fourfold Root*, 1813). If the problem of ground is bound up with the core questions of metaphysics, it must also be alive where it is not treated under that title. Kant seems to show the principle little interest, discussing it explicitly only at the start of his career (*Nova dilucidatio*, 1755) and near its end (the 1790 reply to Eberhard); yet it stands at the centre of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, a claim made good at 136. Schelling's *Freedom* essay (1809) matters as much, and quietly forecasts the essay's own conclusion, where freedom becomes the ground of ground and an abyss. The Kantian and Schellingian cases make it doubtful that the problem of ground coincides with the principle of reason, or is even posed by it. The problem must therefore first be awakened, though discussion of the principle may supply the occasion.

To develop the problem is to win the domain within which the essence of ground can be treated, without claiming to show that essence at a stroke. That domain is transcendence, and the relation runs both ways: the problem of ground in turn determines transcendence more originally. Heidegger adds that every illumination of essence, as finite philosophizing, must also bear witness to the "non-essence" (*Unwesen*) that human knowledge makes of every essence. The German idiom (*Unwesen treiben*, to make mischief) returns at the close (173–174), where the principle of reason itself "makes mischief" with the essence of ground and that distortion is traced to finite freedom. The plan follows: I. The Problem of Ground; II. Transcendence as the Domain of the Question concerning the Essence of Ground; III. On the Essence of Ground.

### I. The Problem of Ground

(126–127) The principle of reason, as a "highest principle", seems to forestall any problem of ground. Heidegger asks whether it is a statement about ground at all. In its usual short form, *nihil est sine ratione*, nothing is without ground; positively, every being has a ground. The principle speaks about beings with regard to something like ground, but leaves undetermined what ground is; it takes this for granted as a self-evident notion. It depends on that unclarified essence a second time: its character as a principle, a *Grund-satz*, the "great principle" in Leibniz's phrase, can only be delimited by reference to what ground is. The principle presupposes ground both in what it says and in its standing as a principle. The 1929 marginalia read the double presupposition as a specific interpretation of being: being as asserted (being-true), as produced-from, and in both as constant presence.

(127) The principle is therefore questionable both in how it posits and in what it posits, provided the essence of ground can and should become a problem beyond an indeterminately general notion. The proviso carries the argument: the questionability of the principle is not shown but conditional on the possibility, still to be established, of making ground a problem at all.

(127–128) The principle sheds no light on ground as such, yet it can serve as the starting point for characterizing the problem. Heidegger sets aside its many interpretations and takes it in the form and role Leibniz first gave it. The historical dispute over whether Leibniz held it to be "logical" or "metaphysical" or both remains fruitless as long as nobody knows what logic and metaphysics are or how they relate. Note 10 names the target: Couturat cited the treatise *Primae veritates* as decisive proof that Leibniz's metaphysics rests entirely on logic. Heidegger uses the same text while rejecting Couturat's reading of it, his Leibniz, and his concept of logic. The treatise, he claims, speaks most sharply against deriving the principle from logic, and against the very question of which discipline has priority; Leibniz unsettles that alternative and Kant shakes it, though without lasting effect. This is the programme of the 1928 course, whose title, *Metaphysical Foundations of Logic*, already denies logic the founding role.

(128–129) The quoted passage from *Primae veritates* states that in every truth the predicate or consequent is contained in the subject or antecedent, and that this connection of the terms of an assertion is the nature of truth in general, as Aristotle also observed. In identities the inclusion is explicit; in all other truths it is implicit and must be shown by analysis of notions, which is what a priori demonstration consists in. This holds for every affirmative truth, universal or singular, necessary or contingent, and for intrinsic as well as extrinsic denominations. Leibniz sees here a "wonderful secret" that contains the nature of contingency, the essential difference between necessary and contingent truths, and removes the difficulty of a fatal necessity governing even free things. From these principles, too little considered because of their ease, follows the accepted axiom that nothing is without reason, no effect without cause. Otherwise there would be a truth that could not be proved a priori, that would not resolve into identities, which is against the nature of truth, always identical either expressly or implicitly. The derivation runs from the nature of truth, through the inclusion of predicate in subject, to the principle. The "secret" is Leibniz's doctrine that contingent truths require an infinite analysis, completed only by God; freedom is thus saved inside a theory of truth as containment, a link between truth and freedom that Heidegger's Part III will rebuild on wholly different ground.

(129) Leibniz characteristically joins the account of "first truths" to a determination of what truth primarily is, in order to show the "birth" of the principle from the nature of truth. In doing so he warns that the apparent obviousness of concepts like truth and identity hinders a clarification sufficient to show where the principle and the other axioms come from. Heidegger does not want the derivation, only the unfolding of the problem of ground, and asks what guidance the passage offers for that. He takes from Leibniz the method (seek the origin of the principle in the essence of truth) while discarding its result (the logical derivation).

(129–130) Heidegger reconstructs the argument. The principle holds because without it there would be beings that had to be groundless; for Leibniz, truths that resisted resolution into identities and so violated the nature of truth. Since that is impossible and truth exists, the principle holds as springing from the essence of truth. That essence lies in the connection (*connexio*, Aristotle's *symplokē*) of subject and predicate, so Leibniz takes truth from the outset as propositional truth, appealing to Aristotle with only partial justification (Aristotle also knows a truth of simple apprehension, as *Metaphysics* Θ 10 and *Being and Time* § 44 stress). The nexus is the predicate's being-in the subject, and that being-in is being-the-same (*inesse* as *idem esse*). Identity here cannot mean the empty sameness of a thing with itself; it means unity as the original oneness of what belongs together. Truth is then accord, and accord only as agreement with what announces itself in the identity as unified. True assertions refer by nature to something on the basis of which they can be in accord; the articulating connection in every truth is what it is only "on the ground of" something, that is, as self-grounding. Truth carries an essential relation to ground, so the more originally truth is grasped, the more urgently the problem of ground must press. Heidegger converts Leibniz's deductive relation between two principles into a relation between two essences, which lets him move the inquiry from logic to the question of truth.

(130–133) Heidegger asks whether anything more original can be said of truth than that it is a character of assertions, and answers that the propositional determination, however refined, is unavoidable but derivative (*Being and Time* §§ 33, 44). Agreement of the nexus with beings does not of itself make beings accessible. Beings must already be manifest before and for predication, as its possible about-which; predication can only take root in a making-manifest that is not predicative. Propositional truth is rooted in a more original truth, unconcealment, the pre-predicative manifestness of beings, which Heidegger calls ontic truth. Its character changes with the kind and region of beings: the discoveredness of what is present-at-hand, such as material things, differs specifically from the disclosedness of the existing Dasein that we ourselves are (*Being and Time* § 60). Whatever these differences, no pre-predicative manifestation is primarily a mere representing or intuiting, not even in "aesthetic" contemplation. The intuitive model tempts because truth is first defined as a combination of representations, and the simpler counterpart then appears as uncombined, simple representing; the target is the Brentanian and Husserlian pairing of judgment with the simple presentation that founds it. Simple representing has its own function in objectifying beings that are already manifest. Ontic manifestation itself happens in the attuned and driven finding-oneself among beings (*Befindlichkeit*, *Being and Time* § 29) and in the striving and willing comportments founded in it. Yet even these could not give access to beings as they are in themselves unless an understanding of being, of the constitution of beings in their what and how, already illuminated and guided them. The unveiledness of being first makes the manifestness of beings possible, and is called ontological truth. A 1929 marginal note against "attuned and driven" reads the passage as concerning the openness of the Da itself, prior to any psychology of faculties.

The terms "ontology" and "ontological" are ambiguous in a way that hides the proper problem. The *logos* of beings means both addressing beings as beings and that toward which they are addressed. To address something as something is not yet to grasp it in its essence. The understanding of being that illuminates and guides every comportment toward beings is *logos* in the broadest sense; it neither grasps being as such nor conceives what it grasps, which would be *logos* in the narrowest sense, the ontological concept. Understanding of being that has not yet reached the concept is called pre-ontological, or ontological in the broader sense. Conceiving being requires that the understanding of being develop itself and make the being it has projected and somehow unveiled an explicit theme and problem. Between the two lie many stages. One is the projection of the constitution of a region (nature, history), which marks out a field for scientific objectification and hardens into the basic concepts of a science: space, place, time, motion, mass, force, velocity. Here the being of what is present-at-hand reaches a concept, yet the essence of time and motion never becomes a problem in its own right; the definitions are governed in approach and reach by the guiding question each science puts to beings. The basic concepts of the sciences are not the proper ontological concepts and cannot be turned into them by suitable extension. The ontological concepts must be won before any scientific definition, so that one can judge how, and from what restricted vantage, the sciences' concepts hit the being that ontology conceives. The "fact" of the sciences, the understanding of being that they contain like any comportment toward beings, is neither the court that validates the a priori nor the source of our knowledge of it, only an occasion and pointer toward, say, the constitution of history or nature, itself subject to constant critique from the principled questioning of being. The target is Marburg Neo-Kantianism, which took the fact of science as the starting point of transcendental inquiry.

Note 14 turns on the contemporary fashion for "ontology". Those who use it as a slogan take ontology to mean a "realist" attitude, naive or critical, opposed to an "idealist" one. Ontological problems have so little to do with realism that Kant, with his transcendental question, took the first decisive step since Plato and Aristotle toward explicitly grounding ontology. To champion the reality of the external world is not yet to be ontologically oriented. What the popular sense calls "ontological", an attitude that lets beings be what and how they are in themselves, should rather be called ontic; it poses no problem of being and secures no foundation for ontology. The polemic is aimed at the "new ontology" of Nicolai Hartmann and kindred realisms, and it blocks the most common misreading of Heidegger's own project.

(133–135) The stages and variants of ontological truth in the broader sense show how rich is the original truth that underlies all ontic truth. Unconcealment of being is always truth of the being of beings, whether or not these are actual; conversely, unconcealment of beings already includes that of their being. Ontic and ontological truth concern, in different ways, beings in their being and the being of beings. They belong together through their relation to the difference between being and beings, the ontological difference. A 1929 marginal note fixes the term's provenance: its first public use was in the summer 1927 course *Basic Problems of Phenomenology* (GA 24, § 22). The essence of truth, necessarily "forked" into ontic and ontological, is possible only together with the breaking open of this difference. If Dasein's distinction is that it relates to beings while understanding being, then the capacity to distinguish, in which the difference becomes factical, must have its roots in the ground of Dasein's essence. Heidegger names this ground of the difference, provisionally, the transcendence of Dasein. The argument has now moved from the principle, through truth, to the difference, and from there to transcendence as its ground. A 1929 marginal note judges the step harshly: truth is here derived from the difference treated as a fixed marker, instead of the difference being thought from the truth of being. The later preface's insistence that the difference is no product of the understanding answers this same weakness.

(135) If all comportment toward beings is called intentional, intentionality is possible only on the ground of transcendence; it is neither identical with transcendence nor what makes transcendence possible. Note 15 refers to *Being and Time* § 69c and to the note on p. 363, where intentionality is grounded in the ecstatic unity of temporality. The sentence reverses the founding order of Husserlian phenomenology in a volume written for Husserl: intentionality, the basic theme of phenomenology, becomes a derivative structure. The 1928 course makes the same claim at length (GA 26, § 9c).

(135) So far Heidegger has shown, in a few essential steps, that the essence of truth must be sought more originally than as a property of assertions. If the essence of ground relates internally to the essence of truth, the problem of ground can be at home only where truth draws its inner possibility, in the essence of transcendence. The question of the essence of ground becomes the problem of transcendence. The inference uses the result of 129–130 (truth is intrinsically related to ground) as its middle term.

(135) If the linkage of truth, ground and transcendence is originally unified, the corresponding problems must come to light together wherever the question of ground is taken up resolutely, even if only as an explicit discussion of the principle of reason. Heidegger turns his thesis into a test: the history of the principle must bear it out. The next unit applies the test to Leibniz and Kant.

(135–136) The Leibniz passage already betrays the kinship of the problem of ground with the problem of being. True being means being-in as being-the-same. For Leibniz true being also means being "in truth", being as such; the idea of being in general is therefore interpreted as being-in qua being-the-same. What makes a being a being is identity, rightly understood: a unity that, as simple, originally unifies and in unifying also individuates. This anticipatory, simply individuating unification, which makes up the essence of beings as such, is the essence of "subjectivity" in its monadological sense, the substantiality of substance (the monad as unifying drive, analysed in GA 26, §§ 5–6). Leibniz's derivation of the principle from propositional truth thus rests on a definite idea of being, in whose light alone the "deduction" works. The link between ground and being shows even more clearly in Kant. One commonly misses an explicit treatment of the principle in the critical writings, unless the proof of the Second Analogy is allowed to stand in for it. But Kant did discuss the principle, in a prominent place, as the "highest principle of all synthetic judgments": the conditions of the possibility of experience are at the same time the conditions of the possibility of its objects. That principle sets out what belongs, within Kant's ontological horizon, to the being of beings as accessible in experience; it gives a real definition of transcendental truth, determining its inner possibility through the unity of time, imagination and the "I think" (note 16 refers to *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics*, 1929). Kant said of Leibniz's principle that it was a "remarkable pointer to investigations still to be made in metaphysics"; the same holds of his own highest principle, which hides the problem of the essential connection of being, truth and ground. Only from there can one ask how transcendental and formal logic are related at their origin, or whether the distinction is justified at all. Heidegger reads the highest principle of synthetic judgments as Kant's version of the principle of reason; the reading depends on the Kant book's thesis that the *Critique* is a grounding of ontology.

(136–137) The short account of Leibniz's derivation was meant to show how the problem of ground connects with the inner possibility of ontological truth, and finally with the more original and therefore more encompassing question of transcendence. Transcendence is the domain in which the problem of ground must be met, and Part II sets out its main features. A 1929 note to the heading of Part I judges the whole approach: the truth of being is here still treated within traditional metaphysics, in simple correspondence to the truth and unconcealment of beings; a path toward overcoming "ontology" is entered (see Part III) but the overcoming is not built from what has been reached.

### II. Transcendence as the Domain of the Question concerning the Essence of Ground

(137) A terminological remark fixes the word. Transcendence means surpassing (*Überstieg*). What is transcendent, or transcending, is what performs the surpassing and dwells in it; surpassing belongs as a happening to some being. Formally it is a relation running "from" something "to" something; to it belongs that toward which the surpassing goes, usually and wrongly called "the transcendent", and that which is surpassed. These moments come from a spatial event, which is what the word first means. Heidegger moves the adjective from the object to the one who surpasses. In Husserl and in epistemology generally, the "transcendent" is the object lying beyond consciousness; here it is Dasein. The three moments (the one surpassing, what is surpassed, the toward-which) structure all of Part II.

(137) Transcendence in the terminological sense belongs to human Dasein, not as one possible comportment among others, enacted now and then, but as its basic constitution, happening before all comportment. As spatially existing, Dasein can of course also cross a spatial barrier or gap. Transcendence is rather the surpassing that makes existence possible at all, and with it every movement of oneself in space. The spatial sense is not discarded but grounded: crossing a gap is one of the things transcendence makes possible.

(137–138) If one calls the being that we ourselves are and understand as "Dasein" a "subject", transcendence designates the essence of the subject, the basic structure of subjectivity. The subject never exists first as a subject and then, should objects happen to be present, also transcends; to be a subject means to be a being in and as transcendence. The problem cannot be posed as whether transcendence can belong to the subject. Understanding transcendence already decides whether we have any concept of subjectivity or merely posit a truncated subject. The "torso" is the epistemological subject of the external-world problem (*Being and Time* § 43), which must then build a bridge to objects it has been defined without.

(138) Calling transcendence the basic structure of subjectivity gains little for entering this constitution of Dasein. On the contrary, once every explicit or tacit concept of the subject is fended off, transcendence can no longer be determined as a subject-object relation. Transcendent Dasein, an already tautological phrase, surpasses neither a "barrier" set before the subject that confines it to immanence, nor a "gap" separating it from the object. Nor are objects, beings made into objects, what the surpassing goes toward. What is surpassed is beings themselves: every being that can be or become unconcealed to Dasein, including precisely the being as which Dasein "itself" exists. Dasein surpasses itself as a being along with everything else, which rules out locating the self on the near side of transcendence.

(138–139) In surpassing, Dasein first comes toward the being that it is, toward itself as "itself". Transcendence constitutes selfhood. Yet never selfhood alone: the surpassing always concerns beings that Dasein is not; more exactly, only in and through it can the line be drawn within beings between who a self is, and how, and what is not a self. Only as existing as a self can Dasein relate "itself" to beings, and these must first have been surpassed. Though it is among beings and embraced by them, Dasein as existing has always already surpassed nature. Selfhood is a result of transcendence, not its precondition; the distinction between self and non-self is drawn by the surpassing, not given in advance. The remark on nature anticipates note 55, where Dasein's being embraced by nature is assigned to attunement.

(139) What a Dasein has surpassed has not simply come together piece by piece. Beings, however they are determined and articulated in detail, are surpassed beforehand as a whole. This whole may remain unrecognized as such, though it is always interpreted from beings, mostly from some insistent region of them, and so is at least known in some way. "Beings as a whole" becomes the theme of the contemporaneous "What Is Metaphysics?", where attunements such as boredom and anxiety reveal it.

(139) Surpassing happens as a whole, never only sometimes, and certainly not merely or first as the theoretical grasping of objects. With the fact of Da-sein, surpassing is there. The hyphen stresses the "there": to be Dasein at all is to have surpassed. Heidegger denies theoretical cognition any priority, against the epistemological starting point.

(139–140) If beings are not what the surpassing goes toward, how is the toward-which to be determined, or even sought? Heidegger calls it world and now determines transcendence as being-in-the-world. World co-constitutes the unified structure of transcendence; because it belongs to transcendence the concept of world is called transcendental. The term names everything that belongs essentially to transcendence and holds its inner possibility from it as a fief; only for this reason can the illumination of transcendence be called a "transcendental" discussion. What "transcendental" means must not be taken from a philosophy that treats it as a "standpoint", let alone an epistemological one. This does not deny that Kant recognized the transcendental as the problem of the inner possibility of ontology, though for him it kept an essentially "critical" sense. For Kant the transcendental concerns the possibility of knowledge that does not illegitimately overfly experience, that is not transcendent but is experience itself; it gives the restricting yet positive definition of the non-transcendent ontic knowledge possible to humans. A more radical and universal grasp of transcendence brings with it a more original elaboration of the idea of ontology and so of metaphysics. Heidegger rebinds "transcendental" to "transcendence": in Kant the two words point in opposite directions (the transcendental limits knowledge to experience, the transcendent exceeds it), whereas here the transcendental is whatever belongs to Dasein's surpassing. The target of the "standpoint" remark is Neo-Kantian transcendental philosophy.

(140) The phrase "being-in-the-world" names what looks like an easily grasped state of affairs. What it means depends on whether world is taken in a pre-philosophical, everyday sense or in a transcendental one. Discussing this double sense will make it clear.

(140) Transcendence as being-in-the-world is supposed to belong to human Dasein. Taken one way, that is the most trivial and empty thing one could say: the existing human occurs among other beings and can be met with there. Transcendence would then mean belonging among what is already present-at-hand, or among beings whose number can always increase. World would be the title for everything that is, the totality, a unity that determines the "all" no further than as a collection. On this concept of world, transcendence would have to be ascribed to every being present-at-hand, since whatever occurs among others "is in the world". If "transcendent" meant only "belonging among the other beings", transcendence obviously could not be the distinctive essential constitution of Dasein. The sentence "being-in-the-world belongs to the essence of Dasein" would even be plainly false, since it is not essentially necessary that a being such as human Dasein factually exist. It could also not be.

(141) If, on the other hand, being-in-the-world is rightly ascribed to Dasein alone, and as its essential constitution, the expression cannot have that meaning, and world means something other than the totality of beings present-at-hand. To ascribe being-in-the-world to Dasein as its basic constitution is to say something about its essence, its innermost possibility as Dasein. Whether and which Dasein factually exists cannot count as evidence. The phrase reports no factual occurrence; it is no ontic statement at all but an ontological thesis about an essential feature of Dasein. Dasein is not in-the-world because, and only because, it factually exists; conversely, it can only be as existing, that is, as Dasein, because its essential constitution lies in being-in-the-world. The order of founding is reversed: essence makes factual existence possible rather than being read off from it.

(141) Heidegger sets out three sentences built from the same words. "Factical Dasein is in a world", meaning it occurs among other beings, is an empty tautology. "It belongs to Dasein's essence to be in the world", meaning to occur necessarily alongside other beings, is false. "Being-in-the-world belongs to the essence of Dasein as such" contains the problem of transcendence. The difference lies entirely in which concept of world, and so which sense of "in", governs the sentence.

(141) The thesis is original and simple. Its unveiling is not therefore simple: being-in-the-world can be brought to understanding only in a projection of varying transparency, which prepares the understanding and then, always relatively, closes it off conceptually. Heidegger applies to his own thesis the hermeneutic structure of understanding as projection (*Being and Time* §§ 31–32): philosophical concepts are themselves projected, never simply read off.

(141–142) So far the transcendence of Dasein has been determined only by contrast. World belongs to transcendence as that toward which the surpassing happens. The positive problem of what world is, how Dasein's "relation" to world is determined, and how being-in-the-world is to be grasped as an originally unified constitution, is treated here only as far as the guiding problem of ground requires. To that end Heidegger attempts an interpretation of the world phenomenon meant to illuminate transcendence as such.

(142) For orientation he sketches, necessarily with gaps, the main meanings that have come forward in the history of the concept of world. With such elementary concepts the everyday meaning is usually not the original and essential one; the essential meaning is repeatedly covered over and rarely reaches its concept. The historical survey is therefore retrieval, not doxography: it seeks the covered meaning beneath the dominant one.

(142–143) Something essential appears already at the decisive beginnings of ancient philosophy (note 18 cites Reinhardt's *Parmenides*). *Kosmos* does not mean this or that being that presses in on us, nor all of them taken together, but a "condition": the how in which beings are, and are as a whole. "This *kosmos*" does not mark off one region of beings from another but this world of beings from another world of the same beings, being as it is "according to *kosmos*" (Melissus fr. 7, Parmenides fr. 2). World as this "how as a whole" already underlies every possible fragmentation of beings; fragmentation does not destroy world but always needs it. What is in "the one *kosmos*" (Anaxagoras fr. 8) did not form it by being pushed together but is pervaded and governed by it beforehand. Heraclitus (fr. 89) recognizes a further essential feature: the waking have one common world, while each sleeper turns aside into a world of his own. Here world is related to basic ways in which human Dasein factually exists. In waking, beings show themselves in a consistently harmonious how, accessible on average to everyone; in sleep, the world of beings is individuated to each Dasein alone. The Greek evidence gives world as a mode rather than a collection, and from the start relative to how Dasein exists.

(143) Four points follow. World means a how of the being of beings rather than the beings themselves. This how determines beings as a whole; it is at bottom the possibility of any how whatever, as limit and measure. This how as a whole is in a certain sense prior. And this prior how as a whole is itself relative to human Dasein. World belongs to human Dasein, though it embraces all beings, Dasein included, as a whole. The four points set the programme of the systematic section (156–158): world as non-being, as whole, as prior, and as Dasein-related.

(143) However surely this still inexplicit, dawning understanding of *kosmos* can be gathered under these meanings, the word just as often names the beings experienced in such a how. Heidegger concedes the ambiguity between the how and the beings themselves already in the Greek, which explains why the everyday meaning could cover the original one.

(143–144) It is no accident that with the new ontic understanding of existence that broke through in Christianity, the relation of *kosmos* to human Dasein, and with it the concept of world, grew sharper and clearer. The relation is experienced so originally that *kosmos* comes to be used directly as a title for a definite basic kind of human existence. In Paul (1 Corinthians, Galatians), "this *kosmos*" means not primarily the condition of the cosmic but the condition and situation of the human being, his stance toward the cosmos, his valuation of goods. *Kosmos* is human being in the how of a disposition turned away from God ("the wisdom of the world"); it names human Dasein in a definite "historical" existence, distinguished from another that has already dawned, the age to come. "Ontic" is precise: Christianity changes the existentiell self-understanding, not the ontological concept, a distinction Heidegger drew in his 1920–21 Pauline lectures (GA 60) and in "Phenomenology and Theology" (1927).

(144) The Gospel of John uses *kosmos* unusually often compared with the Synoptics, and in a wholly central sense. World designates the God-estranged basic shape of human Dasein, the character of human being as such. It therefore also becomes a regional title for all humans together, without distinction between wise and foolish, just and sinners, Jews and gentiles. The centrality of this wholly anthropological concept shows in its functioning as the counter-concept to Jesus's sonship of God, which is understood as life, truth (*alētheia*) and light. Note 22 cites Bauer's commentary and Schlatter's *Theology of the New Testament*. In John, world and truth are opposed terms; Heidegger's own analysis will instead join them, making world the condition of truth.

(144–145) The meaning first shaped in the New Testament appears unmistakably in Augustine and Aquinas. For Augustine *mundus* means at times the whole of creation, but as often the world's inhabitants, and then in the specifically existential sense of those who love the world, the impious, the carnal. The just are not called world because, though they dwell in it in the flesh, they are with God in their hearts. Augustine probably drew this concept, which went on to help shape Western intellectual history, as much from Paul as from John. His *Tractates on John* prove it: commenting on John 1:10 ("he was in the world, and the world was made through him, and the world knew him not"), he shows that *mundus* is used twice in two senses. In "the world was made through him" it means created being; in "the world knew him not" it means dwelling in the world with the heart, loving the world, which coincides with not knowing God.

(145) The quoted passage asks what "the world was made through him" means, and answers that heaven, earth, sea and all they contain are called world. In another sense the lovers of the world are called world. Did the heavens, the angels, the stars not know their Creator, whom even the demons confess? All things everywhere bore witness. Who then did not know him? Those who, by loving the world, are called the world, since by loving we dwell with the heart, and by loving they earned the name of the place where they dwelt. We call a house bad or good without blaming or praising its walls; we mean bad or good inhabitants. So too we call world those who inhabit it by loving it; those who do not love the world move about in it in the flesh but dwell in heaven in their hearts. The metonymy is grounded in a relation of existence: love is a way of dwelling, and world names those who dwell in it in that way. Heidegger had already derived "being-in" from dwelling (*Being and Time* § 12); Augustine supplies a precedent in which being "in" the world is a how of the heart, not spatial containment.

(145) World therefore means beings as a whole, taken as the decisive how by which human Dasein takes its stand and holds itself toward beings. Aquinas likewise uses *mundus* both as equivalent to the universe, the totality of creatures, and in the sense of *saeculum*, a worldly disposition, since "the lovers of the world are signified by the name of world"; *mundanus* (*saecularis*) is the counter-concept to *spiritualis*. Note 25 cites the *Summa*: one can be in the world in two ways, by bodily presence or by the mind's affection. Heidegger's summary fuses the two senses the tradition kept apart: world is the whole of beings, but as the how of Dasein's stance toward them.

(145–146) Passing over Leibniz, Heidegger turns to school metaphysics. Baumgarten defines the world as the series (multitude, whole) of actual finite things that is not part of another. World is equated with the totality of what is present-at-hand, understood as created being, so the concept of world depends on how one understands the essence and possibility of proofs of God. Crusius shows this clearly: a world is a real connection of finite things that is not itself part of another to which it would belong by real connection. God is opposed to the world; the world is also distinguished from a single creature, from several coexisting creatures with no connection at all, and from a sum of creatures that is only part of another with which it stands in real connection. The Christian existential sense has vanished; world has become a cosmological object defined by its contrast with God.

(146) What belongs essentially to such a world must derive from two sources: what follows from the general essence of things, and what can be known as necessary from God's essential properties once certain creatures are posited. The "doctrine of the world" is therefore subordinate, within metaphysics as a whole, to ontology (the doctrine of the essence and most general differences of things) and to theoretical natural theology. World is the regional title for the highest unity of connection of the totality of created beings. Rational cosmology, a branch of special metaphysics, rests on general metaphysics and theology; world has become one region among others.

(146–147) If the concept of world functions as a basic concept of metaphysics, specifically of rational cosmology as a discipline of special metaphysics, and Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* is a grounding of metaphysics as a whole (note 30 refers to the Kant book), then the problem of world must change shape with the transformation of the idea of metaphysics. A brief indication is all the more needed because, beside the "cosmological" sense of world, Kant's *Anthropology* again lets the existential sense break through, though without its specifically Christian colouring. Kant will bear both senses at once, which makes him the pivot of the survey.

(147) Already in the Inaugural Dissertation of 1770, where the introductory characterization of *mundus* still largely follows traditional ontic metaphysics, Kant touches a difficulty in the concept of world that later sharpens into a main problem of the *Critique*. He begins with a formal determination: world is essentially related to synthesis as its "terminus". Just as analysis in a substantial composite ends only with a part that is not a whole, the simple, so synthesis ends only with a whole that is not a part, the world. In § 2 he names the moments essential to a definition: matter, in the transcendental sense, the parts taken as substances; form, consisting in the coordination, not the subordination, of substances; and universality, the absolute allness of the component parts. Of the third he remarks that this absolute totality, though it looks like an everyday and obvious concept, especially when stated negatively as in the definition, "seems to crucify the philosopher" when weighed more deeply. The three moments return at 151–152, each transformed.

(147–150) This "cross" weighs on Kant through the next decade, for in the *Critique* the totality of the world becomes a problem in several respects. Heidegger formulates three questions Kant does not pose as such: to what does the totality represented as "world" refer, or alone can refer? What, accordingly, is represented in the concept of world? What character has this representing of totality, that is, what is the conceptual structure of the concept of world? Kant's answers transform the problem of world completely. The totality still refers to finite things present-at-hand, but their finitude receives a new sense. It is no longer established by an ontic proof that they are created by God; it is interpreted from the fact that, and the extent to which, things are possible objects for a finite knowing, one that must let them be given as already present. Beings dependent for access on receptive intuition Kant calls appearances; the same beings taken as possible objects of an absolute, creative intuition he calls things in themselves. The unity of the connection of appearances, the constitution of being of what finite knowledge can reach, is determined by the ontological principles, the system of synthetic a priori cognitions. The content these principles represent, their "reality" in the old sense of thinghood that Kant retains, can be exhibited intuitively and independently of experience from the objects, that is, from what is necessarily intuited a priori with them, the pure intuition of time; their reality is objective. Yet the unity of appearances, being tied to a contingent givenness, is always conditioned and in principle incomplete. If it is represented as complete, there arises the representation of a sum whose content cannot be projected in an image (*Bild*), anything intuitable. This representation is "transcendent"; since it is nonetheless necessary a priori, it has, though transcendent, transcendental reality. Kant calls such representations Ideas. They contain a completeness no empirical knowledge reaches; reason intends a systematic unity to which it tries to bring empirical unity closer without ever attaining it. A system is the unity of manifold cognitions under an Idea, the rational concept of the form of a whole. Since the unity represented in Ideas can never be projected in an image, it can never relate directly to the intuitable; as a higher unity it concerns only the unity of the understanding's synthesis. The Ideas are not arbitrarily invented but set as tasks by the nature of reason itself, and so relate necessarily to the whole use of the understanding. As pure concepts of reason they arise not from the understanding's reflection, which still refers to the given, but from reason's procedure of inference; hence Kant calls them "inferred" concepts in contrast to the "reflected" concepts of the understanding. In inferring, reason aims to find the unconditioned for given conditions, so the Ideas, as pure concepts of totality, are representations of the unconditioned. Kant: the transcendental concept of reason is that of the totality of conditions for a given conditioned; since only the unconditioned makes this totality possible, and the totality is itself unconditioned, a pure concept of reason can be explained through the concept of the unconditioned "insofar as it contains a ground of the synthesis of the conditioned". The Kantian world is defined by finitude, the finitude of receptive intuition; and at the summit of reason the unconditioned is defined as a ground of synthesis, so the problem of ground reappears at the very point where world is conceived. The motif of the "image" will return in Heidegger's own claim that Dasein is "world-forming" (*weltbildend*, 158).

(150–151) As representations of the unconditioned totality of a domain of beings, Ideas are necessary representations. Since representations can relate to the subject, to the object, and to the object in two ways, finitely (appearances) and absolutely (things in themselves), three classes of Ideas arise, to which the three disciplines of traditional special metaphysics correspond. The concept of world is the Idea in which the absolute totality of the objects accessible to finite knowledge is represented a priori; world means "the sum of all appearances" or "of all objects of possible experience", and Kant calls all transcendental Ideas that concern the absolute totality in the synthesis of appearances "world-concepts". Because beings accessible to finite knowledge can be considered ontologically with respect to their what (essence) and their existence, the distinction Kant calls "mathematical" and "dynamical" and by which he divides the categories and principles, the world-concepts divide in turn into mathematical and dynamical ones. The mathematical are world-concepts "in the narrower sense"; the dynamical Kant also calls "transcendent concepts of nature". Still he finds it "quite fitting" to call them all world-concepts, because world means the sum of all appearances and the Ideas aim only at the unconditioned among appearances, and because world in the transcendental sense means the absolute totality of the sum of existing things, where only the completeness of the synthesis, in the regress to conditions, is at issue. Note 41 adds a pointer that exceeds the *Critique*: for a more radical problem of world and of beings as a whole, one should consult the distinction between the mathematical and the dynamical sublime (*Critique of Judgment* § 28). Beings as a whole would then be disclosed in a feeling, an attunement, rather than in an Idea of reason, which is precisely the route "What Is Metaphysics?" takes.

(151–152) Kant's remark shows both the continuity of his concept of world with traditional metaphysics and the transformation, the more original ontological interpretation, achieved in the *Critique*. The three questions can now be answered. First, the concept of world is no ontic connection of things in themselves but a transcendental (ontological) sum of things as appearances. Second, it represents not a "coordination" of substances but a subordination, the series of conditions of synthesis ascending to the unconditioned. Third, it is no conceptually indeterminate "rational" representation but is determined as an Idea, a pure synthetic concept of reason distinct from the concepts of the understanding. Each answer revises a moment of 1770: matter becomes appearances, coordination becomes subordination, and the vague universality becomes the strict concept of an Idea.

(152) The concept of world thus loses the character of universality (allness) once assigned to it; this is reserved for a still higher class of transcendental Ideas, to which the concept of world itself points, and which Kant calls the "transcendental ideal" (A 572/B 600 note). The "cross" of 1770 is resolved by relocating absolute totality from world to the ideal of God.

(152) Heidegger forgoes interpreting this highest point of Kant's speculative metaphysics. Only one thing needs saying, so that finitude, the essential character of the concept of world, emerges more clearly. His selection is explicit: he extracts finitude from Kant's cosmology.

(152) As an Idea, the concept of world represents an unconditioned totality. Yet it does not represent the absolutely and "properly" unconditioned, since the totality it thinks stays referred to appearances, the possible objects of finite knowledge. World as Idea is transcendent, surpassing appearances, yet as their totality it is referred back to them. Transcendence in Kant's sense of surpassing experience is therefore ambiguous. It can mean surpassing, within experience, what is given in it, the manifold of appearances; this holds of the representation "world". Or it can mean stepping out of appearance as finite knowledge altogether and representing the possible whole of all things as "object" of original intuition (*intuitus originarius*); this transcendence yields the transcendental ideal, against which world appears as a restriction and becomes the title for finite human knowledge in its totality. The concept of world stands between the "possibility of experience" and the "transcendental ideal", and so means, at its core, the totality of the finitude of human essence. Heidegger extracts from Kant the thesis his own account needs: world is essentially finite and relative to human being. The pairing of the ideal with original intuition returns at 161 as the culmination of a history that replaced world with a region of eternal beings.

(153) From here a view opens onto the second, specifically existential meaning that world has in Kant beside the "cosmological" one. The finitude just extracted is the bridge: world as the totality of human finitude leads directly to world as human existence.

(153) Kant writes in the preface to the *Anthropology* that the most important object in the world to which humans can apply all cultural progress is the human being, because he is his own final end; to know him according to his species as an earthly being endowed with reason deserves particularly to be called knowledge of the world, though he is only one part of earthly creatures. What is called knowledge of the world here is knowledge of the human being with regard to what he, as a freely acting being, makes, can and should make of himself, not knowledge of him in a "physiological" respect. Knowledge of the world means pragmatic anthropology. Such an anthropology, Kant adds, is called pragmatic not when it contains extensive knowledge of things in the world, animals, plants and minerals in various lands and climates, but when it contains knowledge of the human being as a citizen of the world. In Kant, "world" names the human being in his freedom; Part III will join world and freedom systematically.

(153) That "world" means the existence of humans in historical being-with-one-another, not their cosmic occurrence as a species of living thing, is clearest in two idioms Kant uses: "knowing the world" and "having world". Both aim at human existence but differ: the one who knows the world "only understands the play he has watched", while the other "has played along". World is the title for the "play" of everyday Dasein, for everyday Dasein itself. Note 47 adds Kant's lecture phrases: a man of the world is a fellow player in the great game of life; to have world means to have maxims and imitate great models, an expression from the French, reached by conduct, manners and social intercourse. The image of play is load-bearing for Heidegger: in the winter course of 1928–29 (GA 27) he develops world as the "play" of transcendence, and Part III speaks of the "room for play" (*Spielraum*) that transcendence forms (166, 170).

(154) Kant accordingly distinguishes worldly prudence from private prudence: the former is "the skill of a human being in influencing others so as to use them for his purposes". A history is written pragmatically, he says, when it makes prudent, that is, teaches the world how to secure its advantage better than, or at least as well as, the world before it. Both uses confirm that "world" here means humans in their dealings with one another.

(154) From this "knowledge of the world" in the sense of experience of life and understanding of existence, Kant distinguishes "school knowledge", and along this distinction he develops the concept of philosophy according to the "school concept" and according to the "world concept". Philosophy in the scholastic sense remains the business of the mere "artist of reason"; philosophy according to the world concept is the concern of the "teacher in the ideal", the one who aims at the "divine human within us". "The world concept here means the concept that concerns what necessarily interests everyone." Philosophy itself receives its essence from the world concept: what matters to every existing human. Heidegger adopts this Kantian distinction to define philosophy against scholarly specialization (GA 24, § 2; GA 27).

(154) In all this, world designates human Dasein at the core of its essence. The concept corresponds exactly to Augustine's existential one, except that the Christian valuation of "worldly" existence, the lovers of the world, has disappeared, and world positively means the "fellow players" in the game of life. The survey thus closes a circle: Kant restores, in secular form, the existential concept that school metaphysics had lost.

(154–155) The existential meaning announces itself in the later expression "world-view" (*Weltanschauung*), and in phrases like "man of the world" and "fashionable world". "World" here is not a mere regional title for human society as opposed to the totality of natural things; it means humans in their relations to beings as a whole. The "fashionable world" includes hotels and racing stables. The example is precise: things belong to this world as the how of a human way of existing, not as natural objects. Note 54 lists three questions that cannot be pursued here: to what extent being-in-the-world necessarily includes something like a world-view; how, with regard to Dasein's transcendence, the essence of world-view must be delimited and grounded in its inner possibility; and how world-view, given its transcendental character, relates to philosophy. GA 27 takes up all three.

(155–156) It is equally wrong to take "world" either as the title for the totality of natural things (a naturalistic concept) or as the title for the community of persons (a personalistic one). Note 55 adds a decisive self-correction to the reception of *Being and Time*. If one identifies the ontic context of things of use, equipment, with world, and interprets being-in-the-world as dealing with things of use, any understanding of transcendence as Dasein's basic constitution is hopeless. The ontological structure of beings in the surrounding world, as discovered equipment, has the advantage, for a first characterization, of leading over into the analysis of world and preparing its transcendental problem. That was the sole purpose of the analysis of the surrounding world in §§ 14–24, an analysis that, measured against the whole and its guiding goal, remains of subordinate importance. If nature seems missing from the analytic, both nature as the object of science and nature in an original sense (*Being and Time*, p. 65), there are reasons. The main one is that nature can neither be encountered within the surrounding world nor be primarily something we comport ourselves toward. Nature is originally manifest in Dasein because Dasein exists as attuned in the midst of beings. Since attunement (thrownness) belongs to Dasein's essence and is expressed in the unity of the full concept of care, only here can the basis for the problem of nature be won. A 1929 marginal note adds that the concept of world is framed to avoid from the outset the traditional ontic metaphysics of nature as created being. The note pre-empts the pragmatist reading of *Being and Time*, and assigns nature to attunement and thrownness rather than to use, which Part III makes systematic as Dasein's "absorption" by beings (166).

The main text concludes that the metaphysically essential element in the more or less clearly articulated meanings of *kosmos*, *mundus* and world is that they aim at interpreting human Dasein in its relation to beings as a whole. For reasons not discussed here, the concept first arrives at the meaning in which it characterizes the how of beings as a whole, with its relation to Dasein understood only vaguely. World belongs to a relational structure distinctive of Dasein as such, which was called being-in-the-world. This use of the concept is anything but arbitrary; the historical indications were meant to show that it tries to raise a phenomenon of Dasein, always already known but never unitarily grasped in ontology, into the explicitness and sharpness of a problem.

(156) Human Dasein, a being situated among beings and comporting itself toward beings, exists in such a way that beings are always manifest as a whole. The wholeness need not be explicitly grasped, its belonging to Dasein may be veiled, its breadth can vary. It is understood without the whole of manifest beings being explicitly grasped, or "completely" explored, in its specific connections, regions and strata. The understanding of this wholeness, which reaches ahead and encompasses, is the surpassing toward world. A more concrete interpretation results from answering two questions: what is the basic character of this wholeness, and how does that character illuminate Dasein's relation to world, that is, the inner possibility of being-in-the-world (transcendence)? The wholeness is understood, not known; it is the pre-ontological understanding of being of Part I now described as a relation to totality.

(157) World as wholeness "is" no being, but that from which Dasein gives itself to understand which beings it can relate to and how. That Dasein gives "itself" to understand from "its" world means: in this coming-toward-itself from the world, Dasein temporalizes itself as a self, as a being that has been entrusted with being. In the being of this being, its ability-to-be is at issue; Dasein exists for the sake of itself. If world is that in surpassing toward which selfhood first temporalizes, it proves to be that for the sake of which Dasein exists. World has the basic character of the for-the-sake-of (*Umwillen*), in the original sense that it first provides the inner possibility for every factically determined "for your sake", "for his sake", "for that reason". What Dasein exists for the sake of is itself. To selfhood belongs world; world is essentially related to Dasein. The phrase "gives itself to understand" (*sich zu bedeuten geben*) recalls significance, the relational whole of *Being and Time* § 18, where the for-the-sake-of was the terminus of the chain of references. Here it becomes the primary character of world as such, which links world directly to selfhood and prepares the move to freedom in Part III.

(157) Before asking about the nature of this relation and interpreting being-in-the-world from the for-the-sake-of as the primary character of world, Heidegger fends off some obvious misinterpretations.

(157–158) The sentence "Dasein exists for the sake of itself" posits no egoistic, ontic aim for the blind self-love of a factual human being. It cannot be "refuted" by pointing out that many people sacrifice themselves for others, or that humans exist in community rather than alone. It implies neither a solipsistic isolation of Dasein nor an egoistic inflation of it. It states instead the condition of possibility for humans to behave either "egoistically" or "altruistically". Only because Dasein as such is determined by selfhood can an I-self relate to a you-self. Selfhood is the presupposition of I-hood, which discloses itself only in the You. Selfhood is never related to a You; because it makes all this possible, it is neutral toward being-I and being-You, and still more toward "sexuality". All essential propositions of an ontological analytic of Dasein take this being in advance in this neutrality. The likely targets are the dialogical philosophies of the I-You relation (Buber, Ebner) and Löwith's *The Individual in the Role of Fellow Human* (1928). GA 26 (§ 10) develops the neutrality in detail: it is not an empty abstraction but the source of the power that disperses Dasein factically into bodiliness, sexuality and the plurality of I and You.

(158) How is Dasein's relation to world determined? Since world is no being and is supposed to belong to Dasein, the relation cannot be one between Dasein as one being and world as another. Does world then get taken into Dasein, the subject, and declared purely "subjective"? But only the illumination of transcendence makes it possible to determine what "subject" and "subjective" mean. The concept of world must finally be conceived so that world is subjective, belonging to Dasein, yet precisely for that reason does not fall as a being into the inner sphere of a "subjective" subject. For the same reason it is not merely objective either, if that means belonging among the objects. *Being and Time* § 69c made the same point in the language of temporality: world is "more subjective" than any subject and "more objective" than any object. The dilemma of subjective and objective is not decided but shown to presuppose what it cannot explain.

(158) World, as the respective wholeness of the for-the-sake-of of a Dasein, is brought by Dasein before itself. This bringing of world before itself is the original projection of Dasein's possibilities, insofar as it is to be able to comport itself toward beings amid which it is. The projection of world, just as it does not explicitly grasp what is projected, is also always a casting of the projected world over beings (*Überwurf*). This prior casting-over first lets beings reveal themselves as such. The happening of projecting casting-over, in which Dasein's being temporalizes itself, is being-in-the-world. "Dasein transcends" means: it is, in the essence of its being, world-forming, "forming" in several senses. It lets world happen; with world it gives itself an original view or image (*Bild*), which is not explicitly grasped yet functions as a model (*Vor-bild*) for all manifest beings, Dasein itself among them. The German forms a family on "throw" (*werfen*): projection (*Entwurf*), casting-over (*Überwurf*), and the casting-ahead (*Vorwurf*) of Part III; at 175 the series closes with thrownness, "all projection of world is thrown". "World-forming" also plays on Kant: what Kant denied the Idea, that it can be projected in an image, Heidegger grants world, as an image that is formed but never seen. The winter course of 1929–30 (GA 29/30) builds its comparative thesis (the stone is worldless, the animal poor in world, the human world-forming) on this phrase.

(159) Beings, nature in the broadest sense for instance, could in no way become manifest unless they found occasion to enter a world. Heidegger therefore speaks of the possible and occasional world-entry of beings. World-entry is no process in the beings that enter, but something that "happens with" beings. This happening is the existing of Dasein, which transcends as existing. Only when, in the totality of beings, a being becomes "more beingful" in the manner of Dasein's temporalizing, does the hour and day of the world-entry of beings arrive. Only when this primal history, transcendence, happens, when a being with the character of being-in-the-world breaks into beings, is there the possibility that beings reveal themselves. World-entry changes nothing in the beings that enter: what depends on Dasein is their manifestness, not their existence, the position of *Being and Time* § 43c. "Primal history" and "breaking into" (*Einbruch*) belong to the vocabulary of the 1928 "metontology" (GA 26, § 11), which turns from fundamental ontology back to beings as a whole. Note 56 adds that the interpretation of Dasein as being-in-the-world decides neither positively nor negatively about a possible being toward God; but the illumination of transcendence first yields an adequate concept of Dasein, by reference to which it can be asked how Dasein's relation to God stands ontologically. A 1929 marginal note judges the relation between transcendence and the "distinction" here wrongly determined: transcendence essentially unfolds in the distinction, which carries out the difference, and the "happening" ought to be thought as a leap, finally as appropriation (*Ereignis*).

(159) The illumination so far already shows that transcendence, if beings come to light as beings only within it, forms a privileged domain for working out every question that concerns beings as such, in their being. Before laying out the guiding problem of ground in this domain, Heidegger makes transcendence more familiar through a further historical reminder. The reminder concerns Plato, and so moves from world to ground: the good is the Platonic name for the ground of truth and being.

(160) Transcendence is explicitly stated in Plato's "beyond being" (*epekeina tēs ousias*, *Republic* VI 509b). Can the good be interpreted as Dasein's transcendence? A glance at the context dispels such doubts. The problem of the good is only the culmination of the central, concrete question about the leading basic possibility of Dasein's existence in the polis. Even if the task of an ontological projection of Dasein onto its metaphysical basic constitution is neither explicitly posed nor developed, the threefold characterization of the good, carried out in constant correspondence with the "sun", presses toward the question of the possibility of truth, understanding and being, that is, of the originally unified ground of the possibility of the truth of the understanding of being. This understanding, as unveiling projection of being, is the primal action of human existence, in which all existing among beings must be rooted. The good is the sovereign power (*hexis*, *Mächtigkeit*) that commands the possibility, in the sense of enabling, of truth, understanding and even being, all three at once. The sun analogy supplies the mapping: as the sun gives visible things their visibility, the eye its seeing, and things their coming-to-be, the good gives truth, understanding and being. A 1931 marginal note retracts the reading: "No! Da-sein not grasped and not experienced at all. *Epekeina* not transcendence either, but the good as cause." The retraction coincides with the 1931–32 course on the cave allegory (GA 34), where the good becomes "what makes fit" and the later critique of Plato's doctrine of truth begins.

(160–161) It is no accident that the good is indeterminate in content, so that all definitions and interpretations of it must fail. Rationalist explanations fail as much as the "irrationalist" flight into "mystery". Following Plato's own indication, its illumination must hold to the task of interpreting the essential connection of truth, understanding and being. Inquiring back into the inner possibility of this connection is "compelled" to perform explicitly the surpassing that happens necessarily, but mostly hiddenly, in every Dasein. The essence of the good lies in its sovereignty over itself as that for the sake of which (*hou heneka*); as the for-the-sake-of it is the source of possibility as such. And because the possible lies higher than the actual, the power of the good, the essential source of possibility, is to be honoured still more highly (509a). The good is read as the Greek name for the primary character of world, the for-the-sake-of (157). "The possible lies higher than the actual" repeats the maxim of *Being and Time* § 7, and the "compulsion" alludes to the prisoner forced up out of the cave.

(161) Precisely here the relation of the for-the-sake-of to Dasein becomes problematic. Yet the problem does not come to light. According to the doctrine that became tradition, the Ideas remain in a "place above the heavens"; the task is only to secure them as the most objective of objects, the being of beings, without the for-the-sake-of showing itself as the primary character of world, so that the original content of the "beyond" never takes effect as Dasein's transcendence. Conversely, there later awakens the tendency, prefigured in Plato's "recollecting" "dialogue of the soul with itself", to take the Ideas as innate in the "subject". Both attempts show that world is both held out before Dasein (as beyond) and also forms itself within Dasein. The history of the problem of the Ideas shows transcendence always pressing toward the light, yet swinging between two poles of interpretation that are themselves insufficiently grounded and determined. The Ideas count as more objective than objects and at the same time as more subjective than the subject. Just as a privileged region of what always is takes the place of the unrecognized phenomenon of world, so the relation to world is interpreted as a particular comportment toward these beings: as *noein*, intuition, unmediated apprehending, "reason". The "transcendental ideal" goes together with original intuition. The history of metaphysics replaces world with a region of beings, and transcendence with a cognitive comportment toward that region; Kant's pairing of the ideal with *intuitus originarius* (152) is the late form of that double substitution. The oscillation between "beyond" and "innate" restates the dilemma of 158, now as the hidden motor of the history of idealism.

(161–162) This brief reminder of the still concealed history of the original problem of transcendence should yield the insight that transcendence cannot be unveiled and grasped by fleeing into the objective, but only by a constantly renewed ontological interpretation of the subjectivity of the subject, which works against "subjectivism" as much as it refuses to follow "objectivism". Note 59 expands this into a defence of *Being and Time*. The published part has no other task than a concretely unveiling projection of transcendence (§§ 12–83, especially § 69), in order to gain, as the title of Part One states, the "transcendental horizon of the question of being". All concrete interpretations, above all that of time, are to be assessed solely by how they make the question of being possible; they have as little to do with modern "dialectical theology" as with medieval scholasticism. To interpret Dasein as the being that can pose the problem of being as part of its existence does not make it the "proper" being among all others, the rest being mere shadows of it. On the contrary, the illumination of transcendence is to gain the horizon in which the concept of being, including the much-invoked "natural" one, can first be grounded philosophically as a concept. Ontological interpretation of being in and from Dasein's transcendence is no ontic derivation of all non-Dasein beings from the being that is Dasein. As for the charge of an "anthropocentric standpoint", it says nothing so long as one fails to see how, through the working out of Dasein's transcendence, "man" comes into the "centre" precisely so that his nothingness within beings as a whole can and must become a problem. What danger lies in an "anthropocentrism" whose whole effort is to show that the essence of the Dasein standing "in the centre" is ecstatic, that is, "ex-centric", and that the supposed freedom from all standpoints is therefore a delusion, contrary to philosophy as an essentially finite possibility of existence? Heidegger turns the charge against itself: Dasein is central only as ecstatic, and its centrality reveals its nothingness. The rejection of dialectical theology answers the Protestant reception of *Being and Time*; "ex-centric" may echo Plessner's "eccentric positionality" (*The Stages of the Organic*, 1928), though Heidegger grounds it in the ecstatic structure of time rather than in a biology of the human.

### III. On the Essence of Ground

(163) Heidegger recapitulates. The discussion of the principle of reason referred the problem of ground to the domain of transcendence (I); transcendence was determined, through an analysis of the concept of world, as Dasein's being-in-the-world (II). The task now is to illuminate the essence of ground from Dasein's transcendence. A 1929 marginal note claims more for this part than the text does: Part III begins a destruction of Part I, of the ontological difference and the scheme of ontic and ontological truth, and steps into a region that forces a complete overturning; the will appears here as Da-sein itself, sublating every "faculty".

(163–164) How does transcendence contain the inner possibility of anything like ground? World gives itself to Dasein as the respective wholeness of its for-the-sake-of, that is, for the sake of a being that is, equiprimordially, being-alongside what is present-at-hand, being-with the Dasein of others, and being toward itself. Dasein can be toward itself as itself only if it surpasses "itself" in the for-the-sake-of. This surpassing happens only in a "will" that projects itself onto possibilities of itself. Since this will casts the for-the-sake-of over and ahead of Dasein, it cannot be a particular willing, an "act of will" distinct from other comportments such as representing, judging or rejoicing; all comportments are rooted in transcendence, whereas this "will" is to "form" the for-the-sake-of itself, in and as the surpassing. What by its essence casts ahead the for-the-sake-of in projecting, and does not merely produce it now and then as an achievement, is what we call freedom. The surpassing toward world is freedom itself. Transcendence therefore does not come up against the for-the-sake-of as a value or end present in itself; freedom, as freedom, holds the for-the-sake-of out before itself. In this transcending holding-out of the for-the-sake-of, Dasein happens in the human being, so that in the essence of his existence he can be bound to himself, that is, be a free self. Freedom thereby reveals itself as what makes binding and obligation possible at all. Freedom alone can let a world prevail and world for Dasein. World never is; it worlds. Identified with transcendence, freedom ceases to be a property of a subject and becomes the structure of surpassing. The for-the-sake-of is denied the status of a value present in itself, against the value ethics of Rickert and Scheler. And freedom is made the source of obligation, which preserves the Kantian bond between freedom and self-legislation while removing it from practical reason. A 1929 marginal note reverses the phrase "Dasein happens in the human being": Dasein withstands the essence of truth and so grounds the possibility of being human. "Worlds" (*weltet*) is a verb Heidegger coined in 1919 (GA 56/57, "it worlds"); paired with "prevail" (*walten*), it denies that world is a being.

(164–165) In the end, this interpretation of freedom, won from transcendence, gives a more original account of its essence than the determination of freedom as spontaneity, a kind of causality. Beginning of itself characterizes freedom only negatively: no determining cause lies further back. Above all, this characterization speaks of "beginning" and "happening" with ontological indifference, without determining what it is to be a cause from the specific kind of being of the being that causes, Dasein. If spontaneity is to characterize the "subject" essentially, two things are first required. Selfhood must be clarified ontologically, so as to grasp the "of itself" adequately; and the same clarification must prefigure the character of happening proper to a self, so as to determine the kind of movement that "beginning" is. The selfhood of the self that already underlies all spontaneity lies in transcendence. Projecting, casting-over letting-prevail of world is freedom. Only because freedom constitutes transcendence can it show itself in existing Dasein as a distinctive kind of causality. The interpretation of freedom as "causality" already moves within a definite understanding of ground. Freedom as transcendence is not merely one "kind" of ground but the origin of ground as such. Freedom is freedom for ground. Kant's cosmological freedom, the power to begin a series of itself (A 533/B 561), is the target; the summer 1930 course *On the Essence of Human Freedom* (GA 31) pursues the critique at length. The decisive inversion: causality presupposes ground, so freedom cannot be a kind of causality if it is the origin of ground.

(165) The original relation of freedom to ground is called grounding. In grounding, freedom gives ground and takes it. This grounding, rooted in transcendence, is strewn into a manifold of ways, of which there are three: grounding as instituting (*Stiften*), grounding as taking up a basis (*Bodennehmen*), and grounding as justifying (*Begründen*). If these belong to transcendence, the titles "instituting" and "taking up a basis" cannot have an everyday ontic sense; their sense must be transcendental. In what way is Dasein's transcending a grounding in these three ways? "Gives ground and takes it" anticipates the first two ways, the giving of possibilities and the being taken in by beings. "Strewn" (*gestreut*) takes up the 1928 doctrine of Dasein's transcendental dispersion (GA 26, § 10): multiplicity belongs to the origin itself rather than to its decline.

(165) "Instituting" is listed "first" on purpose. It does not let the others emerge from it, and it is neither the most familiar nor the first recognized kind of grounding. Yet it has a priority, shown by the fact that the preceding illumination of transcendence could not avoid it. This "first" grounding is simply the projection of the for-the-sake-of. If this free letting-prevail of world was determined as transcendence, and if the other ways of grounding necessarily belong to world-projection as grounding, then neither transcendence nor freedom has yet been fully determined. World-projection always implies that Dasein, in and through the surpassing, comes back to beings as such: the for-the-sake-of cast ahead points back to the whole of beings that can be unveiled in this world-horizon, which always includes, at whatever level of articulation and explicitness, beings that are Dasein and beings that are not. Heidegger concedes that Part II gave only one of the three ways and so an incomplete account of transcendence. Instituting has priority without being the source, a structure he will explain by the priority of the future within temporality.

(166) In world-projection, however, these beings are not yet manifest in themselves; they would have to remain concealed unless projecting Dasein were already in the midst of them. "In the midst of" means neither occurring among other beings nor explicitly directing oneself toward them. It belongs to transcendence: what surpasses and so raises itself must find itself, as such, among beings. As attuned, Dasein is taken in (*eingenommen*) by beings, so that, belonging to beings, it is attuned throughout by them. Transcendence means world-projection, such that the one projecting is already pervaded and attuned by the beings it surpasses. With this absorption by beings, which belongs to transcendence, Dasein has taken up a basis in beings, won "ground". This "second" grounding does not arise after the "first" but is "simultaneous" with it. That does not mean both are present in the same now; it means world-projection and absorption by beings, as ways of grounding, belong to one temporality, in that they co-constitute its temporalizing. Just as the future comes first "in" time but temporalizes only insofar as time, having-been and present included, temporalizes in its specific unity, so the ways of grounding that spring from transcendence show the same connection. The correspondence holds because transcendence is rooted in the essence of time, its ecstatic-horizonal constitution; note 60 adds that the temporal interpretation of transcendence is deliberately left aside throughout. The mapping Heidegger leaves implicit is plain: instituting answers to the future, taking up a basis to having-been (thrownness), and the third way, justifying, to the present. "Absorption" (*Eingenommenheit*) is the transcendental name for what *Being and Time* called attunement and thrownness; note 55's claim that nature is manifest through attunement is here given its systematic place.

(166–167) Dasein could not, as the being it is, be attuned through by beings, and so for instance be embraced by them, captivated (*benommen*) by them, and resonated through by them; it would lack all room for play (*Spielraum*) for this, unless an upsurge of world, even if only a dawning of world, went along with the absorption. The world unveiled may be conceptually opaque or not transparent at all; world may even be interpreted as a being among others; explicit knowledge of Dasein's transcending may be lacking; the freedom that brings world-projection with it may scarcely be awake. Still, Dasein is absorbed by beings only as being-in-the-world. Dasein grounds (institutes) world only as grounding itself in the midst of beings. The two ways condition each other: there is no attunement without world, and no world-projection except from amid beings. Captivation without world is precisely what the winter course of 1929–30 (GA 29/30) will ascribe to the animal; the word *benommen* already marks the boundary.

(167) Instituting grounding, as the projection of Dasein's own possibilities, implies that Dasein in each case swings beyond itself. The projection of possibilities is by its essence always richer than the possession already resting in the one projecting. Dasein has such a possession because, as projecting, it finds itself amid beings, and through its facticity alone certain other possibilities are already withdrawn from it. Yet precisely this withdrawal of certain possibilities of its ability-to-be-in-the-world, contained in its absorption, first brings the "actually" graspable possibilities of world-projection toward Dasein as its world. The withdrawal gives the binding force of the remaining projection the power to prevail in Dasein's realm of existence. Corresponding to the two ways of grounding, transcendence is at once excess (*Überschwung*) and withdrawal (*Entzug*). That the world-projection, in each case exceeding, becomes powerful and a possession only in withdrawal is at the same time a transcendental document of the finitude of Dasein's freedom. Does the finite essence of freedom as such show itself here? Finitude does not limit an otherwise infinite freedom; it is what makes freedom binding. Without withdrawal, possibility would have no force, and the obligation derived from freedom at 164 would be empty. The closing question prepares the claim of 175 that finitude is revealed in freedom for ground.

(167) For interpreting the manifold grounding of freedom, the essential first step is to see the unity of the two ways of grounding discussed so far, which comes to light in this transcendental interplay of excess and withdrawal. The unity of grounding is not a common genus but a reciprocal play, the formula the closing section will need (171).

(167–168) Dasein, however, is a being that not only finds itself amid beings but also comports itself toward beings, and so toward itself. This comportment is usually even equated with transcendence. That misjudges transcendence, but the transcendental possibility of intentional comportment must still become a problem; and if intentionality is a distinctive constitution of Dasein's existence, an illumination of transcendence cannot pass it over. The demotion of intentionality at 135 left a debt, which this unit acknowledges.

(168) World-projection makes possible a prior understanding of the being of beings, though this cannot be shown here, but is not itself a relation of Dasein to beings. Absorption, which lets Dasein find itself amid beings and attuned by them (never without the unveiling of world), is not a comportment toward beings either. Both, however, in their unity, are the transcendental enabling of intentionality, such that as ways of grounding they co-temporalize a third: grounding as justifying (*Be-gründen*). In it Dasein's transcendence takes over the enabling of the making-manifest of beings in themselves, the possibility of ontic truth. The hyphen marks the prefix that makes the verb transitive: to ground something, to give it grounds. The third way returns the argument to Part I, where ontic truth was rooted in ontological truth; now that rooting is itself a way of grounding.

(168–169) "Justifying" is not meant here in the narrow, derivative sense of proving ontic-theoretical propositions, but in a fundamentally original sense: it means making possible the why-question as such. To make visible the originally grounding character of justifying is to illuminate the transcendental origin of the why as such. What is sought is not what occasions the why-question to arise factually in Dasein, but the transcendental possibility of the why at all. Transcendence must therefore be questioned as determined by the two ways of grounding already discussed. Instituting grounding, as world-projection, provides possibilities of existence. To exist always means to comport oneself, attuned amid beings, toward beings, toward beings unlike Dasein, toward oneself and toward others like oneself, such that in this attuned comportment Dasein's own ability-to-be is at issue. In world-projection an excess of the possible is given, and with regard to it, and in being pervaded by the beings (the actual) that press around in attunement, the why springs forth. The why is born in the gap between excess of possibility and pressure of the actual: only a being for which more is possible than is actual can ask why something is so.

(169) Because the first two ways of grounding belong together in transcendence, the springing forth of the why is transcendentally necessary. With its origin, the why already multiplies. Its basic forms are: why thus and not otherwise? why this and not that? why something at all and not nothing? In every why, however expressed, there already lies a pre-conceptual pre-understanding of what-being, how-being and being (nothing) as such. This understanding of being first makes the why possible; it already contains the first-and-last primal answer to all questioning. As the most prior of all answers, the understanding of being gives the first-and-last justification. In it transcendence as such justifies. Because being and the constitution of being are unveiled in it, transcendental justifying is called ontological truth. The answer precedes the question: every why already moves within an understanding of being that answers it in advance. The three forms correspond to how-being, what-being and that-being, and so recall Aristotle's three grounds of the introduction; the third, "why something rather than nothing?", becomes the fundamental question of metaphysics in *Introduction to Metaphysics* (1935). A 1929 marginal note recasts the "primal answer" as the relation of being to the human essence, and asks why genuine thinking of being is no questioning, a theme of the later Heidegger's hearing of the "pledge".

(169–170) This justifying lies "at the ground" of all comportment toward beings, so that beings can become manifest in themselves, as the beings they are and as they are, only in the light of the understanding of being. Because all becoming-manifest of beings (ontic truth) is pervaded in advance by this justifying, all ontic discovering and disclosing must be "justifying" in its way, that is, must demonstrate itself (*sich ausweisen*). In demonstration the being is cited that the what- and how-being of the being in question, and its kind of unveiling (truth), require; this being then shows itself, for instance, as "cause" or as "motive" for a connection of beings already manifest. Because Dasein's transcendence, as projecting and attuned, justifies by forming understanding of being, and because this grounding is equiprimordial with the other two in the unity of transcendence, that is, springs from Dasein's finite freedom, Dasein can in its factual demonstrations and justifications dispense with "grounds", suppress the demand for them, pervert and conceal them. Owing to this origin of justification and demonstration, it is left to freedom in each case how far demonstration is pursued, and whether it understands itself as proper justification, that is, as the unveiling of its transcendental possibility. Though being is always unveiled in transcendence, this does not require ontological, conceptual grasp. Transcendence as such can therefore remain concealed and be known only through an "indirect" interpretation. It is unveiled even so, since it lets beings break open in the basic constitution of being-in-the-world, in which the self-unveiling of transcendence shows itself. But transcendence unveils itself explicitly as the origin of grounding when grounding is brought to spring forth in its threefoldness. Ground accordingly means possibility, basis, demonstration. The threefold, strewn grounding of transcendence first produces, in an originally unifying way, the whole in which a Dasein can exist. Freedom is, in this threefold way, freedom for ground. The happening of transcendence as grounding is the forming of the room for play of irruption (*Einbruchspielraum*) for the factual self-maintenance of factual Dasein amid beings as a whole. Causes and motives are regional forms of demonstration, so causality is derived from justifying, not the reverse. Because justifying springs from freedom, the refusal or perversion of grounds is a structural possibility, which prepares the "non-essence" of 173–174. And the coinage *Einbruchspielraum* joins "irruption" (*Einbruch*, 159), the breaking of Dasein into beings, with "room for play" (*Spielraum*, 166), the Kantian "play" of world (153).

(170–171) Do we, then, reduce the traditional fourfold of grounds to three, or do the three ways of grounding coincide with the three variants of Aristotle's "first from which"? The comparison cannot be made so externally. What is peculiar to the first exhibition of the "four grounds" is that it does not yet distinguish in principle between transcendental grounds and specifically ontic causes; the former count merely as the "more general" form of the latter. The originality of the transcendental grounds and their specific character as grounds stay concealed under the formal characterization of "first" and "highest" beginnings. They therefore lack unity, which can consist only in the equiprimordiality of the transcendental origin of threefold grounding. The essence of "the" ground cannot even be sought, let alone found, by asking for a general genus to be reached by "abstraction". The essence of ground is the transcendentally arising threefold strewing of grounding into world-projection, absorption in beings, and ontological justification of beings. The doubt raised in the introduction (124) is answered: the unity of ground is not the unity of a genus but the equiprimordial origin of three ways of grounding in one transcendence.

(171) Only for this reason does even the earliest inquiry into the essence of ground prove entangled with the task of illuminating the essence of being and truth. Aristotle's list included the grounds of what-being, that-being and being-true because grounding is, in its origin, the unveiling of being and the possibility of truth.

(171) Can one not still ask why these three mutually belonging components of transcendence are given the same title, "grounding"? Is this only an artificially forced, playful verbal commonality? Or are the three ways of grounding, after all, identical in one respect, though each differently? The question must be answered yes. But the meaning in which the three inseparable ways of grounding correspond to one another, unitarily yet strewn, cannot be clarified at the "level" of the present inquiry. A hint must suffice: instituting, taking up a basis, and giving right (*Rechtgebung*) each spring in their way from the care for constancy and subsistence (*Beständigkeit*, *Bestand*), which is itself possible only as temporality. A 1929 marginal note specifies: temporality as grounded in Temporality (*Temporalität*), the time of being itself. The unity of ground is deferred to the unpublished third division of *Being and Time*, "Time and Being". "Giving right" names the third way afresh and confirms the juridical sense of justifying. The phrase "care for constancy" carries an irony the later Heidegger would exploit: the constancy that here belongs to Dasein's care is the same constant presence his marginalia (127) diagnose as the hidden meaning of being in metaphysics.

(172) Turning deliberately away from this problem and looking back at the starting point, Heidegger asks briefly whether anything, and what, has been gained for the problem of the principle of reason. The principle says that every being has its ground. The preceding first explains why this is so. Because being, understood in advance, originally justifies, every being as a being announces "grounds" in its way, whether these are explicitly grasped and properly determined or not. Because "ground" is a transcendental essential character of being in general, the principle of reason holds of beings. And ground belongs to the essence of being because there is (*es gibt*) being, not beings, only in transcendence, as world-projecting, attuned grounding. A principle about beings holds because of a character of being, and that character holds because being "is given" only in Dasein's transcendence, as *Being and Time* § 43c put it. The phrase *es gibt* is the seed of the later thinking of the "it gives".

(172) Second, it has become clear that the "birthplace" of the principle lies neither in the essence of the assertion nor in propositional truth, but in ontological truth, that is, in transcendence itself. Freedom is the origin of the principle of reason, for in freedom, the unity of excess and withdrawal, is grounded the justifying that forms itself as ontological truth. The metaphor of birth is taken back from Leibniz (129), who placed the birth of the principle in the nature of propositional truth; Heidegger moves it two levels down, past ontological truth, to freedom.

(172–173) Coming from this origin, we not only understand the inner possibility of the principle but also notice something curious and hitherto unexplained in its formulations, which the everyday formula suppresses. Leibniz in particular has formulations that express a seemingly negligible moment of its content. Schematically: there is a reason why this exists rather than something else; why it exists thus rather than otherwise; why something exists rather than nothing. The "why" appears as "why rather than". Again the first problem is not by what route and means these questions, posed factually in ontic comportments, can be decided. What needs clarifying is why "rather than" could attach itself to "why" at all. The three Leibnizian formulas reproduce exactly the three basic forms of the why derived at 169, so the history of the principle confirms the transcendental derivation.

(173) Every demonstration must move within a range of the possible, because, as intentional comportment toward beings, it is already subject in its possibility to an explicit or implicit (ontological) justification. This justification by its essence always prescribes ranges of latitude (*Ausschlagbereiche*) of the possible, the character of possibility varying with the constitution of being of the being to be unveiled, because the being (constitution of being) that justifies is rooted, as transcendental bindingness for Dasein, in Dasein's freedom. The reflection of this origin of the essence of ground in the grounding of finite freedom appears in the "rather than" of the formulas. Yet clarifying the concrete transcendental connections between "ground" and "rather than" again presses toward clarifying the idea of being as such (what- and how-being, something, nothing, and nothingness). The "rather than" is the trace, inside the principle, of the excess of possibility over the actual (167). *Ausschlag* is the swing of a needle or pendulum; the image restates the "room for play" in which possibilities range. "Nothing and nothingness" point to "What Is Metaphysics?" and to the nullity of *Being and Time* § 58.

(173) In its traditional form and role, the principle of reason has remained stuck in the externalization that any first clarification of what is "principial" necessarily brings with it. To declare it a "principle", perhaps placing it beside the principles of identity and contradiction or even deriving it from the latter, does not lead to its origin but amounts to cutting off all further questioning. Moreover, the principles of identity and contradiction are not only transcendental too; they point back to something more original that has no propositional character but belongs to the happening of transcendence as such (temporality). The target is the rationalist derivation of the principle of reason from that of contradiction (Wolff, *Ontologia* § 70) and its classification among the "first principles" of logic. The claim that identity itself points to something non-propositional anticipates *Identity and Difference* (1957).

(173–174) And so the principle of reason, too, makes mischief with the essence of ground, its non-essence (*Unwesen*), and in the sanctioned form of a principle holds down a problematic that would first loosen it. This "non-essence" is not to be blamed on the supposed "superficiality" of individual philosophers and cannot be overcome by some allegedly more radical "progress". Ground has its non-essence because it springs from finite freedom, and freedom cannot withdraw from what springs from it in this way. Ground, arising in transcending, folds back upon freedom, and freedom as origin itself becomes "ground". Freedom is the ground of ground. This is not a formal, endless "iteration". The ground-being of freedom does not have the character of one of the ways of grounding, as one is always inclined to think, but is determined as the grounding unity of the transcendental strewing of grounding. As this ground, however, freedom is the abyss (*Ab-grund*) of Dasein. Not that the single free comportment is groundless; rather, freedom in its essence as transcendence places Dasein, as ability-to-be, into possibilities that gape open before its finite choice, that is, in its destiny. The introduction's remark on non-essence (126) is now explained: the distortion of ground is structural, because what grounds is finite freedom, which can dispense with grounds (170). "Ground of ground" escapes regress because freedom is not a fourth ground beside the three but their unity. The hyphen in *Ab-grund* splits the word into "away from" and "ground": freedom grounds by withholding a further ground, opening a space of possibilities rather than determining a choice. Destiny refers to *Being and Time* § 74. A 1929 marginal note judges the passage still "the vain attempt to think Da-sein while screening off the truth of being in its turning (*Kehre*)".

(174) But Dasein, in surpassing beings in world-projection, must surpass itself in order to understand itself, from this elevation, as abyss. This abyssal character is nothing that would open itself to a dialectic or to psychological analysis. The breaking open of the abyss in grounding transcendence is rather the primal movement that freedom performs with us, thereby "giving us to understand", that is, prescribing as original content of world, that the more originally this content is grounded, the more simply it strikes the heart of Dasein, its selfhood in action. The non-essence of ground is therefore "overcome" only in factual existing, never eliminated. Freedom acts on us rather than being something we have; GA 31 formulates the same reversal as "the human being is a possibility of freedom". "Giving to understand" returns to 157, where Dasein gives itself to understand from its world; the abyss is the deepest content of that self-understanding. The non-essence cannot be removed by a better theory of ground, only taken over in existing, as inauthenticity is in *Being and Time*.

(174–175) If transcendence, as freedom for ground, is understood first and last as abyss, the essence of what was called Dasein's absorption in and by beings is sharpened too. Though amid beings and attuned by them, Dasein is thrown, as free ability-to-be, among beings. That it is a self in possibility, and factually a self in accordance with its freedom, that transcendence temporalizes itself as primal happening, does not lie within the power of this freedom itself. Such powerlessness (thrownness) is not first the result of beings pressing in on Dasein; it determines Dasein's being as such. All world-projection is therefore thrown. The clarification of the essence of Dasein's finitude from its constitution of being must precede any "self-evident" positing of the finite "nature" of the human being, any description of properties that follow from finitude, and still more any hasty "explanation" of their ontic origin. Freedom is powerless over its own being free: it does not posit itself, which excludes reading it as self-positing in the idealist manner. The demand that finitude be clarified ontologically before any anthropology restates the position Heidegger defended against Cassirer at Davos in March 1929 and in *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics* (§§ 41–45). A 1929 marginal note distinguishes the finitude of Dasein from the finitude of the human being, a distinction the text implies but does not state.

(175) The essence of Dasein's finitude reveals itself in transcendence as freedom for ground. The question raised at 167 is answered: finitude is not a property added to freedom but the very way freedom grounds. A 1929 marginal note revokes the essay's central thesis outright: "But freedom has nothing in common with grounding and ground, any more than with cause and causing and every way of 'effecting' and 'making'." For the later Heidegger, linking freedom to ground keeps it within the metaphysics of production that the essay tried to leave behind.

(175) And so the human being, as existing transcendence that swings beyond into possibilities, is a creature of distance. Only through original distances, which he forms in his transcendence toward all beings, does true nearness to things begin to rise in him. And only the ability to hear into the distance temporalizes for Dasein, as a self, the awakening of the answer of the Dasein of others, in being-with whom it can surrender its I-hood in order to win itself as an authentic self. The closing image converts excess into distance: because Dasein exceeds every being toward world, it can let things come near. Hearing echoes *Being and Time* § 34, where hearing is Dasein's openness to others, and "answer" echoes the "primal answer" of the understanding of being (169). The essay ends where the charge of solipsism (157–158) began: selfhood, not I-hood, is the authentic self, and it is won in being-with, by giving up the I.

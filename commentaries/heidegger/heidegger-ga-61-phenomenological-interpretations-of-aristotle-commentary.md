# **Phenomenological Interpretations of Aristotle: Introduction to Phenomenological Research**

## **Commentary on Martin Heidegger, *Gesamtausgabe* Band 61 (Winter Semester 1921/22)**

---

## **Part I: Aristotle and the Reception of Aristotle**

### **A. What Does History of Philosophy Mean?**

The lecture opens by framing its central subject: the investigation of a past philosophy — in this case, Aristotle's — falls under the heading of "history of philosophy." But Heidegger immediately challenges the self-evidence of this designation, asking what it really means to engage historically with philosophy.

Heidegger identifies a first way in which the history of philosophy has been pursued: always from within a particular cultural consciousness (*Bildungsbewußtsein*). What currently dominates is a typologizing intellectual history (*Geistesgeschichte*) that understands itself as strict factual research operating within a specific mode of positing and intending facts. For this so-called "exact" research, anything beyond its own framework counts as idle talk — even the attempt to bring its own conditionedness and standpoint into view. Philosophy is placed alongside science, art, religion, and the like, and through this grouping it is pre-conceptually determined as something historically objective, bearing objective and object-like relations and properties. The problem, Heidegger signals, is that this mode of investigation has already made a decision about what philosophy is before it has even begun to ask the question.

A second, contrasting approach is then introduced: the historical dimension of philosophy can only be grasped in the very act of philosophizing itself. It is accessible only as existence is accessible — from out of purely factical life, and therefore with and through history. But this entails two demands for principled clarity: first, regarding the sense of the enactment (*Vollzugssinn*) of philosophizing, and second, regarding the enactment- and being-connection of philosophizing to the historical and to history. Heidegger thus insists from the outset that the relationship between philosophizing and its own historicity is not an external or accidental matter but belongs to the inner problematic of philosophy itself.

These questions, Heidegger continues, can neither be shunted aside nor dealt with in advance, as if one could first get them settled in isolation and then proceed to "work with" the results. That would contradict the very inner problematic of the questions themselves. Rather, it is precisely by taking seriously the task of engaging philosophically with the history of philosophy — without compromises from the side of objective historiography — that the decisive problematic reveals itself as concrete, determinate, and radical. Philosophy is historical (i.e., enactment-historically understanding) cognition of factical life. It must arrive at a categorial (existential) understanding and articulation — a knowing enacted in performance — in which what is inseparable is interpreted not as a combination and origin derived from what has been traditionally separated, but positively, from the fundamental comportment toward factical life, life as such.

Heidegger then introduces a decisive consideration about the relationship between philosophy and revelation. Insofar as ruinance and questionability (*Fraglichkeit*) are experienced, and philosophy decides to explicate its own factical being radically, it relinquishes the possibility of holding fast to revelation or to any certification of its possessions and the possibilities of possession — not because it wants to be presuppositionless, but because it originally stands in a prior-having (*Voraushabe*) of the factical. Questionability and questioning sharpen the comportment toward history — the how of the historical. Everything is set upon confrontation, upon understanding in and out of this confrontation. This existentially determined, understanding confrontation appears "one-sided" from the outside — but the idea that one reaches understanding by calmly and objectively doing justice to history in some unknown form of rest is dismissed as weakness and convenience. The confrontational tendency has its own radical power of illumination and disclosure.

The scope of the history of philosophy is then delimited. In ordinary usage, the term covers the manifold succession of philosophical opinions, theories, systems, and maxims from the seventh century BCE to the present, specifically those philosophies that developed within the life-context of Greek civilization and which subsequently entered into the history of Christianity, undergoing various transformations and new formations in the medieval and modern periods. Heidegger restricts the meaning of "history of philosophy" to this temporal and regional scope — not merely because the treatment of other philosophies tends toward dilettantism and intellectual mischief, but because this restriction follows from the sense of philosophy itself.

How the history of philosophy comes into view for any present, how far it can be understood, how strongly it can be appropriated, and how decisively it can be subjected to critique — all of this depends on how genuinely philosophical the philosophy is for which and in which this history exists, and in which someone relates to history while living. This means two things: first, that one stands in fundamental questioning, and second, that one is engaged in concrete research as answer-seeking. What is decisive is the radical and clear development of the hermeneutical situation as the temporalizing (*Zeitigung*) of the philosophical problematic itself.

In every generation, certain possibilities of access to history as such become established — certain fundamental conceptions of total history, certain estimations of individual epochs, and certain "preferences" for individual philosophies become fixed. Heidegger then announces that the relationship of the present to Aristotle is determined in three respects. Beyond these, however, there has existed since Aristotle an unspoken influence in modes of seeing and, above all, in patterns of articulation — logic being the prime example. This serves as a forward reference to the radical and central problematic that the lecture will pursue.

### **B. The Reception of Aristotelian Philosophy**

#### **a) Middle Ages and Modernity**

Heidegger traces the first major line of Aristotle-reception: Aristotle enjoys a secure, positive valuation grounded in the high scholasticism of the Christian Middle Ages, within the life- and culture-conception determined by the Catholic-ecclesial confession.

A counter-movement arose with the renewal of Kantian philosophy in the 1860s. Neo-Kantianism, as it gained influence, took a principally opposed stance toward the aforementioned positive valuation of Aristotle. The renewal of Kant was specifically "epistemological" (*erkenntnistheoretisch*), leading to the formation of the philosophical discipline known as "theory of knowledge." For this reading, Kant's "critique" was essentially the grounding of mathematical natural science and scientific theory, and Kant was the "destroyer" of old metaphysics and empty speculation. From this standpoint, Aristotle was cast as the specifically uncritical philosopher — naive metaphysics. This was mediated by a quick glance at the general opinion that medieval metaphysics had its full archetype in Aristotle, whom the Middle Ages honored as "the Philosopher." Thus the first great and radical scientific human being was placed in the ranks of supposed obscurantists.

Heidegger then draws an important distinction: Kant and Aristotle share the assumption that the external world is there. For Aristotle, cognition of it is not a problem; he dealt with cognition as the illumination of the surrounding world in an entirely different way. One can call him a "realist" only insofar as he does not even ask about the existence of the external world. For Kant, shaped by Aristotelian conceptuality and Descartes' position, cognition is a problem in a quite different aspect — that of science in particular — which receives a very specific solution. From this standpoint one cannot stamp Aristotle as a realist or invoke him as a crown witness, quite apart from the fact that Kant is equally misunderstood in the process. The confusion of the most heterogeneous motives, questions, answers, and procedures in the problem of knowledge reaches its highest pitch in Nicolai Hartmann, who retains the old problematics and titles while hoping that the idea of metaphysics can still help.

The opposition to Kantianism, usually not very thorough, was driven toward an apologetics for Aristotle that had to run in the same direction as Neo-Kantianism itself. Thus Aristotle was turned into an "epistemologist" and a crown witness for epistemological realism. The polemical stance of Neo-Kantianism toward Aristotle became entrenched in modern cultural consciousness. Heidegger notes the rootless haste of the present: philosophers who half a decade earlier had sniffed condescendingly at Aristotle's name now proclaim his unrecognized greatness and even "depth" — then as now without serious knowledge.

The polemically negative evaluation of Neo-Kantianism fell victim to the erroneous assumption that Aristotle had even the slightest connection with the Middle Ages or Kant. The reverse is in fact the case. But the intellectual-historical connections decisive for the present spiritual situation have not yet been grasped in their root-strands; the decisive formulation of the problem is still lacking. Philological-historical research in tracing literary filiations (doxographically) has been fruitful and necessarily brings with it a certain co-interpretation of the content of the relevant literature.

#### **b) Prior Hellenization of Christian Life-Consciousness**

The Christian life-consciousness of early and high scholasticism, in which the actual reception and thus a very specific interpretation of Aristotle took place, had already passed through a "Hellenization." Even the earliest Christian life-contexts temporalized themselves in an environment whose expressive orientation was co-determined by specifically Greek interpretation of existence and its conceptuality (terminology). Through Paul, in the apostolic age, and especially in the age of "patristics," an assimilation (*Einbilden*) into the Greek lifeworld took place.

Despite the uncontested scientific achievements of research in the history of dogma, this intellectually decisive process has not been grasped in its deepest meaning-laden interconnections and is therefore not yet ripe for philosophical problematic and discussion. The reasons are manifold: the state of theology, the research direction of the history of dogma itself, the state of research into Greek philosophy. The main reason lies in the absence of a principled problematic into which the processes in question could be placed — namely, the problematic of existence (*Dasein*), factical life, and immanent interpretation.

Against the scholasticism consolidated through the reception of Aristotle, further transformed through Scotism and Occamism and simultaneously loosened in its experiential vitality through Taulerian mysticism, Luther carried out his religious and theological counter-thrust. In the reception, continuation, and partial suppression of the new motifs of Lutheran theology, there arose the Protestant scholasticism to which Melanchthon directed Aristotelian motifs with specific interpretations. This dogmatics, with its essential Aristotelian directives, is the root-soil of German Idealism.

The decisive conceptual structures and leading joints of the comprehension and interpretation of existence in this philosophical epoch are charged with the intellectual history just characterized. Any serious research into German Idealism must take its departure from the theological situation of the time. Fichte, Schelling, and Hegel were theologians, and Kant — if one does not want to reduce him to the clattering skeleton of a so-called epistemologist — can only be understood theologically. Dilthey had a sure instinct for these connections but had to work with inadequate methodological and conceptual means, which blocked his path to a radical grasp of the problems.

#### **c) Philological-Historical Research**

Alongside the two opposing directions of positive valuation and rejection of Aristotle, there runs, fortunately barely touched by either, a fruitful philological-historical investigation of the Aristotelian writings throughout the nineteenth century to the present. This research originates with Schleiermacher's call for a critical complete edition of Aristotle, taken up by the Berlin Academy of Sciences. The Academy edition remains the basis for citing Aristotle, though it is far from the final word on the difficult textual constitution of the Aristotelian writings. Later, the same Academy undertook the edition of the Greek commentators on Aristotle (*Commentaria in Aristotelem Graeca*, 1882–1909). This provided, for the first time, a broad and secure foundation for effective philosophical research on Aristotle.

From this philological research a secondary line branched off with Trendelenburg, whose student Brentano became of decisive significance for contemporary philosophy in its main currents (with the exception of the Marburg School). This claim loses its character of exaggeration, Heidegger insists, when one views the development of modern philosophy not from the outside, clinging to the external succession of schools and directions, but when one follows the genuinely effective problems, forces, and motifs.

With Brentano, Husserl saw what was decisive and therefore most radically went beyond him, whereas others influenced by Brentano took up individual interpretations, reflected on them, but never arrived at a genuine understanding — that is, a problem-advancing continuation. This sets up the transition to Part II, where the question "What is philosophy?" will be directly addressed.

## **Part II: What Is Philosophy?**

The investigations that follow have neither the intention of mounting a philosophical rescue and apology for Aristotle, nor do they aim at a renewal of Aristotle or the initiation of an Aristotelianism patched together with the results of modern sciences. These are not serious goals for philosophical research, whether they concern Aristotle, Kant, or Hegel. The interpretations of Aristotelian treatises and lectures arise rather from a concrete philosophical problematic — one in which the investigation of Aristotelian philosophy is not merely an incidental offshoot, a supplement, or historical illustration, but constitutes a fundamental piece of the problematic itself. The problematic alone determines the weight and direction of the investigations.

For someone encountering such a problematic for the first time, even a first step in a flickering illumination requires a preliminary rough indication of the direction in which the investigation proceeds. And for someone who has already gained a certain firm foothold, and even more so for someone who believes they are secure in their grasp and execution of tasks, there must be a recurring return from the concrete work itself to the methodological question of conscience: Is the goal genuinely original and the path genuinely appropriate?

A recurrent presentation of the goal and way of the investigation, corresponding to each achieved level of the problem, is an indispensable propaedeutic — because it is a principled necessity. The two bare questions in philosophy are: What is the main thing? What is the genuinely directed questioning aimed at it? What is the real subject matter, and what must be the relentlessly principled topic of discourse in philosophy?

A concretely determined philosophical research problem, if it is genuine, will in its directional aim run toward the end that philosophy as such must have fixed for itself. What is philosophy? This must be set in sufficient clarity — sufficient for the situation and the problematic in which the question is posed — if every concrete investigation is to have its secure direction and corresponding methodological cleanliness and genuine objectivity.

### **Chapter 1: The Task of Definition**

At the question "What is philosophy?," posed in this formula and on this occasion — that is, at and for the starting point of a philosophical investigation itself — a manifold torment typically arises, from which one seeks to escape by various side-paths and compromises. This is a sign that the principled sense of the question and the task have not been clarified.

The failures in treating this question and definitional task are twofold: on the one hand, the question is underestimated — taken too lightly precisely at this juncture; on the other, it is overestimated — one gets lost in protracted efforts that eventually degenerate into a fixation on the question itself, which quietly transforms the question overnight. The truth does not lie in the middle, in some bourgeois compromise. If there is anything that does not exist in philosophy, it is compromises as ways of reaching the matter itself. A brief discussion of the two modes of failure can prepare a genuine understanding of the question and its answer.

The underestimations are themselves twofold. First: discussions about the concept of philosophy are unfruitful, logico-methodological games; one should follow the example of the sciences, which do not first engage in lengthy deliberations about their own concept but simply begin. The mathematician and the philologist will promise themselves little benefit from such empty speculations. Philosophy too should be advised to take concrete hold and to abandon the fruitless business of first concocting a universally valid definition.

This rejection is grounded in the fact that the concept and task of philosophy are instinctively set according to the model of some concrete particular science. There prevails (within a certain situation of the particular sciences) a principled foreignness and insensitivity to philosophy's own task, which is not necessary and, as genuine, not only does not hinder concrete research but enables the formation and safeguarding of pre-conceptual understanding (*Vorhabeausbildung*).

The second underestimation comes from the opposite direction. Precisely because philosophy is really more than science — something "deeper," "higher" — it cannot be pinned down in a pedantic definition. To dwell on such definitional questions is the hallmark of the "Wagner soul" that "rejoices when it finds earthworms." Philosophy cannot and should not be defined; philosophy can only be "experienced" (and then the story is over).

The overestimations are also twofold. First, the tendency aims at obtaining a definition general enough to encompass every concrete historical form of philosophy, and at the same time demands that the definition be a proper and strict one satisfying the requirements of school logic. The securing of such a definition must be carried out in advance, requiring a comparative survey of the entire history of philosophy and a delimitation of how philosophy relates to the individual sciences, to art, to religion. On this path, a sufficient definition is to be won, on whose basis the elaboration of individual disciplines can then proceed.

Both failures, the underestimation as much as the overestimation, will — insofar as they come to speech here with sense and right — have to display something of a genuine intention toward the sense of philosophy and its possible mode of appropriation. This can only be seen from a full and radical tendency toward philosophy. The genuine intention in the overestimation lies in its emphasis on the necessity of a principled orientation: from the first step onward, it must be clear what is actually willed. Principles play a different and more decisive role in philosophy than in the particular sciences. The genuine intention in the underestimation lies in its emphasis on the necessity of genuinely concrete philosophizing — albeit in two fundamentally different ways.

The failure of both positions is not clarified by saying that the deficiency of each consists in ignoring the merit of the other. Rather, one must clarify wherein the positive tendency of failure lies in each case, in what respect "definition," "the task of defining," and "philosophy" are misunderstood. In this way one can work one's way toward an understanding of the question from different sides.

### **A. The Twofold Error of Overestimation**

#### **a) The Uncritical Idea of Definition**

The errors in the overestimation are of a double kind. First, the idea of definition developed by a particular formal logic is uncritically taken as the norm to which a universally valid determination of the sense of philosophy must conform. One seeks in the direction of the conceptual structures of the object prescribed by the definitional idea: *definitio fit per genus proximum et differentiam specificam*. Insofar as one holds to this definitional idea as a guiding thread — which happens even when one resignedly declares that this norm cannot be satisfied for such ultimate objects without falling into the circle that the same logic strictly forbids — insofar as one holds to this norm, one takes philosophy as an object of the same character as those for which the said definition is tailored. The rose is a plant, a plant is an organism, and so on. For entirely specific object-domains and cognitively specifically intended objects, this definitional norm has a sense. Philosophy is something — formally speaking, an object. But is it an object of the character of a rose, or of a thing, a matter? May it, simultaneously with adherence to this definitional norm, be tacitly posited in advance as such an object?

It is important at the outset to win back the original sense of definition, from which the usual idea is only a particular derivation. *Definitio: decisio, determinatio alicuius dicitur, quod tenendum et credendum declaratur, manifestatur et indicatur.* The specific grasping of the definition\! The full definition is not merely its content, the proposition\!

The usual definitional idea has the peculiarity, within its domain of validity and in view of the leading cognitive tendency, of determining the object properly and securely, so that one can adduce further illustrative cases that contribute nothing in principle. But this moment does not belong to every definition. There are precisely those that give the object indeterminately, in such a way that the enactment of understanding this peculiar definition leads to the right possibility of determination. They are definitions that first introduce the corresponding full determination. As introductory, they must prepare direction, troop strength, and ammunition, while the position of the object must be correspondingly reconnoitered.

When the demand is made, in the customary way, to define phenomenology, the answer is: there is no definition in the usual sense, and in philosophy there are no definitions of that kind at all. The questioner, long settled on an uncritical and unclear idea of definition, turns with a superior gesture away from a philosophy that cannot even define what it is.

As an object, philosophy — like every object — has its mode of genuine possession. Every object has its specific mode of access, of holding onto it, and of losing it. These latter modes are generally not seen, let alone placed in the problematic. But they are precisely the modes in which we "ordinarily" move — they constitute "habit." They will gain principled significance in the problematic to be developed (facticity). In these respective modes, formally designated as modes of having (losing is a particular how of having), there are immanently co-functioning — according to the character of the modes of having, or the what-how-being of the object — particular modes of cognitive apprehension and determination. These are not something superimposed or glued on; the mode of having the object as such is itself an addressing of the object. In every having as such, "something is said about the object." The genuine having can demand of itself an explicit discourse: it can become the task, in the how of having, to bring explicitly to discourse the what of the object.

The formal sense of definition is therefore: the situation- and pre-conceptually appropriate, from the basic experience to be won, object-addressing determination of the object in its what-how-being.

From this basis, Heidegger develops the idea of the definition further. What is now given is an idea of definition arising from a phenomenologically radical interpretation of cognition, which has different senses in different cognitive and experiential contexts. In the sense of this formal idea of definition, the task is to win the idea of the philosophical definition — that is, the definition of philosophical objects. The proper order of derivation, however, is actually that the philosophical definition is the original one; the formal idea of definition arises from it through formalization, and the definitional character in the various sciences likewise takes its origin from the philosophical, though in a different manner.

The philosophical definition is a principled one — philosophy is not a "thing"; it involves "principled having." Therefore it must be "indicative" (*anzeigend*): it shows what matters. This is only a sharper explication of the specific principle-character. It is "formally indicative" — a commitment that is content-indeterminate but enactment-determined. The phenomenological definition is one of specifically existential temporalizing: in it, the enactment of understanding proceeds so that from the basic experience, the way as indicated is traced "back" — that is, only now does the addressing become explicit, and the task (categorial research), the situational and pre-conceptual idea, is established as a problematic, so that existential basic experience as the factically decisive can be concretely taken up in concern (*Bekümmerung*).

What is important for now is this one thing: the idea of determination, the logic of apprehending the object, must be drawn from the way in which the object originally becomes accessible. Decisive for definition become the situation of life in which the object comes to experience, and the fundamental intention in which experience from the start approaches the object — as befits the sense of the situation and the pre-conceptual grasping tendency (pre-conception, *Vorgriff*).

The definitional idea of "formal" logic is sublated in this, and precisely because this idea and "formal" logic are not at all "formal" but always essentially oriented by a material object-region (things, living things, the significant) and their specific cognitive apprehension tendency (ordering collecting). The error-tendency lies in the fact that a norm of determination is uncritically brought to the object and its possible having, one that from the outset distorts the apprehension-tendency. This uncritical employment is made possible by the lack of the basic experience in which philosophizing "comes to speech," a lack that is taken as compensated by gathering together a variety of opinions and statements about what counts as philosophy, and choosing at last by custom, taste, need, convenience, and fashion.

The absence of full basic experience pushes back a radical problematic of logic, so that philosophy has not actually understood the problem of genuine logic since the time after Aristotle. Even Kant — who stated that logic had made neither a step forward nor backward since Aristotle, understanding logic in a narrow sense (of school logic) — is more deeply entangled in pseudo-Aristotelian logic than he could have realized.

#### **b) The Misrecognition of the Sense of "Principle"**

In the overestimation lies a further error-tendency springing from the same hasty, uncritical, yet genuine effort at a definition. The genuine consciousness is there: somehow it must be clear and secure what is willed, what philosophy is supposed to be; that everything else somehow depends on this; that something principled must come to speech in the determination of the concept of philosophy. Principle is that from which something in its own way "is," that on which everything depends. The object must be given in the definition so that from it the "other" depends — given so that it is had as principled.

But the sense of "principle" and "principled" is equally misrecognized. Principle here means the universal, the most general, what holds "for" everything, "in every case." The individual cases stand "under" the principle; it is the "highest," encompassing every specification. The definition of philosophy must be a principled one and must determine what it determines as principle, as the most general, so that the determination fits every particular task-domain of philosophy. The driving concern is that, with respect to the said definitional norm and the supposed idea of the principled, everything "checks out" with what is handed down and how it is handed down, with no contradictions or circles. That perhaps the tradition itself lies in an interpretation just as superficial as the definitional business is not disturbing.

But if an object is to be grasped principally, its what-how-being must come to determination precisely in the respect of its principle-character. The principled definition must make the object accessible so that its how-being is genuinely determined as being-a-principle; more precisely, the how in which it functions as principle must come to initial indication. This principled functioning is in a principled definition the determination of the object's genuine what-being. But the how-being is genuinely given in such a definition only when the genuine understanding of the definition can and must take from it the reference to the for-which — that for which the object is principle. Only with the reference to the for-which does the how of principle-being come to understanding.

A principled definition gives the object as principle. It is principle only in the being of the for-which — that is, it is had as principle only when neither it nor the principle are thematic, but when the definition is such that the object is had as principle, the tendency of this enactment-direction is awake, and the understanding takes this direction. The genuine principle is existentially-philosophically to be won only in the basic experience of passion (*Leidenschaft*). There it is unilluminated. "On principle" (*aus Prinzip*) is from the outside, "without passion," in reflection, having gone into loss. "On principle" one can be and have anything (Kierkegaard).

So precisely a principled consideration must radically know what it wills. It is not enough to emphasize the principled (one is not yet at the principle as such; one talks about it, gives and takes cognizance). Rather, one must "have" principle qua principle. Insensitivity to principles means two things: one does not concern oneself with anything principled at all, or one has it but not "principally." Principled means: to have the principle genuinely — to temporalize it illuminatingly in unilluminated passion and to take it into "keeping" (*Behalt*); that means for us: first to win this basic experience. The way is far for philosophy as research.

The overestimation genuinely seeks a principled orientation in the definition of philosophy. But with the misrecognition of the object-character goes the misrecognition of the principled functioning in question — the function of the principle's pointing to that for which it is principle is decisive in a principled definition. Instead, the definitional determinations themselves become thematic and object of proof; the for-which and thus the genuine principle-character becomes "incidental." Hence the protracted dwelling on the task.

What matters is not just to note the misrecognition but to bring the error-tendency positively to understanding. The definitional effort aims at the determining formation of a general, encompassing, universally fitting concept. But where a generically universal qua universal, functioning as principle, stands in the apprehension-tendency, there what the intended principle can be principle for is necessarily a thing (*Sache*); what the principle points to is a case. If a thing qua thing is to be grasped principally, it must be grasped in what matters for it as a thing to be grasped — in the specific object-logic of the Greeks, that is, in its genus or ultimately its highest genus and region. With respect to philosophy as object and the definitional tendency aimed at something general, this means: philosophy is posited as a thing in the pre-conception — the pre-conception guiding the definitional tendency is, with respect to the genuinely intended object, a mis-grasp.

The same error-tendency operates as in the setting up of the quite specific definitional idea tailored to things and their specific mode of apprehension. And the same deficiency shows itself: an uncritical positing of the entire principled problematic. The principle-character and principle-function — the for-which — becomes "incidental" in the ordering-collecting, typologizing classification tendency, and the access to it is an access to the incidental. If it is then supposed to become the main thing again, the concealment is already there and the way to genuine apprehension is blocked. All talk is then useless, and the appeal to praxis and action is, philosophically considered, only the abandonment of radical categorial research — an evasion and the genuine philosophical oversight (Jaspers\!).

Heidegger then makes a broader cultural diagnosis: never has there been so "unphilosophical" a time as the present, precisely because metaphysical needs are sprouting up. Talk of decline and technologization (Bergson, Spengler) remains confused as long as the phenomena in which and for which the decline takes place are not positively made into a problem. That is precisely the tendency toward radical problematic.

### **B. The Underestimation of the Task of Definition**

The same fundamental deficiencies emerge in the underestimation. Here the concrete is emphasized against the empty and merely logical deliberations and formalistic violations. The concrete is emphasized by working concretely. Heidegger makes a fiction for the sake of simplicity: that this concrete work is indeed such, that it in some way advances what matters. This fiction is justified for the first type of underestimation; for the second it is pure fiction.

#### **a) The Resolve for "Concrete Work"**

Insofar as one decides for concrete work and gets to grips, one has decided for a specific how of such working, whether the sense of such concreteness is explicitly or implicitly present. One chooses particular object-domains (e.g., psychology), grasps objects in a specific way, moves in particular conceptual structures, has particular demands regarding justification and clarity, and sees the work in a particular overall horizon of cognition and in a particular relation to history. Concrete work means: approaching the object in its concrete form. But what counts as concrete depends on what one takes the main thing to be about the object — and that depends on how the object is taken "in principle."

The first underestimation takes its ideal of concrete research from the sciences. And how? On the basis of a particular valuation: the style of the sciences impresses — the "business," the constant production of new and difficult findings, the advancing influence on human development. One sees the sciences according to their current usage and style; the decisive impression is that they dispense with principled deliberations and nonetheless arrive at rich results. One fails to recognize that every science once, at its birth, made a principled decision and lives on its basis.

If philosophy is something for which concreteness is somehow decisively important, then its principled definition must be such that it carries within itself the reference to concreteness, so that the understanding of the definition, by its own enactment- and temporalizing-sense, leads into concreteness. This is just the explication of the task of a principled definition already arrived at.

Now the attention is drawn to the fact that the for-which of the principle matters decisively. Indicatively: the concrete must be appropriated as that for which the principle "is" — one must go there\! About the "how," nothing yet. It was only attention drawn, without the necessity having become insightful and clear.

Negatively, this much is already clear: not from the model-taking at the sciences — and therefore also not by negative delimitation, as if the sciences stood as what philosophy is not. The discussion of the underestimation does not give us a positive instruction, but we take it for preparatory illustration of the moments that matter in the definition of the object called "philosophy." Indirectly, the genuine element in the failures has been brought to its proper sense and placed at its proper "site." Just as there is destruction in phenomenological research, there is, in unity with it, phenomenological existential topics.

The principled definition of the object called philosophy, and therewith the principled definition of all "philosophical objects," must be such that in the determination of the what-how of the object (temporalizing, approach, access, appropriation, safeguarding, renewal), its principle-being-function is emphasized in the decisive sense — so that the definitional content "indicates" the genuine, necessarily appropriable concreteness. The principled definition is an indicative one: what is said in the definition, the definitional content, must be understood "as indicating"; in understanding, the definitional content must be placed in relation to what is indicated, and the content, the determinations given of the object, must precisely not become thematic. Instead, the apprehending understanding must follow the indicated direction of sense.

As indicative, the definition is at the same time characterized as one that precisely does not give the object to be determined fully and properly, but only indicates it — yet as genuinely indicative, it precisely gives it principally in advance. It lies in the indication that concreteness is not available without further ado but represents a task of its own kind and for an enactment of its own constitution. The positive instruction for this is given by the further character of the definition: that it is a "formally" indicative one.

"Formally indicated" does not mean merely imagined, hinted at, suggested, so that one would be free to get hold of the object itself somewhere and somehow. Rather: indicated so that what is said has the character of the "formal" — unproper, but precisely in this "un-" at the same time the positive instruction. The emptily contentful in its sense-structure is at the same time what gives the enactment-direction.

In the formal indication lies a quite specific binding: it says that I stand at a quite specific direction of approach, and that the only way to reach the genuine is to savor and fulfill what is improperly indicated — to follow the indication. A savoring that, the more radically the understanding of the empty as formally such proceeds, the richer it becomes, because it is such that it leads into the concrete.

The formal indication must not be improperly assisted\! For the indicative and referential character, the determination "formal" says something decisive\! Object "empty" means: and yet decisive\! Not arbitrary and without approach, but precisely "empty" and direction-determining, indicating, binding. To grasp the sense fully, one needs the radical interpretation of the "formal" itself: the existential sense of the formal. The opposite is not "material," contingently contentful. Formal is also not identical with eidetic. "Formal" gives the "approach-character" of the enactment of the temporalizing of the original fulfillment of what is indicated.

The evidence regarding the adequacy of the definition to the object is no proper and original evidence; rather, this adequacy is absolutely questionable, and the definition must be understood precisely in this questionability and lack of evidence. The genuine foundation of philosophy is the radical existential grasping and temporalizing of questionability: to place oneself, life, and the decisive enactments into questionability is the fundamental grasp of all and the most radical illumination. Skepticism so understood is the beginning, and as genuine beginning it is also the end of philosophy.

#### **b) Philosophy as "Experience"**

This evidence-situation of the primal decision, the experience in which the object genuinely gives itself as what it is and how it is (the basic experience) — more precisely: the specific enactment-connection in the direction of winning this situation — is what the second mode of underestimation confusedly means with the phrase: philosophy and what it is can only be "experienced." Something unclear and confused is meant, and it is not accidental that enthusiasm (*Schwärmerei*) always somehow clings to the decisive, if in wholly impossible forms, for only so can it thoroughly ruin everything in philosophy.

In some sort of enthusiasm one takes "great" philosophies from the outside and admires their "depths." In the misguided attempt to imitate them, one falls into the fatal confusion of enthusiasm for so-called "depth" with the radical methodological approach to the principled. Where "experiencing" is so emphasized, such a philosophy must either remain privately to itself, or it must believe that the surrounding world can be brought to such experiencing through some tricks — beautiful book design, "wonderful" rhetorical style, content suited to the needs of the times (today preferably religious and metaphysical).

Or else the public communication of philosophy must have a comprehensibility and one that places itself precisely before the co-worldly decision in a specific situation. Then it must be, insofar as it aims at something principled, correspondingly radical and strict in comprehensibility, demonstrability, and the manner of presenting.

The enthusiastic intention on primal decision is seductive — one seeks what philosophy is supposed to "give" as a perverted historical "salvation." One is easily of the opinion that this situation is something fixed, temporally and locally there, to which one comes or should come, as if one could walk there like to a watchtower. One overlooks that with the temporalizing of access itself the how of the decision enters into experience, but that with the enactment — for example, of grasping the concern (*Bekümmerung*) for existence — the fundamental difficulties only begin.

This situation is not the saving shore but the leap into the drifting boat, and it then depends on getting the rope for the sails in hand and looking for the wind. One must see the difficulties; this illumination first opens up the genuine horizon onto factical life. The heirs have squandered it. To be thrust into absolute questionability and to have it seeingly — that means to genuinely grasp philosophy. The firm ground lies in grasping the questionability, that is, in the radical temporalizing of questioning. "Grasping" is concern: concretely bringing oneself into the decision in the explicit research task.

Both errors — overestimation and underestimation — are only possible and maintain their footing today on the basis of their ambiguous character. The overestimation simulates logical determinacy, rigor, and the radicalism of genuine questioning; the underestimation simulates experiential richness, "depth," and genuine originality of basic experiences. Both ambiguities coupled together suffice to make an epoch today. The one misunderstands the radicalism of genuine "logic"; the other misunderstands the originality of full concreteness. And both misunderstand the connection of these misunderstood determinacies of philosophy.

These misunderstandings are possible from one fundamental deficiency: the situation of understanding belonging to philosophy is not appropriated — more precisely: the opinion that it is available without further ado; the blindness to one's own spiritual situation, which is distinguished from every other in the previous history of the spirit precisely by being so far from the situation of understanding as ever, yet in such a way that it has in itself a specific direction of determination driven to genuine superficiality, which is decisive for the appropriation of the situation of understanding. This "decline" characterizes the leveled mode of apprehension and experience — "historical consciousness."

#### **c) The Concept of Philosophy**

There is no revelation about what philosophy is and ought to be. Is it "invented"? It can be shown that something like it "can" exist. Where, for what? For factical life. What does that mean? Must philosophy be? In some way yes, if life, existence, is to be. "Ought"? — "It is" factically there. Is there a tendency to get rid of it? The ruinant flight into the world; away from the object; the positive sense of Husserl's "reduction."

There remains only one way: to look with cold eyes and ruthlessly; "looking into": the problem of interpretation — being in philosophy\! The question cannot be carried through doctrinarily in a methodological purity that is dreamed of and does not see the ground (facticity); one must not lazily appeal to crown witnesses but must radically understand them\! One must look into what is historically there under this name — not to take it over, but to have a clear possibility and a clear resistance, the genuine direction for reflection.

There exists absolutely no right, in philosophy, to vague appeals and to working from moods and fashions, as long as the motivating force of what is not even sharply recognized as a determinate tradition (but is held to be a priori, "clear in itself") is not determined in its capacity to speak (goal-setting), or as long as one does not expressly say that one is simply recapitulating this philosophical ideal.

### **Chapter 2: The Appropriation of the Situation of Understanding**

The first thing is the appropriation of the situation of understanding; the full, concrete appropriation itself is a task that may exhaust the strength of the present generation. The appropriation must therefore be won at its starting point and outset. From the critical consideration, Heidegger draws the lesson: what matters is the motivated direction of interest; no preparation for this lies in inauthentic knowledge of things and results — that is misdirected curiosity. Interest should not aim at getting to know a matter, and no result in an external sense is to be expected — no draft of a system, no sketch of grand perspectives, no shimmering circumscription of a standpoint, nothing "other," new, unusual. There should be no misplaced tendency toward certainty and security, no calming wishes.

Genuine preparation is: positively following the idea of definition — the philosophical definition. Genuine readiness to understand, initially situation- and pre-conceptually appropriate. The pre-conception is fixed: philosophy is intended as something we want to originally appropriate, to win the fundamental relation to it in which it is genuinely there. Not to acquire a "knowledge" of it, not to orient ourselves about philosophy, no philosophical "education" and possession. Only and primarily to appropriate a certain information about what of philosophy has been and is, what philosophy has influenced or should influence.

#### **A. Pre-conception from Linguistic Usage**

The situation is brought to a first interpretive relief through the presentation of a linguistic usage (*Sprachgebrauch*). A linguistic usage comes to us from a history and has at some point grown from a specific experience. The history can fall into oblivion; the expressive tradition can break off. In sliding into a linguistic usage lies a peculiar trust in the history of the spirit, a grasping of "tradition" — of the quite specific, enactment-historical kind — but at the same time the possibility of derailment. That initially characterizes only the questionability of the starting point.

Heidegger asks us to enter into a linguistic usage — one that is available to us and somehow intelligible. With the presentation of a linguistic usage, a situation of understanding is activated. With the understanding of the linguistic usage — even an indeterminate one, but one fixed in the indicated tendency — a situation is indicated. The ongoing interpretation remains within it. From it, interpreting it, the formally indicative definition of philosophy arises for us.

The expressive tendency of the word itself is to be followed, not a discussion opened about whether the usage is rightly established, not whether it is clear enough or not, nor a tracing of its objective-historical derivation.

##### **a) Philosophy Is Philosophizing**

It is customary to say, in discussing "What is philosophy?": One should not ask in this way; one can (namely) only say what "philosophizing" is. One cannot teach and learn philosophy, only "philosophize." The usual discourse then continues — or rather, it had its target from the start: "science" has nothing to do with philosophizing; the decisive thing in philosophizing is to form a "worldview" (*Weltanschauung*), and indeed a comprehensive and secure one.

Heidegger subjects this concept of Weltanschauung to sharp criticism. It means: a system as an orderly arrangement and characterizing of the various domains and values of life and their connections — together with the "side-thought" that this provides security and determinacy for the orientation of one's own practical life. Weltanschauung further means: the ordering and determination of the principles of taking a stance toward people, values, and things, and in a particular sense: the regulation of the relation and comportment toward a so-called Absolute. To occupy oneself with such tasks and so that they are solved at the right time — that is philosophizing. The proper philosopher must first and always be a lexical individual.

Plato and Aristotle did not have the word "Weltanschauung" — that is, the experiential and attitudinal context it expresses. In this "word" — fully understood — the disaster of our present spiritual situation comes to expression. Philosophy participates in the disaster, sharpens it, precisely by orienting its problematic to Weltanschauung. Even the counter-position of "scientific philosophy" against Weltanschauungsphilosophie participates in the disaster, since in delimiting itself against Weltanschauung, the determination of science is co-determined by Weltanschauung, which remains posited as the final, though distant, goal.

Heidegger then inserts a lengthy and important remark on the expression "scientific philosophy." The expression usually conceals the problem. The question is not how philosophy relates to sciences, how it uses their results, how it relates to sciences as pre-given in the sense of a specific epistemology. In the expression lies a task, a problem (indication\!): to determine the idea of cognition, research, and method that is prescribed for the sense of philosophy itself, from itself and its basic experience. If the expression is rightly understood, it is merely an emergency aid that negatively wards off enthusiasm, superficiality, and literary scribbling; positively, only a task is indicated today — it is a "transitional expression" that must disappear. For understanding the task indicated in the expression, it is important to see that for the sake of philosophy's scientificity, one must precisely refrain from orienting oneself to the sciences, in order to discover "scientificity" all the more originally.

Returning to the main thread: the opposition of "philosophizing" against "philosophy" (teaching and the like) is examined. A comparison helps: for "biology" we do not have "to biologize"; for "philology" not "to philologize." One can form such words, but one immediately understands that in "philosophizing" something "more" is expressed: it does not just mean "doing philosophy," "occupying oneself." When we say "making music" and better: "musizieren" — a genuine musician is one who precisely is, in and with musicking, what he is. Musicking is not a mere comporting oneself toward a possible "business," mastering a technique. Between "philosophizing" and "musizieren" there exists an "analogy," though Heidegger warns against concluding from this a kinship between philosophy and art.

##### **b) Plato on Philosophizing**

Heidegger turns to Plato for historical support, citing four passages. From *Republic* VII (521c), the characterization of philosophy as the turn of the soul from the nocturnal day to the genuine — the way up to being as such. From *Republic* V (480a), the characterization of philosophers as those who draw each thing toward themselves according to its being. From the *Apology* (28e), Socrates' defense of his philosophical life as one that demands standing firm in danger, following the direction given by the god. From the *Phaedo* (61a), the characterization of philosophy as the greatest *mousikē* — rhythmic, internally ordered "forming." What matters: philosophy is a how of comporting oneself. Plato would never determine philosophy as *technē*.

The expressive tendency of the linguistic usage of "philosophy" has been brought to a first relief. "Philosophieren" is an indication of the archontic sense. This is what the linguistic usage, the playing off of "philosophieren" against "Philosophie"-doing, indicates about the peculiarity of the object (philosophy) in relation to its having. In it, a specific moment of philosophy — the comportment to it, in an autonomous sense — wants to make itself felt, and in this expressive tendency there is an echo of its peculiar earlier life.

This comportment expresses itself particularly in the saying: "studying philosophy" must be a genuine "philosophizing." The object to which one comports oneself is such that from its character it determines the comportment-toward. It lends the comportment its name, so that it expresses precisely the autonomy of the comportment: not the comporting-to but the comporting as such, the being-in-such-comporting, is what is decisive.

#### **B. Comportment**

The following are all formally indicative determinations, principally of the same methodological and expressive character as the philosophical definition. Neither nominal definitions (in the sense of old logic) nor a priori essential laws nor determinations that properly give their object.

"Comportment" (*Verhalten*) has a double meaning that Heidegger holds fast: (1) to comport oneself, to behave; (2) to comport oneself toward, to stand in relation to. The second meaning is genetically more original; the first is the surplus of sense over "having." The first equals enactment (*Vollzug*) in the broader sense, and "together" with the second, temporalizing, existence. The second is relation (*Bezug*), and detached from the first, it becomes objectified intentionality.

What we address as a comportment can be determined simultaneously in various respects: as comportment toward something (relational sense, *Bezugssinn*); as a how of formal happening, proceeding, with respect to the way it is enacted (enactment sense, *Vollzugssinn*); as the way the enactment becomes and is in and for its situation — temporalizing sense (*Zeitigungssinn*). The relation of comportment is to something; the comportment holds to something — the content (*Gehalt*). The categorical what-toward of the relation is the content-sense (*Gehaltssinn*).

##### **a) Philosophizing According to Its Relational Sense Is Cognitive Comportment**

Philosophizing is (formally indicated) a comportment. Its relational sense is to be grasped more sharply. Philosophizing according to its relational sense — the comportment toward the object in philosophy — is cognitive comportment. Cognition is an apprehending of the object "as" object and a determining of it thus apprehended. The comportment as apprehending determining holds to the object insofar as it somehow "is." The relation is held by something as a being, as being, and as being-thus.

The idea of definition is nothing other than the formal interpretation of the full sense of cognition. Each science, for example, as an enactment-connection of cognitive comportment, is determined in the contentful respect so that the objects to which it relates, as beings belonging in their connection of beings, are more or less sharply delimited. This connection of beings, as what is cared for by the relevant science, is designated as its field (*Gebiet*). Every science has its field of beings, insofar as it is cognitive comportment of the character of sciences.

Thus it seems that nothing more can be given about "philosophizing" than: "cognitive comportment toward…," where the object as cognitive object is addressed as a being. But this appearance is misleading.

##### **b) The Principled Definition of Philosophy**

At the determination of the idea of the philosophical definition, we encountered the moment of the principled. A principled definition is to be given of philosophy: the prior setting-forth of what is the main thing, what matters, in order to be able at all to direct a radical questioning at it.

Why does the search for the grounding of philosophy keep reviving? If a field were visible and delimited, a straying from it and seeking the proper field could not be possible. What philosophy as cognitive comportment is directed at must therefore always be thrown forth anew, re-captured, sought in various directions. The field — if it has one — does not lie within the total domain of the sciences. Philosophy cognizes, it is said, the common element of the cognitive fields, what underlies them. It is not particular cognition, not specialist knowledge; it is "fundamental science." Philosophical cognition aims at something ultimate, universal, highest. Because it has no field among others, its object must be sought from itself, on its own initiative. Philosophy is cognitive comportment *κατ᾽ ἐξοχήν* — the principled one. Its determining apprehension of itself must itself be principled, a principled definition.

The sense of "principle" is not self-evident; there are principles of different principle-character. Recognizing principles need not be principled cognition. It is precisely our problem to grasp radically the formally indicative determination regarding philosophizing as principled cognitive comportment. "Attitudinal cognitive comportment toward principles" is not enough; philosophical cognition must itself be principled in its enactment-character.

Philosophy is principled cognitive comportment toward beings as being (*Seiendes als Sein*). What it comports itself toward must give itself in its genuine and ultimate principle-character: beings, ultimately considered, not in relation to other beings but at themselves and as such. And what is the principled element for such beings in themselves? Being — or, more precisely, in view of the way such "being" is graspable: the "sense of being" (*Seinssinn*). Being, the sense of being, is the philosophically principled of every being; but it is not the "universal," the highest genus having beings as particular cases under it. Being is not the most comprehensive domain for each and every being, not the "highest region."

The object of the definition of philosophy is thus determined: cognitive comportment toward beings as being. Whether toward all and every being, all "fields," or indeed toward no "field" as field, remains open. What the "as" means and in what sense it is to be understood is not yet further determined.

From this determinative approach it is already evident that philosophy as "cognitive comportment" is not something like a thing, a matter. The object of the definition is not identical with the object of philosophy. The object of philosophy is co-intended in the object of the definition but does not exhaust it.

As the formally indicative principled definition of philosophy, Heidegger arrives at: Philosophy is principled cognitive comportment toward beings as being (sense of being), in such a way that in the comportment and for it, the respective being (sense of being) of the having of the comportment is decisively what matters. Philosophy is "ontology" — radical ontology, and as such phenomenological (existential, historical-intellectually-historical); or ontological phenomenology. The object of philosophy — beings as being — determines from itself (principle-function) the comportment. As principled, what matters is its being. The cognitive comportment has toward beings as being a principled relation of wholly original and radical kind: not attitude and apprehension, but such that it even and precisely through apprehension "is" what it apprehends, and apprehends what it "is."

The understanding of this definition must take it as indicative. The content is such that its appropriation is a concrete enactment-task of its own. Following the indication means bringing this concrete task into view. The whither is already indicated: the sense of being of the having of the comportment. What is decisive for the having of the object of the definition is indicated: the being of the having, and indeed the being of the having of the cognitive comportment toward beings as being. But the genuine having of a comportment qua comportment is a how of its enactment. Decisive is therefore the being of the enactment (temporalizing, the historical).

#### **C. The Situation of Access: The University**

The having of the comportment as a being, in its what-how, is to be taken into the understanding grip — in the sense of the idea of definition, of genuine definitional understanding, situation- and pre-conceptually appropriate. The determinacies of the definition are not settled with the first approach; on the contrary, they become more pressing with the advance toward genuineness of understanding. As pressing together and in growing urgency, they temporalize precisely the appropriation of the concrete.

The pre-conception itself demands its due: the genuine comportment toward the object of philosophy must be pressed upon. What is already evident: the comportment toward philosophy itself cannot be an arbitrary, half and inauthentic orienting think-about and occupational working of handed-down and picked-up problems. Rather, the more genuinely the fundamental sense of the factical situation of comportment is appropriated, the more originally and genuinely the illumination of the formally indicated sense of philosophy takes place.

The endeavors of access are not carried out at any time and any place by anyone; but now and here we live in them, at this place, in this lecture hall. You before me, I before you, we with one another. This situation, this life-connection, is designated with the title University. The access-comportment to the cognitive comportment, philosophizing, is to be determined in its concreteness affecting us, the comporting ones — and what is already evident from this: the comportment toward philosophy itself.

If philosophizing is to be, here and now, then it can determine itself only in the direction of the factical life-connection we indicate with the title University. The first task of access in the pursuit of understanding the definition is to determine this life-situation as a being in its what and how.

Heidegger then takes up two objections. The first: Is philosophy to be identified with university philosophy? Heidegger shows that the formally indicative definition precisely excludes this identification: (1) by the indicative emphasis on the decisive-becoming of the respective sense of being of the cognitive comportment, the possibility is negatively warded off of speaking about philosophy in a general, indeterminate way — as if it could be proclaimed from some elevated, unfixable position, guaranteeing future cultural periods. (2) Positively, the instruction is given to develop the problematic by looking at the fundamental sense of the respective situation. The definition is essentially a task and cannot comfortably hand over something knowable.

The second objection: Can the contingent university situation be normative for philosophy? This leads into a deeper discussion of the historical character of the university, tradition, and the right of the past to normative authority. Heidegger argues that the question of tradition, of whether objective historical past can be a decisive directive for a spiritual situation that has never before existed in this way, must itself be taken back into the problematic of the historical, which is rooted in the facticity of factical life itself. Before one can decide whether the university is suited to ground a basic-experience-situation for philosophy, one must first determine the being-character of the university itself as a factical life-connection.

The objective way of settling the question — through comparative historical assessment — is itself an abfalling detour; it need not be taken. The objection is not accidental but an authentic expression of the abfallen situation. As long as we pursue the objection and try to settle it in its own style, we move abfallingly. The unresolved moments of the objective way are: (1) the sense and right of tradition to normative authority; (2) what the university itself is as factical life-connection, what is genuinely relevant in it, what constitutes it as a life-connection.

As a life-connection, the university is in factical life; the facticity of life, existence, is in itself historical and has as historical a relation (as comportment) to the objective historical prior-world and prior-time. The problem of the relation of objectively historical to historical facticity carries the foregoing question. Since the historical lies in the sense of facticity, it is appropriate to bring the facticity itself — in the form under discussion — more sharply into view.

## **Part III: Factical Life**

With the now-beginning interpretation, given directly, we are led indirectly into the situation of the genuine primal decision. That it is the situation — each must understand this themselves; then they enter into the immediately following explication of the task of philosophy. The following sense-indications of phenomenological basic categories and their categorial connections are given only so far as is necessary for what immediately follows. The genuine interpretation and original winning of these will occupy us — at least for one part (the enactment- and temporalizing-problem — facticity) — in the inseparable connection with the interpretation of the sense of the categorial itself, in the Aristotle-interpretation.

"Factical life": the expression "life" is a phenomenological basic category, indicating a basic phenomenon. If the expression can with demonstrable right be addressed as indicatively announcing a basic phenomenon, then the possibility of the relief of sense-directions — and for a basic phenomenon in an exemplary mode — is given.

Certain sense-moments that crowd forward in modern life-philosophy are noted: not a fashionable philosophy, but one that in its time genuinely wanted to arrive at philosophy — Dilthey, Bergson. The crowding forward is in itself unclarified, which gave the occasion for literary types and philosophers who prefer to enthuse rather than think to easily seize the matter. But one must not see the problem-situation of life-philosophy in the form of today's common waste-products. Heidegger references Nietzsche, Bergson, and Dilthey, and for orientation Scheler's essays.

Against this, Rickert is quoted as saying one should stop seeing philosophizing about life as a mere repetition of life and measuring the value of philosophizing by its vitality. Heidegger responds sharply: "Repetition" (*Wiederholung*) — everything depends on its sense. Philosophy is a fundamental how of life itself, such that it genuinely re-trieves life, takes it back from decline — and this retrieval itself, as radical research, is life. For Rickert, cognition becomes what his concept is — and that is a "pure ghost."

The expression "life" has today a peculiar vagueness; in its use lies the meaning of a more comprehensive, ultimate, and at the same time significant reality: "life." Together with this, the linguistic usage shows an ambiguity: "political life," "a botched life," "he carries life heavily," "he lost his life in the sailing trip." The vagueness and ambiguity coexist with a pronounced dominance of this expression: life-experience, life-intensification, life-originality, complete life, and the like. These modes of occurrence give, on the one hand, the possibility of saying unclearly much and everything about "life" and of moving in a playful dialectic; on the other, since "life" means something ultimate, one is always already where one wants to be and claims to be with the use of the expression.

The verbal meaning of "leben" is explored. (1) As intransitive: being alive, someone lives (intensively), "he lives wildly," "he lives withdrawn," "he lives only half," "one just lives." (2) In the transitive sense: "living the life," "living for one's task"; compositionally: "living through" something, "spending the years"; above all: "experiencing something" (*etwas erleben*). These verbal meanings are nominalizable in the expression "das Leben" (the life/life), which already has a quite specific intransitive-transitive ambiguity that Heidegger wants to retain and hold fast rather than eliminate.

### **Chapter 1: Basic Categories of Life**

As noun and substantive, the expression "life" has an enriched and autonomous meaning, outlined in three respects: (1) Life as the unity of the succession and temporalizing of the two modes of "living" — this unity in its total or respectively limited extension, in its full or partial enactment-manifold, and respective originality or distance from origin. (2) Life grasped as such limited succession-unity: in the meaning of something that carries possibilities within itself in a specific sense, some of which are temporalized in and for it. Life, of which we say what it can all bring, what is incalculable — and it itself is something that carries within itself and is its possibilities, it itself as possibility (a phenomenologically strictly graspable category, having nothing to do with logical or a priori possibility). (3) Life understood in a meaning in which (1) and (2) intertwine: the unity of extension in possibility and as possibility — possibility-fallen, possibility-laden and self-charging, forming possibilities — and this whole taken as reality, indeed reality in its specific impenetrability as power, fate.

In these three substantival meanings, the following structural indications are to be retained: the extension-, succession-unity-, and enactment-manifold-character; the articulation as possibility; and as reality, power in its impenetrability, fate. Through these indicated expressive directions there resonates a peculiarly pervasive sense: life \= existence (*Dasein*), in and through life "being." It is characteristic that this sense mostly only weakly and lostly "strikes" and is usually not properly heard.

#### **A. Life and World**

The intransitive-verbal meaning of "leben" explicates itself, concretely realized, always as "living in" something, "living from" something, "living for" something, "living with" something, "living against" something, "living toward" something, "living from" something. The "something" that indicates its manifold of relations to "leben" in these seemingly only incidentally gathered prepositional expressions is fixed with the term "World" (*Welt*).

This starting point is to be kept in mind: world arises from the phenomenological interpretation of the phenomenon "life," articulated through the intransitive and transitive in, from, for, with, and against a world. It is not a matter of the prior selective positing of a particular reality — say the cosmos of nature — and its designation as world. With the phenomenological category "world" we address what is lived, what life is held by, what it holds to.

Formally — and easily misleadingly — it can be said: life is in itself world-related; "life" and "world" are not two independently existing objects. The relatedness is that of a relation (*Bezug*), that is, enacted, lived, and, as lived, pre-conceptually to be intended for interpretation. The sense-connection of "life" and "world" expresses itself in that characteristic expressive contexts can substitute one word for the other: "standing out in life," "standing in the world," "he lives entirely in his world," "entirely his life." World is the basic category of the content-senseful in the phenomenon life.

When "categories" are spoken of in this context, it means: something that in its sense interprets a phenomenon in a sense-direction in a determinate way, principally — brings the phenomenon as interpretate to understanding. Category is interpreting and is only interpreting — and indeed factical life, appropriated in existential concern (*Bekümmerung*).

When the basic category "world" is further determined in one respect, this determination proceeds categorially, in new categories that grow up and become experienceable in the enactment of interpretation — interpretation that takes place in factical life, for it, and out of it. Categories come to understanding only insofar as factical life itself is forced into interpretation. What this means (that it does not mean simply reflecting on life and the like) will be shown later — above all in the deduction, derivation of phenomenological interpretation from the facticity of life itself. It is something that life is in its most proper self and through which it is; not something imposed on it from caprice. This forcing is not an improper imposition in the violence and arbitrariness of a rootless, alien, ordering systematics, but is demanded by factical life itself, "still in privation" (*Darbung*), and is what genuinely constitutes it.

#### **B. Relational Sense of Life: Caring**

Life, taken in the verbal sense, is to be interpreted according to its relational sense as caring (*Sorgen*): caring for and about something, caringly living from and out of the world. With this character, it is not meant that life always goes about with the face of a pallbearer. In wild revelry, in indifference, in stagnation — however it may be — "living" is characterized as caring. What caring is directed at and holds to is to be determined as significance (*Bedeutsamkeit*). Significance is a categorial determination of world; the objects of a world are lived in the character of significance.

Life in its most broadly grasped relational sense is: caring about "daily bread" — understood in the broadest, formally indicative sense. "Privation" (*Darbung*) is the relational- and enactment-mode fundamental how of the sense of being of life. Where the opposite aspect pushes forward — where life is in possession, absorbed in an objective task, seemingly "there of its own accord" — this fundamental how is all the more tenacious because it eats in and has eaten in hiddenly. The secure objectivity is insecure flight from facticity, and it misrecognizes itself precisely in believing that, on the basis of flight, it enhances objectivity, whereas objectivity actually comes to the most radical appropriation in facticity.

The categorial sense of significance must be taken broadly and not identified with "value." Value is a category that first comes to relief through a specific formation from concrete world-experience and from there is then rightly or wrongly posited as its own sphere of being. What grounds the theory that treats nature as fundamental reality and values as superimposed is a tradition rooted in Greek philosophy, in which both motifs (original experience-explication and categorial theoretical explication) were alive, and only one was lost in the process of flattening. The hints here serve only to ward off a narrowing of the sense of significance and the assumption of its founded character, so as not to impede the understanding of what follows.

The restlessness (*Unruhe*) of factical life is introduced: the how of this restlessness as full phenomenon determines the facticity. Regarding life and restlessness, Heidegger directs attention to Pascal's *Pensées* — valuable for the descriptions, though not for the theory. The restlessness-illumination — the illuminated restlessness; the undecided, unarticulated between of the aspect of factical life: between surrounding-world, co-world, self-world, fore- and after-world — something positive. The everywhere seeping-through of restlessness, its forms and masks. Rest and restlessness — phenomenon and movement (cf. the movement-phenomenon in Aristotle).

##### **b) Directives of Caring**

Caring always proceeds in a determinate or indeterminate, secure or fluttering directive (*Weisung*). Life finds, takes up, grows into, gives itself, or lives in a directive. With the shaping of the directives of caring, there comes the relief of particular caring-worlds. What a factical life caringly absorbs itself in, what it lives from, is always in one of the distinguishable fundamental worlds: surrounding-world (*Umwelt*), co-world (*Mitwelt*), and self-world (*Selbstwelt*).

These worlds must not be set alongside one another as three districts, three pre-given, absolutely delimited reality-domains. Their distinction takes its sense from the shaping (possibility- and temporalizing-character) of the caring-mode. The self-world must not be identified with the "I." The "I" — a category of complicated formation — need not encounter me as such in my caring about my self-world. As self-world, the "me" for which I am in care is experienced in specific significances that are absorbed in the full life-world, in which the co-world and surrounding-world are always there with the self-world.

Self-worldly experience has nothing to do with psychological and certainly not with theoretical-psychological reflection, inner perception of psychic experiences, processes, and acts. Self-world is the world in which I encounter myself worldly — in which I am somehow there with, taken along, in which something "happens" to me, in which "I" work. The co-world encounters partly in my self-world, insofar as I live with other people, am caringly related to them, find myself in their caring-world. The surrounding-world has no ordered boundary; the "around" determines itself from the enactment- and relational-sense of caring.

What is gained: the basic characterization of the relational-sense of "life" as caring; the content-basic-character of life as "world," categorially determined as significance; at the same time, regarding the possible shaping of caring or the relief of the relevant world, three characteristic possible directives that life can "have." Life receives a fundamental directive and grows into one; it can come to an explicit taking of direction, but need not. The directives fall to, suggest themselves (gliding in\!).

#### **C. The Categories in the Relational Sense of Life**

The relational sense itself is now to be interpreted still more sharply. The following categories contain in their terminological expression the indication of movement.

##### **a) Inclination**

In caring about significances, in life's directional taking toward its world, in the caring living from and out of the world, lies the sense-character of "inclination" (*Neigung*). This categorial sense gives life a peculiar weight, direction of weight, a pull toward. The weight comes to life not from outside but is at it and with it. The world as encountered always already impinges upon this inclining caring and is itself what takes the inclination-pull, in factical temporalizing as it were, from life itself on commission. The inclination-characterized relational sense temporalizes a how of enactment: the being-inclined (*Geneigtheit*). Caring enacts itself in the enactment-sense of being-inclined.

This being-inclined presses life into its world, holds it fast there, temporalizes a consolidation of life's directional taking. Life finds itself genuinely where it holds fast its own being-inclined; and life takes from there the "representation" it has formed in itself about itself (i.e., the world). In its being-inclined toward its world, life "has itself" and experiences itself always only in the form of its "world." The caring-relations, life in the world, scatter and the waking being-inclined holds life in its dispersions (*Zerstreuungen*). The being-inclined precisely does not want to miss anything of the dispersion and thereby intensifies it. The arising and suppressed "claims" of life are met by and from its world; life forms what we designate as self-sufficiency (*Selbstgenügsamkeit*) — a how of caring in the scattering, world-drawn life.

##### **b) Distance**

A further structure in the relational sense of life — co-original with inclination — is now to be brought to relief. Equally originally enacted with inclination, this character is one that inclination precisely covers over, displaces, and drags into dispersion. This relational-sense category is designated as distance (*Abstand*) — or more precisely, distance-elimination (*Abstandstilgung*).

Distance, which co-enables inclination, is precisely swept along by it. In the caring relatedness of life to its world, life has its world before it. This "before" — a categorial articulation of the relation-to-something — is precisely suppressed in the caring absorption in significances. "Caring absorption in" means precisely: elimination of the "before." In the being-inclined and in dispersion, life does not hold the distance; it oversees itself. In the dispersing displacement of the "before," the distance as such is not explicitly there.

The distance is not eliminated; it is in the being of the caring relation itself; but it is life-wise taken along into the dispersion. It surfaces in what the being-inclined lives in — in the world, in its "sense" as the how of significances. The distance-character is there insofar as life in its caring over-measures itself in the significances, expands these, is out for a reckoning and ranking within the significant world: rank, success, position in life (world), overtaking, advantage, reckoning, busyness, noise, display. These are ways in which life lets itself be taken along by the worldly distances that come toward it. Life takes the measures as wide and important as possible and thereby eases for itself the how of its dispersion. The life is in the inclination-wise dispersion of its distance-relational-sense hyperbolical (*hyperbolisch*). It seeks differences and distances in what it lives in, in the significances.

##### **c) Barricading**

The third character of the relational-sense caring — barricading (*Abriegelung*) — is even less in the open than the distance. With the inclination in its distance-diminishing dispersion, what further goes into loss? What I caringly live in, what I caringly comport myself toward, is something that can explicitly be "before" me — "before" phenomenologically grasped, not spatially. In caring, this "before" is liftable and explicitly appropriable. But insofar as caring in the fundamental how of its enactment — the being-inclined — factically lives and consolidates itself, the appropriation of the "before" fails to occur.

The appropriation of the "before" fails all the more as the distance itself and the distance-caring settle into the world and are not missed there. In the caring barricading against itself, the life within the increasing caring about its world, factically temporalizes a not-caring (factically) about itself: unconcernedness — itself a cared-for caring. In the caring barricading against itself, factical life constantly forms new possibilities of significance in which it can bustle about and so secure its "significance."

This manifold of possibilities is always an intensification of the possibilities of ever-new self-misgrasping. Factical life temporalizes in itself the distance-related endlessness of failabilities, and since these are all in the character of significances — worldly-significant objects in which life lives — this endlessness becomes what is formalistically characterized as the infinity and inexhaustible richness, the never-to-be-mastered, the always-more-living and more-than-living. This infinity is the mask that factical life factically puts on and holds before itself. With this infinity, life blinds itself, puts out its own eyes. In the barricading, life itself is left out; it comes up short. The barricading has the specific enactment- and temporalizing-character of the elliptical (*das Elliptische*). Factical life paves its own track, inclining, displacing distance, barricading itself, in the direction of the easy (*das Leichte*).

##### **d) The "Easy" (Aristotle)**

Heidegger cites Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* B 5, 1106b28ff.: failing is manifold (for the bad belongs to the unlimited, as the Pythagoreans judge, but the good to the limited), while acting rightly is of one kind. Therefore the one is easy, the other difficult. Easy is it to miss the target, difficult to hit it. Therefore excess and deficiency belong to vice, the mean to virtue.

Factical life seeks easing in each case; inclination follows the pull, of its own accord, without doing. In the being-inclined lies what corresponds to the pull, what springs toward it, "without further ado." The "further" would be what does not lie in the field of being-inclined. Even worldly difficulties are easings. With convenience, life simultaneously seeks the security that nothing can remain closed to it.

Life is caring, in the inclination of making-it-easy-for-itself, the flight. Thereby the directional taking on the failable as such is temporalized: failability, decline, making-it-easy-for-oneself, self-delusion, enthusiasm, exuberance. The life determined through inclination is more sharply to be grasped as determined through guilt (*Schuld*) and haziness (*Diesigkeit*). Life seeks to secure itself in looking away from itself. This sight is the primary one, giving the fundamental image of how life is seen by itself. It forms in itself its own self-regarding temptation, which in falling transforms into carelessness (*securitas*, *Sorglosigkeit*).

#### **D. Retrospect and Prospect**

At the start of the introductory considerations, an objective task presented itself: interpretation of Aristotle. Though pushed into the background, it is there insofar as it matters and the present preliminary considerations serve it dienstfully. It is not the case that the "introductory" is the actual main thing and the Aristotle-interpretation a random application; nor that the introduction treats the real systematic problems while the other is a historical illustration; nor that the introduction is what directly concerns us while the historical consideration is a matter of scholarly interest.

The understanding appropriation of principled cognition should lead precisely and principally out of every situation that would make possible a separation of "history" and "systematics." Principled cognition is not a newly invented objective ideal of a philosophy; in its own sense it indicates what claims to become principally decisive, in an inevitability and rigor of binding before which the supposedly timeless, eternally valid ideality of values and the like flutter away as phantoms.

In the appropriation of principled cognition, a false ideal of exactness fades before the urgency and rigor of an unrelenting questionability. Philosophy must consider giving up the swindle of aestheticizing self-befuddlement. The being of the (historically) historical and the sense of the historical first arise and are in the enactment of principled cognition. In philosophizing there is no history of philosophy, and in the historical of factical (philosophizing) life there is no timeless in-itself problematic and systematics of philosophical questions. Philosophizing as principled cognition is nothing other than the radical enactment of the historical of the facticity of life, so that in it and for it history and systematics are equally foreign.

The task-circle of the Aristotle-interpretation is no other than that of the introduction. In content, three problem-groups have already come to expression: principled cognition that in its enactment concerns its own facticity. Schematically: (1) the problem of the principle and the principled (*ἀρχή — αἴτιον*); (2) the problem of apprehending determining and conceptual articulating (*λόγος*); (3) the problem of beings and the sense of being (*ὄν — οὐσία — κίνησις — φύσις*).

The further course of the consideration: the categorial explication of facticity enables the sense-determination of what we indicated as situation. We will encounter the question: what life lives in within our life-connection, what its world consists of, what it means to live in sciences, what sense of facticity lies therein, what its appropriation entails.

#### **E. The Movement Categories: Relucence and Praestruktion**

Each of the three relational-sense categories (inclination, distance-elimination, barricading) expresses something movement-like (*bewegungshaft*) in itself. It now matters to make this explicit through further interpretation, to bring the movement itself more originally into view. This will then enable interpreting this movement as such toward a fundamental sense, which in turn gives the specific movement-categories (relucence and praestruktion) their sense and governs their own movement-structures, courses, and stages.

The task is to press interpretively forward to a movement that constitutes a genuine movedness (*Bewegtheit*) of life — in which and through which it is — from which life is determinable in its sense of being. This wins the fundamental sense from which all existentials take their own and relational sense interpretively.

Regarding the determination of the two movement-categories — fixed terminologically as relucence (*Reluzenz*) and praestruktion (*Praestruktion*): relucence designates the movement of life in the encounter-direction back upon itself. What factical life cares into its world encounters it from there as care. Life is through its world and with it relucent upon itself as caring life. From the how of the encountering offering, caring life supplies its need for caring-directives; it builds forward from this world and for it; it secures itself with a pre-having (*Vorhabe*) and cares in explicit or implicit regard to it. Caringly, it is always building forward — in its relucence it is at the same time praestruktive.

The securing-tendency of this relucence-arising praestruktion can be so far displaced that cultural creating and life, working comportment toward values, is interpreted from self-sufficient life itself as closed, autonomous, positive. This means: the securing-tendency intensified relucently into the mode of positive creative expression can go into loss as such (wherein a fundamental peculiarity of the movedness of life expresses itself: ruinance, hardening), and with it the possibility of life-like encountering of what the securing-tendency is a tendency against — an insecurity announcing itself in factical life.

The peculiar inseparability of the three fundamental categories of the relational-sense caring, and the possibility of their mutually interpreting and determinately-sensefully moving interconnection, becomes visible precisely in the fact that, like inclination, so also distance-elimination and barricading in their movement-character, each for themselves and among themselves, are relucently and praestruktively characterized. One must go further and see that this movedness itself, precisely in and through the named categorial explicities, is in itself and to itself relucent and praestruktive — indeed, that it moves itself in the how of relucence and praestruktion.

#### **F. Connections**

The factical sense of the movedness becomes clearer through the interpretive appropriation of the movement-like relations of the relucent-praestruktive movedness expressed in each relational-sense category to the others. In the last-characterized praestruktive movedness of the barricading — in the elliptical — it becomes manifest how this, and thus the barricading, in various ways comes to meet (*entgegenkommt*) the inclination. The coming-to-meet as stimulation, encouragement, keeping-in-motion, calming justification, stimulation of inclination, is itself a movedness-character expressing the relation of the two relational-sense characters.

The inclination so affected and stimulated by the barricading is itself in turn relucent upon the distance-elimination. Inclination opens up and makes available from its world significances as possibilities for the formation of worldly distances. The hyperbolical of the distance-elimination, which itself praestruktively follows the relucent dispersion-possibilities temporalized by the barricading "with the help" of the inclination, is thereby itself relucent upon the barricading, offering the occasion for looking-away.

These peculiar relations show the movedness of caring itself — how it enacts itself, its enactment-sense. Here it becomes visible what it is with sense-connection, connection of relational-sense and enactment-sense. The factical to-one-another is an expression for the fact that the relucence as how of the movedness is itself praestruktured by this movedness and that this movedness is such that it forms out and temporalizes the praestruktion movement-wise as relucent.

In the expression "formation" (*Bildung*) this peculiar connection can be categorially indicated: grasped as temporalizing according to an image, something pre-given, pre-held, relucent; and as forming out in the holding to the image — *struere*. The first sense-moment of formation — temporalizing according to an image — becomes more sharply graspable in connection with the phenomena designated under "illumination" (*Erhellung*). Negatively this indicates that cognition, knowledge, and their various formations — sciences, philosophy — cannot be interpreted in isolation.

In the movement-connection of relucence and praestruktion, the movedness of life expresses itself such that factical life in every, if varying, way cares to live-itself-firm (*sich festzuleben*) in its world. Each movement in itself and toward the other is directed at factically living this world-absorbed self-securing. The movedness has the character of a particular autonomy — a self-movedness (*Eigenbewegtheit*) that is its own precisely in that life lives out of itself. The movedness is one that, as movement, assists itself to itself; it is the movedness of factical life that life itself makes — yet such that factical life, as living in the world, does not actually "itself" make the movement, but the world, as the wherein and whereupon and wherefor of living, lives.

### **Chapter 2: Ruinance**

This self-forming and thereby, assisting itself to itself, self-intensifying movedness of factical life (which as such is made by its world) is designated as "fall" (*Sturz*): a movement that forms itself and yet not itself, but the emptiness into which it moves; its emptiness is its movement-possibility. With this a fundamental sense of the movedness of factical life is won, fixed terminologically as ruinance (*Ruinanz* — from *ruina*, fall).

In a formally indicative definition, ruinance is determined: the movedness of factical life that factical life enacts in itself as itself for itself out of itself and in all this against itself — that is, "is." The sense of being of this "is" is not yet determined. Heidegger inserts a bracketed reflection on ruinance and intentionality: praestruktion and relucence are the expression of intentionality — the formal primal structure of facticity (of the sense of being of life). What has always concerned him: Is intentionality fallen from heaven? If it is something ultimate, in what ultimacy is it to be accepted? Not secured in a specific theoretical discoverability and experienceability. That I must intentionally live and "be" — this is to be "explained," or rather, the questionability is to be grasped as which\! Correspondingly for all categorial structures of facticity — intentionality is their formal fundamental structure. This is the fundamental sense of phenomenology. Intellectual-historically destructive and leading: how far intentionality has been seen, in what direction and with what means it has been reinterpreted, what has hindered its appropriation. How and how far it became graspable through Husserl via consciousness. Which motifs were effective, which facticity-sense (existential).

The ruinant "against" indicates a "presupposition" of the interpretation to which the interpretation itself must trace itself back. The interpretation would be none if it had no presupposition; at best, it would be an ordering collection of describabilities of a randomly seized psychological attitude. As philosophical interpretation, its presupposition must be a radical one, and the confrontation with it correspondingly lengthy. It is the peculiarity of phenomenological interpretation that in its own enactment it constantly traces itself back; this is nothing other than an expression of a fundamental movedness of facticity and thus the demonstration that the interpretation itself is factical and thereby appropriate to its object.

The phenomenological interpretation as existential displays essentially a "counter-movement" (*Gegenbewegung*). For it is not immediately evident that the caring absorption is a movement of life "against" itself; insofar as this is said, it is supposed that life is "still" something other, which other is indeed there in ruinance, occurs, but in the mode of being displaced.

### **A. Returning and Repetition of the Interpretation**

On the now-reached level of the interpretation, it is possible to pursue the categorial (expressive) character through the previous explications of caring. First, how ruinance categorially determines the movedness-character of relucence and praestruktion both each for themselves and in their mutual relation.

This backward-pursuing of the "effective force" of an interpretation is not a merely subsequent and optional validation, but something belonging to the interpretation's own factical enactment-sense. The interpretation is only fully appropriated when its enactment understands that it itself, in the sense of its own advance, reaches back and interpretively "in-forms" what has been "set aside" into the respectively achieved interpretation-level.

Insofar as everything in factical life is illuminated, stands in some inexpressible discourse, "is" in unrelieved factically ruinant interpretation — therein lies the possibility and factical necessity (or: demonstration of genuineness) of the formal indication as the approach-method of the existential categorial interpretation.

Now ruinance itself is to be more sharply articulated in its own sense, so that the peculiar fall-character and this then as categorial moment of a sense of being (facticity) becomes comprehensible. From the previous explication, it is already clear that caring is not intended as a happening, a playing-itself-out whose existence would be graspable in a simple, horizon-unspecified constituting related to a leveled experienced reality. Caring is not a happeningal struggle for existence, grasped as running and "happening" in so-called objective life-units. With this apprehension-pre-conception one would never arrive at a sense-determination like "caring" and its categorial determinacies.

##### **a) Intensification of Care: Concern (*Besorgnis*)**

Relucence is in caring itself affected by praestruktion in terms of movedness; that is, caring is such that in its enactment it has it in for itself. On "itself" — not necessarily on "itself" (reflexively). With the use of "it" (*es*) it is indicated that where caring takes itself into care, this care taken into caring encounters worldly. The caring is "concerned" (*be-sorgt*), itself overtaken by caring, overloaded. This caring as being-concerned is determined as concern (*Besorgnis*).

In concern, the full movedness of caring throws itself as it were upon itself — the movedness of itself is itself moved. There comes into the caring-enactment what can be formally objectively determined as "intensification" of the caring-movedness. In concern, where the movednesses expressed in the categories of caring's relational-sense are dragged by caring itself into the fall, and inclination, distance-elimination, barricading each intensify in their way, the illumination — the ambiguity — is itself intensified. Factical ruinant life covers itself up in concern\!

The involvement with the caring-world is in concern apparently a seized task — what does not let one rest day and night, what life has apparently committed itself to — and yet it is (and this "at times still" even for concern itself) a mere letting-oneself-be-pulled, being-taken-along, only so that the illumination is ruinantly abandoned and handed over to ruinance itself.

##### **b) Kairological Characters**

Repeatedly, reference has been made to the phenomenon: life occurs (*vorkommt*) in the caring-enactment, it encounters — mostly worldly, yet so that in this worldliness its genuine (being- and object-sense; what and that it is) shines through. This occurring is not meant as objective, happeningal appearing but as a how of the caring-enactment itself. Each mode of occurring has its determinate (factical) kairological character (*kairós* — time): its determinate relation to time, to its time, which lies in the sense of the enactment-connection of facticity.

The question is: how, in kairological respect, in concern, life as such announces itself and is allowed to announce itself. If factical life is in full concern, something like a tormenting, gnawing, boring can encounter. One is on the wrong track if one simply characterizes these (formal) characters as "feelings." "Feeling" is a psychological category whose categorial structure is confused. These characters of the factical life-movedness are left terminologically undetermined — a being-mode and itself existentially factical expression, the "being-to-me" (*Mir-Sein*). They have a particular announcement-sense (*Meldungssinn*), one that does not mediate or intend a taking-cognizance. They are phenomena of their own (factical) appearing in facticity with the tendency to determine factical life, in which they encounter, enactment-wise and in its sense of being, from their specific relational-sense.

Insofar as this (factical) appearing of announcing characters stands in an enactment-connection with the occurring of environmental life, and this proves itself as the historical, the historical of the appearing is co-constitutive for every encounter. How for the first time something tormenting dared forward, how for the first time something fell on one's soul tormentingly, how first the tormenting withdrew of its own accord, how in historical succession it made new demands, how first factical life defended itself, how the appearing settled into the historical expectation-horizon, how it occurred "more seldom," "at times still," "temporarily," how finally factical life "no longer has time" for it.

"Time" here is understood neither as ordering-frame, ordering-dimension, nor as (specifically formal) character of historical event-connections, but as a specific how of the movedness — not merely enabling, permitting, but co-constituting it, itself factically moving as an autonomous character. The kairological modes in which, in concern, life announces itself are for factical life, in its more or less explicit expectation-horizon, not a "less" but a "more": "more seldom and only at times still" are expressions of growing worldly security, of not-caring about such things. Precisely through their unmeltability and their historically un-changing, ever-growing imperceptibility, these kairological characters express an intensification of ruinance.

Factical, ruinant life "has no time" because its fundamental movedness — ruinance itself — takes away the "time," a removable time that factically ruinant life takes away from itself in itself for itself. Ruinance takes away the time — that is, it seeks to eliminate the historical from facticity. The ruinance of factical life has this enactment-sense of time-elimination (*Zeittilgung*). In this peculiar way, the historical is still in life; it is in all ruinance still factically there (the historical as constitutive of facticity).

In concern, where factical caring takes itself into care, ruinant life gets entangled in itself. The care lays itself, enactment-wise, more and more upon life and finally remains lying on it — factical life wants to carry itself — in its factically ruinant way — and in the end, explicitly or not, goes mad or becomes foolish over it.

### **B. Four Formally Indicative Characters of Ruinance**

In the interpretive view of concern, taken in the full movedness, four formally indicative characters can be fixed with respect to ruinance: (1) the seductive (*das Verführerische*, Tentative), (2) the calming (*das Beruhigende*, Quietive), (3) the alienating (*das Entfremdende*, Alienative), (4) the annihilating (*das Vernichtende*, Negative — active, transitive).

##### **a) Prohibitive Function of the Formal Indication**

These characters are formally indicative — they receive their concrete factical categorial determinacy from the respective experience- and interpretation-direction. Nothing is "said" about concrete movednesses of factical life; they give only the direction of the regard. These characters of ruinance are not properties of movedness that could additionally, alongside, be established for a ruinance in itself. The formal indication has in itself, alongside the pointing character, a prohibitive (holding-back, preventing) character. It wards off every drifting into autonomous, dogmatic fixations of categorial sense as in-itself-determinacies of an object whose sense of being is undiscussed.

The hint at the preventing character is motivated here by the fact that precisely the named characters of ruinance could easily be taken as fixed fundamental properties of a being and, as fundamental determinations of the existence of life, could inaugurate an ontological metaphysics of life — perhaps in the style of Bergson or Scheler. That would be a comfortable misunderstanding and a vain misuse, because such an isolated dogmatic stamping of "life" unbinds itself from the interpretation-connection and its presuppositions.

##### **b) The Whither of Ruinance: The Nothing**

A first preview of ruinance can be won by considering it as movedness according to its own directional sense. This whither of ruinance is to be followed in detail. But does this not view the movedness of factical life after the image of a spatial movement? The question is not unimportant; but whether the use of such determinations speaks principally against the interpretation can remain undiscussed here, insofar as the linguistic expressions are understood not according to a next-at-hand meaning but from the phenomenon-connection.

Regarding the expression "direction" (*Richtung*): the question is whether the direction-character as such must primarily and constitutively-necessarily concern spatial object-connections, or whether this meaning is derived and in the spatial "direction-concept" an object-ordering object-determinacy lies that is motivated and "guided" from an original direction-experience. "Direction" is not an ordering concept and does not primarily and necessarily mean spatiality.

Where does "the falling" finally arrive, where does it find its impact, its resistance? The fall as the movedness of factical life does not arrive somewhere, not in a reception-area and container foreign to its being-mode. No impact-possibility is found here from something of a different object- and being-character. This "merely" means: the whither of the fall is not something foreign to it; it is itself of the character of factical life — namely, "the nothing of factical life."

Not every nothing is identical with every nothing. Formally, the interpretation-line for each categorial determination of the nothing can be indicated: as "nothing" — not — no. The formal nothing, precisely as formal, is itself "something" — which is why the discourse within concrete interpretation, "This nothing is a something," has its genuine, object-motivated sense and cannot be mastered by superficial argumentation.

The meanings of "nothing" can be enumeratively indicated: the nothing of historical eventlessness, the nothing of successlessness, the nothing of prospectlessness, the nothing of hopelessness — all in their factical historical-historical situation-connections. Most "nearly" and easily interpretively accessible is the nothing of being-at-hand and being-available. But this nearest-at-hand sense is not thereby already proven as the original one.

Originally, the "nothing" of factical life here in question has the least sense-connection with the "empty." This nothing is a possibility that precisely does not give standing, accommodation, does not catch the fall and end it, but is something that factically co-temporalizes the fall. The fall is in a peculiar way formed from its whither, which for its part temporalizes itself in concern. This fall-character is designated as "annihilation" (*Vernichtung*). The formal definition: the nothing of factical life is its own, by it and for it temporalized, life-wise environmental non-occurring in the ruinant existence of itself (facticity).

The more factical life lives in its ruinant caring-mode, the more pressingly and simultaneously more inexpressly it thereby cares for the non-occurring of itself for itself. This "non-occurring" is not an ordering-mode absence at a place that is empty and objectively ascertainable in its non-existence, but this "non-occurring" is a movement-expression of the how of the "still-being-there" of the environmental life. It is factically "still" there so; the "still" is again a characteristic determination of the temporality at work here.

##### **c) Objecthood**

The interpretive pursuit of the directional character of ruinance as a movedness-moment of itself leads into a sense-connection from which the objecthood (*Gegenständlichkeit*) and being-sense of world in and for factical life becomes categorially graspable. Methodically, the position is won to determine something about the "immediacy" of the givenness of the lived world: (1) that it is not a beginning and not a fundamental measure but precisely a temporalizing in factically ruinant life; (2) but that the immediacy is — formally speaking — mediated, yet this mediation is not one of "thinking" versus "representation."

The unhindered, explosive rushing at and into the world in the caring mode — the plunging-into-things, seizing, hands-on — makes the world experienceable as the nearest-given, first-in-line. But these non-theoretical modes of the relational-sense caring are factically only in the being-sense of the movedness of ruinance; that is, what is given in the mode of such immediacy is not thereby grasped in a manner genuinely demanded by itself.

For environmental life this means: for its self-interpreting apprehension-tendency, the immediacy of the being-over-against of the cared-for world cannot undiscussedly be taken as the fundamental measure of self-givenness. On the other hand, principally: because of the occurring of environmental life in its own facticity, the access-possibility can be won from the named movednesses of factical life. As blind as the holding-fast to said immediacy is, so over-lit and therefore nothing-giving is the schematic counter-attempt to bring life — whose object- and being-sense is undiscussed — to a genuine givenness by way of devised dialectical mediations.

##### **d) Questionability**

The factically interpreting dialogue of the factical life-enactment is the breach that lies in the roundness of immediate life itself. The dialogue can be confrontation-fleeing, in the enactment-mode of superior unconcernedness about enjoyment-hindering questioning- and understanding-tendencies. But from immediate life itself, the question can — need not — be asked about the certainty of the insular untouchability of its immediacy and its possible securing.

It is principally questionable, insofar as factical environmental life as such has and claims an illumination-tendency at all. With this questionability, the philosophical interpretation of facticity makes earnest — not so that it supposed it would find an absolute eternal decision, but so that it merely concretely and in concretely available directions temporalizes and keeps the questionability, and therewith holds the enactment of the access to factical life in vitality.

The validity claimed by the philosophical interpretation is then already genuinely and decisively satisfied — from the side of the understanding reception itself and for it — when such factical immediate life becomes questionable at itself. A counter-ruinant movedness is that of the philosophical interpretation-enactment, enacting itself in the appropriated access-mode of questionability. Factical life comes precisely in questioning to its genuinely developable self-givenness. The holding in genuine questionability does not lie in mechanically reacting to an empty maxim of always-only-questioning, but in questioning from factically situation-illuminated, directional motifs and likewise living researchingly in answering, so that the answer-forming has constant reference to the question — that is, that the question itself is vitally there.

Heidegger then passes over the difficult interpretation of the tentative, quietive, and alienative characters, noting only that the tentative is not religious; it does not require a religious basic experience for its experienceability, even though it was first made visible through Christianity. The tentative is not an in-itself but a how in the concretion.

The next interpretive task is indicated: What is the sense of being of ruinance? What asserts itself in ruinance and as what does it factically assert itself? In ruinance, as a fundamental movedness of caring, it asserts itself that in factical life something is constantly somehow lacking — and indeed so that what it is that is actually lacking is also co-lacking. What asserts itself in ruinance is a condition (*Zuständlichkeit*) designated as "privation" (*Darbung*) — the factically privation-laden life.

This condition questioned as to its sense of being places the phenomenological (unprejudiced) interpretation on a decisive test. Is the object- and being-sense of factical life, whose genuine access-comportment lives in the enactment of questionability — is this sense of being one of seamless purity and univocity? Or is it not categorially non-univocal — principally refusing a thoroughgoing pure functionality of determination-relations — thus broken in its sense?

## **Appendix I: Presupposition**

Given the peculiarity of the philosophical interpretation, methodological reflection cannot be avoided — and indeed because it is not a theory about interpretation, something appended, but an essential piece of its own enactment: it is way in the movedness.

Regarding "presupposition" as a preliminary remark: in order to ward off multiple particularly disturbing misunderstandings, the presupposition-, objectivity-, and validity-question of the interpretation carried out here must be addressed, even though this discussion at this point in the connection — as still too early — should actually be omitted.

The sense of "presupposition" is determined from the leading positing-mode of philosophizing. For philosophizing, positing means formally: object-apprehending. The "before" and the "positing" are considered under three aspects: (1) the "before" as logically in the sense of the grounding ground within a theoretical grounding-connection — then positing is theoretical approaching; (2) the "before" as temporally in the sense of "previously," "first," at the same time as in advance — in the series of positings connected in a non-logical succession, an enactment- and temporalizing-succession of factical life; (3) philosophical presupposition, to be determined from the foregoing.

It belongs to the sense of theoretical presuppositions — those on which theoretical attitude stands and from which it lives — that they are precisely not apprehensible by this attitude. The scientific research-enactment grows in its historical situations into autonomous constitutions. Every attempt to re-appropriate presuppositions is rejected as unfruitful philosophizing — and rightly so, insofar as the suggestion is made that science would thereby reach a new standing. But one overlooks that the appropriation of presupposition must be carried out in the manner of a revision of basic experiences.

Philosophizing is counter-ruinant existentially — it brings the presupposition factically, again and again more radically, to appropriation, and indeed so that it is seen as non-eliminable (privation). This seeing is not a taking-cognizance but in it the environmental life is grasped as counter-ruinant. The presupposition is enclosed in the existence-sense of ruinance, in the existing historical of facticity.

Heidegger then turns to the conditionedness of the interpretation. The point is to expressly indicate the conditionedness, to prevent the interpretations from being taken dogmatically. Characterizations like "relativism" and "skepticism," as well as the opposing "absolutisms," are those that concern cognition, cognition-possibility, cognition-validity — characterizations that carry within them a prior consideration of cognition and a specific ideal-setting of knowledge and truth. These arise from a specific fundamental pre-conception of cognition: cognition as objective ordering-cognition. The principle of contradiction arises from a quite specific logic and ontology.

Has philosophy ever established, and can it establish, that it can and should arrive at absolute, valid truth in the field of its cognition? Only if one fabricates an object of philosophizing or simply does not see the genuine, fundamental one. As long as philosophy cannot give this certification — as long as one does not childishly close one's eyes to the transformation of precisely the strongest philosophical positions — there is absolutely no right to set up absolute truth-cognition as a standard for philosophy. The famous joke with the principle of contradiction (that whoever denies absolute truth contradicts himself) settles nothing about philosophical cognition-possibility, since a formal-logical argumentation is at work whose origin from a specific ordering-logic is demonstrable.

One can design an absolute system of morality, of in-themselves-valid ethical values and value-relations — and can thereby be blind to objects and relations that occasionally occur in living morality. That one moves hourly and daily in lies, half-measures, and worse — that is now just the way it is. Why engage oneself principally with this being called the human? But that is precisely the task: before all comfortable reckoning of validity and objectivity for humanity stands the reflection on what one actually intends and can intend, on the enactment-availabilities for it.

Not only the invocation of these specific cognition-characterizations, but principally every reflection on the validity-character of philosophical cognition with the pretension of settling something philosophically, points philosophical cognition out of its own enactment-tendency. What matters is not whether propositions are universally provable, whether the agreement of as many as possible is compellable, but whether the intended bindingness of the interpretation comes alive.

Heidegger then outlines a way to the object of philosophy through three alternatives: (I) either upon human products (culture) and their curious investigation, or upon the human being in the how of his being — as the origin of his products; (II) if upon the human being, either worldly — as object of curiosity, psychological analysis, and historical narration — or the human insofar as grasped and questioned on his "what he is and how he is"; (III) if the latter, either in the mode of reportingly enumerating what the human could be — remaining unclear about its own presuppositions — or in the tendency to factically appropriate the spiritual situation, attentive to the sense of being of factical life.

The question after the sense of being, and indeed of this peculiar objecthood, is a philosophically principled one. Not about an alternative decision whether cultural creating should be preferred to personal interest, but about first categorially bringing out the genuine object- and being-sense of life in and for which it is to come to a possible factical being in the various directions of possible life-worlds.

The question can be formally indicatively grasped as the question after the sense of "I am" (*ich bin*). But in the tendency of this questioning, the "am" (*bin*) is decisive and not the "I." The question of the "sum" in "cogito-sum" — Descartes already went wrong: he did not stay with it and already had the pre-conception of being-sense in the mode of mere ascertainment of the indubitable. That Descartes could turn into an epistemological questioning is only the expression that the "sum," its being and its categorial structure, was in no way problematic for him.

The sense of being of factical life temporalizes itself in each case — in the concretely so-questioning as enacted — in its facticity, genuine situation, and temporality. This means the temporality of factical life must be primarily involved when it comes to winning a genuine sense of (formal) constancy, objectual binding-possibility and binding-mode. There encounters an objecthood of wholly peculiar and unexceedable, principally incomparable resistancy (*Widerständigkeit*). Against this objecthood and its philosophically interpretive mode of apprehension, every poorly reflective safeguarding of cognition-results and the reach of their validity means a stepping-out of the interpretation's own enactment-sense — something philosophically irrelevant and directly a corruption.

## **Appendix II: Loose Pages**

### **Page 1: Motto**

Heidegger opens with three epigraphs signaling the orientation of the entire lecture. From Kierkegaard's *Training in Christianity*: modern philosophy, both ethically and Christianly, is based on a frivolity — instead of warning against despair and indignation, it invited people to pride themselves on doubting; the other philosophy hovers in the indeterminacy of the metaphysical, creating the appearance that people could speculate themselves out of their own skin and into pure semblance. From Kierkegaard's *Either/Or*: "What is hard for philosophy and the philosopher is stopping" — stopping at the genuine beginning\! From Luther: from the very womb of the mother we begin to die. And: look to it that you do not drink wine when you are still a suckling — every teaching has its measure, time, and age.

### **Page 2: Structure of the Introduction**

The introduction is properly a concerned, enactment-like interim-contemplation (*Zwischenbetrachtung*) for the Aristotle-interpretation. Later interpretations (New Testament, Patristics, Augustine, Luther) require in each case newly staged interim-contemplations — existential logic. The structure: formally indicatively pursuing the movedness of facticity, breaking open its structures at each stage, tending toward a decisive fundamental situation. With this the movedness-sense of philosophizing, phenomenological interpretation, is worked out. So that each stage interprets the other — concrete taking-in into the interpretation-enactment. Then formally indicative, methodically interpretive counter-movement back to the factical starting point, so that now the methodological — the explicitly enactment-like — comes to genuine appropriation. So the historical springs forth, brought to occurrence in its genuine autonomy.

### **Page 3: Connection**

Movedness — categories — relational-sense; one fundamental sense: ruinance. Fall-character. (1) Caring itself taken into care: concern. (2) Kairological character of factical concern. (3) The four formal characters of ruinance. Fall-character as expression of being, how of being: privation. The "being-to-me," the "being-to-it" of factical life as principally co-categorial for this sense of being.

### **Page 4: Caring — Waiting**

Caring — waiting; the "not." Facticity. At the explicit resumption of the departure of the interpretive relief of the relational-sense caring, the enactment-sense is to be explicitly entered: how of the enactment in a waiting-for-something; itself in caring as relation the waiting-for; "constantly" in a "waiting for." Expectation is a variously factically possible grasping and "expressing" of waiting. Ruinance as not-wanting-to-wait and not-being-able-to, precipitating-itself; but precisely therein waiting and waiting as privation. Waiting gives the historical fundamental sense of facticity.

### **Page 5: Illumination and Caring**

Fundamental how of caring illumination: "deliberating" (*Überlegen*). Structure of the full sense of deliberating.

### **Page 6: What It Comes Down To**

(1) Not about gaining new concepts in the old mode of understanding, but through the categorial problematic making attentive to an originally other enactment of understanding and temporalizing. No fantastic representation of new categories that comfortably escort into a new realm. Existentially it becomes more difficult: because ruinance is seen. (2) Not so arbitrarily and as direction and for the whole of culture, but concretely in the situation.

### **Page 7: The Genuine Beginning**

In philosophy no compromise. One thing is certain: not at the end; therefore begin, genuinely begin, approach the beginning — when it itself must first be sought, when access to it has gone into loss. Radical effort toward access-possibility. Genuine beginning has its "time." Beginning for another time is senseless.

### **Page 8: Way of the Interpretation**

Interpretation of facticity; winning the pre-having: not life, not world; but being, existence (*Dasein*). Destructively work out. The winning of the pre-having in the mode of interpretation: situation — the situation-drawing in advance in and as facticity-determined situation — articulate what is lacking with respect to facticity, what covers it. University: develop the situation from the position into the situation: position for the and as how of possible grasping. What kind? "Philosophical" life, that is, existing existence. Being-forming: research, full factical being-character of the same. No grand reform plans before one has really "been there" — achievement.

### **Page 9: Introduction to Phenomenological Research**

Not a particular discipline of philosophy, not a particular self-contained philosophy, but fundamentally seeing research otherwise from facticity. The sense of being of the sciences — phenomenological hermeneutics. Not applying philosophy, taking philosophical propositions to cognizance, but such research as radical, that its understanding for the university prepares itself in the concretely chosen task.

The situation: sentimentality — whineriness as reaction against science. Interpreted and conferenced, brought into esteem as metaphysical, religious "life," psychic need\! (1) Cowardice; cowardice within a misunderstanding. (2) Delivered to every cheap seduction. (3) Convenience: compendia, overviews, excerpts, syntheses, translations, anthologies. Apparent richness — and all from second or third hand in the most uncontrolled interpretation.

### **Page 10: Introduction to Phenomenological Research (continued)**

Temporalizing of the concrete access to the object — that is already genuine engagement, a how of questioning concern. Genuine engagement\! "Genuineness" — taken from the being\! Decision about genuineness: the being-character of access, categories. Indication of the being in the initially: life. Indication of a being-character: life as being. Genuineness of engagement: being\! How-being\!

Research — a questioning seeking in and as temporalizing of a factical life and life-connection. "Questioning" is: "further-," "back-," "repeatingly" questioning, becoming more questionable in questioning\! Questioning is provisional, is in "answering."

### **Page 11: Phenomenological Research, University Philosophy, and Worldview-Teaching**

This writing concerns only phenomenology and is a reflection on its task. It addresses only those philosophers who are convinced that in philosophy it is first important to set one's own house in order before making a world-trip; those who in the genuine sense are pre-Socratics, genuinely wanting first to approach Socrates. Should the demand for concrete achievements be directed at the one who presents the "program," one must first request: to leave the notes of this booklet until these investigations "come." Whoever waits so long without himself grasping has not understood the essential.

### **Page 12: Confrontation**

Those who feel the main deficiencies and rightly think that this principled consideration first has a right to show itself publicly when the concrete achievement is there — they are asked to not take the matters seriously until then, if they can manage it. They can have it much more conveniently by confronting their own concrete research with what has been said.

Phenomenological research — the positive is decisive. Defense against mischief in phenomenology, naiveties, philosophy-improvement. Mischief with phenomenology: degraded into religion and worldview-babble. Against false and half scientific philosophy (Rickert and the like). Against supposed philosophical Sachforschung (Jaspers). Against the monstrosity of university philosophy (the little Fichtes and Hegels). Taking the *Ideas* seriously — for the first time.

### **Page 13: Saying Something New in Philosophy**

Saying something new is not the intention philosophy can have. The old (historical) is to be appropriated and understood in its genuine sense, nothing else. And this is very difficult today, where everyone says everything; when philosophy today is so cunning, deep, and comprehensive that everyone can console and reassure themselves that they have "also already said" this or that. As if something were achieved by having said something.

The guidelines are not a fixed frame but something far more decisive, from which the formation of the problematic has first to take place — guidelines of the temporalizing, the how of the enactment, how of the existence, because that threatens to be lost and because that is no personal, irrelevant life-moment unrelated to science and research — but what philosophy has concretely to co-temporalize.

### **Page 14: Questionability**

The "questionable" — the questionability, as content of wonder — of the astonished curiosity. Two phenomenological fundamental attitudes; one genuine, the other a misunderstanding. The questionable — the questionability (of "anxiety") of historical expectation, unrelieved and radically temporalized; and of the empty "what for and whence" of abandonedness. This is only destructively to be temporalized\! The flight from it is "curiosity."

Fundamental decision and fundamental task: where to question (what and being of life) and about what to become clear\! Principled atheism, principled confrontation — precisely because being is acknowledged as "objective." The devoted trust — and the commitment of ultimate questionability.

### **Page 15: Skepticism**

That there is insight, that I can arrive at cognizance, is to be conceded. The skeptic is the genuine absolutist; he takes formal lawfulness radically seriously. Why does Lotze speak of "human thinking"? Precisely because there are formal laws, skepticism has a sense\! It is about cognition\! And finally cognition of the decisive. And what is the decisive? Winning and appropriating the genuine pre-having and enactment of the genuine pre-conception.

### **Page 16: On the Introduction**

No theoretical skepticism within the theoretical as a skeptical statement about it — the lazy and tired skepticism that never begins but only talks. Rather: genuine position-taking in questioning itself, in the enactment of questionability. Questionability is not religious, but is able to lead into the situation of religious decision. I do not comport myself religiously in philosophizing, even though as a philosopher I can be a religious human being. Philosophy must in its radical, self-standing questionability be principally a-theistic. It must not presume to have and determine God. The more radical it is, the more determinately it is an away-from-him, and thus precisely in the radical enactment of the "away" a peculiar difficult "with" him.

### **Page 17: Illumination and Facticity**

Notes on Ebbinghaus's *Foundations of Hegelian Philosophy*. What "critique of theoretical consciousness" can mean. Separating "moral experience-concepts" from "theoretical object-concepts" — is anything gained? These are both isolations of forces, capacities, attitudes, acts that must not be objectual in this way. Rather, objecthood must be principally categorially made visible. For philosophy it is a matter of the most radical illumination — philosophical categorial-untheoretical breaking-open. The philosophical interest in questioning itself: philosophy self-understood must be atheistic.

## **Afterword of the Editors**

The editors, Walter Bröcker and Käte Bröcker-Oltmanns, explain the provenance and editorial principles of the volume. The lecture was delivered two hours per week in the Winter Semester 1921/22 at the University of Freiburg, where Heidegger was a Privatdozent. The edition rests on a photocopy of Heidegger's manuscript and a typewritten transcription by Dr. Hartmut Tietjen.

Heidegger's manuscript comprises three components: first, a lecture text designated by him as "Introduction"; second, a bundle titled "Presupposition"; and third, a number of unnumbered loose pages, some showing that Heidegger attempted after the lecture to make a book from the manuscript. It cannot be clearly determined what was written before and what after the delivery of the lecture.

The lecture manuscript stands on 43 quarto sheets written in landscape format. On the left stands the main text and to its right, mostly in multiple columns, additions that were presumably partly written after the delivery. The structuring of the texts, the corresponding table of contents, and the headings come from the editors unless otherwise noted. Heidegger's emphasis-markings by underlining served only for oral delivery; he directed that they not be observed for print.

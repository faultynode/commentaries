# Commentary on Heidegger, *Grundbegriffe der aristotelischen Philosophie* (GA 18), pp. 1–268 (Einleitung and Erster Teil)

Citations follow the Gesamtausgabe pagination of GA 18 (given in the running heads as "S 1," "S 2," etc.). Aristotelian passages are cited by Bekker line as Heidegger gives them. A short fixed glossary retains the Greek terms of art Heidegger treats as untranslatable and holds a few German coinages that carry the argument: *Dasein* (the human being's being, "being-there"); *ousia* (rendered "being-there," the being of a being in the how of its being); *horismos* (the delimiting *logos*, "definition"); *peras* (limit, the ground-character of the *Da*); *Sein-in-der-Welt* (being-in-the-world); *Miteinandersein* (being-with-one-another); *Besorgen* (concernful dealing, "taking care of"); *Sorge* (care); *Begegnen* (the world's "coming to meet" a living thing); *Man* (the anonymous "one" of everydayness); *Erschlossenheit* (disclosedness); *Bodenständigkeit* (rootedness — the course's guiding theme, the anchoredness of conceptuality in *Dasein*). Greek is retained where Heidegger's whole point is that no German or English word fits.

Standard traps this course sets, guarded against throughout. Overarching them all: the course's sole aim is to exhibit the *rootedness* (*Bodenständigkeit*) of conceptuality in *Dasein* itself, reading Aristotle's ground-concepts ontologically rather than doxographically — never "what was Aristotle's view of X" but "how is the matter meant in X experienced, addressed, and made intelligible." Being (*ousia*) for the Greeks means *being-there*, unfolded as presentness and being-finished; *horismos* ("definition") is no thought-technique but a mode of being-in-the-world. And more locally: first, Aristotle's *zōē*/*bios* pair is not the modern biological "life": *zōē* names being-in-a-world, *bios* the temporally stretched course of a life one leads and can give an account of. Second, *aisthēsis* is not "sensation" or "having sense-data" but the bare having-present of world. Third, the animal/human contrast (*phōnē* vs. *logos*) is not a contrast of two "views" of one world-in-itself; Heidegger bans the subject/object frame outright, so "the animal's world" and "the world in itself from which each takes a slice" are both misstatements. Fourth, *agathon* is *not* value, and *telos*/*teleion* is *not* purpose, goal, or aim — the primary sense is limit, end, being-finished; goal and purpose are founded, derivative modes. Reading Aristotle "teleologically" in the nineteenth-century sense is exactly the error Heidegger blocks. Fifth, *energeia* is not "activity" in the modern sense but being-at-work, the how of a being's standing in its ownmost possibility. Sixth, the *Man* is not the empirical fact that other people exist alongside me; it is a *how* of my own being. Throughout, Heidegger is reading these ethical and rhetorical texts ontologically — as evidence about how Greek *Dasein* interpreted its own being — not as ethics or as a theory of rhetoric.

## Introduction — The Philological Aim of the Course and Its Presuppositions

### § 1. The Philological Aim of the Course: Consideration of Some Ground-Concepts of Aristotelian Philosophy in Their Conceptuality

The course's aim is to win an understanding of some ground-concepts of Aristotelian philosophy, and to do so by way of occupying ourselves with the text of Aristotle's treatises. Ground-concepts — not all, but some, presumably the main affair of what Aristotelian research occupies itself with. We are favorably placed for the selection, since a treatise is transmitted from Aristotle himself that consists solely of definitions of these ground-concepts: what has come down as Book V of the *Metaphysics* (Δ). Yet we cannot simply hold to this convenient occasion, insofar as we are not in a position to understand as Aristotle's own pupils understood. Heidegger runs through the thirty items Δ defines — *archē*, *aition*, *stoicheion* (element), *phusis*, *anankaion* (necessity as a determination of being), *hen*, *on*, *ousia* (rendered here "being-there," *Dasein*), *tauta* (selfsameness), *antikeimena* (being-other), *protera* and *hystera* (not only temporal but factual), *dynamis*, *poson* (quantity), *poion* (quality), *pros ti* (relation), *teleion* (what is determined in its being-finished, being as finished), *peras*, *to katho* (the at-itself), *diathesis* (situation), *hexis* (the having-oneself, the being-so-and-so-placed toward something), *pathos* (state, disposedness), *sterēsis* (the determination of a being accomplished in what it does *not* have — a wholly *positive* determination: that it is *not* such-and-such makes up its being), *echein*, *ek tinos einai* (that from which something arises or consists), *meros* (part in the sense of moment), *holon* (whole), *kolobon* (the maimed), *genos* (descent), *pseudos*, *symbebēkos* (what steps up to something, is there with it).

The one thing that matters: to see the *ground* out of which these ground-concepts grow, and *how* they grow — i.e. to consider the ground-concepts with respect to their specific *conceptuality* (*Begrifflichkeit*), so that we ask *how the matters meant in them are themselves seen, with a view to what they are addressed, in what manner they are determined*. Bringing these viewpoints to the matter, we enter the milieu of what "concept" and "conceptuality" mean. The ground-concepts are to be understood with respect to their conceptuality, and with the aim of winning insight into the ground-requirements of every scientific research. No philosophy or history of philosophy is offered here. If *philology* means the passion for the knowledge of what is spoken, then what we do is philology. Everything about Aristotle's life, philosophy, and development one may learn from Jaeger's book; Jaeger has the merit of having expressly formulated the (already long-current) thought that Aristotle's writings are no *books* but gatherings of treatises, not published but brought only in his lectures — so that one must from the outset drop the effort to cut the fourteen treatises of the *Metaphysics* to one last and see in them the unitary presentation of an Aristotelian "system." Of a philosopher's biography only this holds interest: he was born then and then, worked, and died. The "figure" of the philosopher will not be given here.

### § 2. The Presuppositions of the Philological Aim. Delimitation of the Way in Which Philosophy Is Treated Here

The course has no philosophical purpose at all; it is a matter of understanding ground-concepts in their conceptuality. The purpose is philological: to bring the reading of philosophers somewhat more into practice. Such an aim naturally stands under a number of presuppositions, so strong that it is questionable whether one may take them on in a course at all. First: that Aristotle in particular has something to say — that Aristotle is chosen and not Plato, Kant, or Hegel — that he holds a distinguished position not only within Greek but within the whole of Western philosophy. Second: that we are none of us so far advanced that we have nothing more to let ourselves be told, that in some respect something is not right with us. Third: that *conceptuality makes up the substance of every scientific research*, that conceptuality is no matter of acumen — i.e. that whoever has chosen science has taken over the responsibility for the concept (a thing today gone missing). Fourth: science is no profession, no acquisition, no amusement, but a *possibility of the existence of the human*, not something one stumbled into by chance, but bearing in itself definite presuppositions one must bring along, insofar as one moves in earnest in the circle of what scientific research means. Fifth: human life has in itself the possibility of standing *solely on itself*, of getting along without faith, without religion and the like. Sixth (a methodical presupposition): the belief in history, in the sense that we presuppose that history and the historical past, if only the track is cleared for them, have the possibility of *giving a jolt* to a present, or better a future.

These six presuppositions are a strong imposition — but we pursue only philology. Philosophy today is better placed, insofar as it lives from the ground-presupposition that all is in best order. To delimit the manner in which we here treat of philosophy, Heidegger calls Aristotle himself as witness. We do treat of philosophy, but with the aim of *planting the instinct for the self-evident and the instinct for the old*. In *Metaphysics* Γ 2 Aristotle distinguishes *dialektikē*, *sophistikē*, and *philosophia*: *sophistikē* and *dialektikē* move in the circle of the same facts as *philosophia*, but *philosophia* differs from both in the manner of treatment. *Dialektikē* differs "in the manner of its possibility" (*tō tropō tēs dynameōs*): it "merely makes the attempt" (*peirastikē*), a *diaporeuesthai tous logous*, as Plato says, a running-through of what might be meant. The *dynamis* of *dialektikē* is, over against philosophy's, a restricted one. Yet *dialektikē* is *earnest* about the matter, about setting out what is meant — whereas *sophistikē* speaks about the same matter but "only looks like" philosophy, "is it not." *Dialektikē* has the earnestness, but only the earnestness of the attempting looking-to-see what in the end might be meant. In this sense we treat of philosophy, in the manner of looking-to-see what in the end might be meant. The decisive thing is that we reach, provisionally, an understanding about what is meant by philosophy.

## Erster Teil — Provisional Understanding of the Rootedness of Conceptuality by Way of an Explication of *Dasein* as Being-in-the-World, Oriented on Aristotelian Ground-Concepts

## Chapter 1 — Consideration of the Definition as the Place of the Explicitness of the Concept, and Return to the Ground of the Definition

### § 3. The Determination of the Concept through the Doctrine of Definition in Kant's *Logic*

What is meant by "concept" — "the Logic" answers us. But "*the* Logic" does not exist, if one speaks of it as "*the* Logic": it is a growth of Hellenistic school-philosophy that worked over the philosophical researches of the past in school-fashion. Neither Plato nor Aristotle knew "the Logic." Logic as it ruled in the Middle Ages can be determined as a material of concepts and rules assembled school-fashion; the "logical problems" stem from the horizon of the school-like communication of matters — there is no interest in a confrontation with the things, but only the imparting of definite technical possibilities. In this logic one speaks of *definition* as the means in which the concept receives its determination; so in considering definition we can see what is really meant by concept and conceptuality. We hold to Kant's *Logic* to see what, within actual research — the only such since Aristotle — is said about definition. Kant is the only one who lets logic become living; this logic, in its whole traditional stock, works on in the Hegelian dialectic, which is wholly uncreative, only a working-over of the traditional logical material in definite respects.

Looking at what Kant calls definition, one finds the striking fact that it is treated in the chapter on "General Doctrine of Method": definition is a *methodical* affair, to win *distinctness* of knowledge, treated among the means of furthering "the distinctness of concepts with respect to their content." At the same time definition is itself a concept: "definition is alone a logically perfected concept." So we do not, at bottom, learn from definition what a concept is, and hold rather to what Kant himself says of the concept. Every *intuition*, he says, is a *repraesentatio singularis*; the concept too is a *repraesentatio*, a making-present, but a *repraesentatio per notas communes*. The concept differs from intuition in that, though a making-present, it makes present something of the character of the *universal* — a "general representation." Kant illustrates it (Introduction to the *Logic*): in every cognition matter and form are to be distinguished, "the way we cognize the object." A savage sees a house whose *what-for* he does not know quite otherwise than we who know our way in it; he sees the same being, but lacks the knowledge of its use, does not understand what he is to do with it — he forms no concept of *house*. We know what it is for, and thereby represent something universal, have the concept of the house. The concept goes to answering the question *what the object is*. So conceptuality and the sense of the concept hang on how one understands the question after what something is, wherein this question has its origin. The concept, in the explicitness of definition, gives what the object, the *res*, is. Hence the proper definition is the so-called "real definition," which determines what the *res* is in itself. Definition is accomplished by giving the genus and specific difference. This rule at first seems strange — why just genus and species should determine the object in its what. Remarkably, Kant says the real definition has the task of determining the *what* of the matter from the "first ground" of its "possibility," or according to its "inner possibility," but that the determination of *definitio* as happening through *genus proximum et differentiam specificam* holds only for the "nominal definitions," which arise through comparison — precisely for the *definitio* of the *res* this determination does not come into question. Two moments are characteristic of Kant's position: that *definitio* is discussed in the doctrine of method, and that he determines the ground-rule of *definitio* so that it does not come into question for the proper definition.

We shall ask back thus: whence does it come that *definitio* determines the being in its being? Whence that a *definitio*, which is really factual knowledge, becomes a matter of *logical perfection*? In this position of Kant's toward *definitio* lies the *destiny of Aristotelian research*. So we ask back: *definitio* is *horismos*, *horismos* a *logos*, a "speaking-oneself-out" about being-there as being. *Horismos* is not a determination of sharp grasping; in the end the specific character of *horismos* grows from the fact that the being itself is, in its being, determined as *limited* by the *peras*. Being means being-finished.

### § 4. The Moments of the Conceptuality of the Aristotelian Ground-Concepts and the Question of Their Rootedness

Decisive for the return to definition was that in definition, according to traditional logic, the concept becomes explicit in the proper sense, comes to itself. Kant distinguishes concept from intuition: intuition sees an individual merely in its being-there, while the concept sees the same object but, as it were, *understands* it. In the concept's *repraesentatio* I know what is understood by it, but another too knows it — the concept makes the represented intelligible for others too: hence "general representation." In definition the concept is to come to itself: the *definitio* is to give a matter so that it is represented and understood in the ground of its possibility, that I know whence it comes, what it is, why it is that. The proper definition is that of the matter, the real definition (in the Middle Ages, real definition and essence-definition), and it comes about by satisfying the ground-rule: at an object one gives the next-higher genus and the specific difference (e.g. the circle is a closed, curved line [genus], every point of which has equal distance from the center [specific difference]; or *homo animal* [genus] *rationale* [difference]).

To show that what traditional logic treats as *definitio* has a quite definite origin — that definition is a *falling-phenomenon* (*Verfallserscheinung*), a mere thought-technique that was once the ground-possibility of the human's speaking — we go back to Aristotle. In definition the concept becomes explicit; yet what the concept itself is in its conceptuality is not thereby made visible. We do *not* simply want to learn Aristotelian ground-concepts by asking: what did Aristotle mean by movement, what view did he have of it as against the Platonic or modern conception? We are interested in this concept *at once in its conceptuality*. First, we have to ask what is meant by this concept, as what the meant is concretely *experienced*: what hovered before Aristotle as movement, which movement-phenomena did he see, which sense of being did he mean when he speaks of the being-moved? We do not ask with the aim of taking cognizance of a concept-content, but ask *how the meant matter is experienced*. Second, how is the originally seen *primarily addressed*? Does Aristotle explain movement from concepts or theories already at hand — Platonically, as the passage from a non-being to a being — or do determinacies grow for him that lie in the phenomenon itself? In what manner is a phenomenon like movement addressed, according to the *leading claim* on the seen matter? Third, how is the so-sighted phenomenon more closely unfolded, into what conceptuality is it, as it were, spoken? What claim of intelligibility is placed on the so-seen? This raises the question of the *originality of the explication*: is it brought to the phenomenon from outside, or is it fitting to it?

These three moments *indicate* — only indicate, do not exhaust — the conceptuality: first, the *ground-experience* (*Grunderfahrung*) in which I make the factual character accessible to myself — primarily not theoretical at all, but lying in life's dealing with its world; second, the *leading claim* (*führender Anspruch*); third, the specific character of intelligibility, the specific *intelligibility-tendency* (*Verständlichkeitstendenz*). According to these three viewpoints we shall in each case interrogate the Aristotelian ground-concepts, to see whether therewith a proper understanding of the matters meant in them is not at once given. The aim of setting out the conceptuality is that you *sense* that in conceptuality that comes into movement which, in every scientific research, makes up the enactment of questioning and determining. It matters not on taking-cognizance but on *understanding*. You have yourselves to do the proper work — not to philosophize, but each at his own place to become attentive to the conceptuality of a science, really to get it into grip, and to pursue it so that the research-enactment of conceptuality becomes living. Not to study all the philosophies-of-science that appear monthly! It is a matter of having, in the right enactment of the specific science, a genuine, right, and earnest relation to the matters of your science — not so that you *apply* Aristotelian concepts, but that you do at your place what Aristotle did at his, namely see and determine the matter in the same originality and genuineness. My task is merely to procure for Aristotle the occasion to show you the matter.

When we interrogate the Aristotelian ground-concepts after their conceptuality, we must understand how these moments called conceptuality hang together, where they properly belong, where ground-experience, claim, and intelligibility-tendency are *rooted* (*bodenständig*). The rootedness of conceptuality we shall have to seek out — not any arbitrary one, since we want to understand *Aristotle's* ground-concepts; we shall have to see how Greek conceptuality and the rootedness of Greek conceptuality look. Only then do we go securely in following the scientific explication that guides Aristotle.

### § 5. Return to the Ground of the Definition

Going back to what definition originally was, we perhaps learn too what that originally was which today is called concept.

#### a) The Predicables

Genus and species are characteristics that determine every definition, but not the only ones; to them belong the further moment of the *proprium* and the *differentia specifica* as such. These moments that guide concept-formation one calls *predicables* or *katēgorēmata*. They were first systematically treated by Porphyry in his introduction to Aristotle's *Categories*; this *Eisagōgē*, translated into Latin by Boethius, became the Middle Ages' ground-book on logical questions, and from it the so-called quarrel of universals developed. There are five predicables. *Genus est unum, quod de pluribus specie differentibus in eo quod quid est praedicatur* — "curved, closed line," the genus of the circle, is said of several things differing in species (ellipse), but the saying goes to what the circle is in itself. *Species est unum, quod de pluribus solo numero differentibus in eo quod quid est praedicatur* — the individual circles differ *solo numero*. *Differentia specifica* (*diaphora*): *unum, quod de pluribus praedicatur in quale essentiale*, with respect to what belongs to what they are — the rationality of the human. *Proprium*: *unum, quod de pluribus praedicatur in quale necessarium*, a "necessary" determination that belongs to the thing beyond the essence-nexus of species and genus. *Accidens*: *unum, quod de pluribus praedicatur in quale contingens*, insofar as it "falls to" what is addressed (*symbebēkos*). These *praedicabilia* are also called *universalia*; the finer distinction is that *universale* means *unum quod est in pluribus*, whereas *praedicabile* means *unum quod de pluribus praedicatur*. Hence the question whether the universal really exists in the things or is only a meant-content of apprehending thinking (realism–nominalism) — a question that too has its origin in definite factual nexuses of Greek philosophy, more precisely in school-like misunderstandings.

#### b) The Aristotelian Determination of *horismos* as *logos ousias*

We now ask after conceptuality and its rootedness by going back from *definitio* as technical means to *horismos*, "delimitation." *Horismos* is a *logos*, a "speaking" about something, an addressing of the matter "itself in what it is," *kath' auto*. A *legein kath' auto*: the matter "at itself," and only it, is and shall be addressed. Hence *horismos* is determined as *ousias tis gnōrismos*; *gnōrismos* means "making-acquainted-with," "making-familiar-with," to present a matter. *Horismos* is the making-familiar-with a being in its being. What does *logos ousias* mean — *logos* and *ousia*?

*Logos*: "speaking," not in the sense of emitting a sound, but of speaking-about-something in the manner of the pointing-out of the about-which, through which the spoken-of shows itself. The proper function of *logos* is *apophainesthai*, the "bringing-a-matter-to-be-seen." Every speaking is, above all for the Greeks, a speaking to or with others, with oneself or to oneself. Speaking, in concrete *Dasein* — since one does not exist alone — is speaking with others about something. Speaking with others about something is always a speaking-oneself-out; in speaking about something with others I speak myself out, explicitly or not. And what is this *logos*? It is the *fundamental determination of the being of the human as such*. The human is seen by the Greeks as *zōon logon echon*, not only philosophically but in concrete life: "a living thing that [as living] has language." At this definition one must not think of biology, or of humanistic psychology and the like; the determination lies *before* such distinctions. *Zōē* is a being-concept: "life" means a manner of being, namely being-in-a-world. A living thing is not simply present-at-hand but is in a world in such a way that it *has* its world. An animal is not simply set on the street and moved along it by some apparatus being shoved; it is in the world in the manner of *having* it. The human's being-in-the-world is at bottom determined by *speaking*: the manner of the human's fundamental being in his world is to speak *with* it, *about* it, *of* it. So the human is determined precisely through *logos*, and one sees where — if definition is a *logos* — this matter of definition has its ground, insofar as *logos* is the ground-determination of the human's being. The *logos* as *horismos* addresses the being in its *ousia*, its being-there. So we must reach an understanding about *ousia*.

### § 6. Preliminary Clarification of *logos*

The conceptuality meant in the ground-concepts is *ground-experience that gives the matter*, not a theoretical grasping of the matters. What is experienced in it is addressed to something; the so-experienced, placed into this respect, is explicated and becomes living in the addressing. We must win the rootedness as it was living in Greek science. *Horismos*: *logos ousias*. Traditional school-language has for "concept": *notio*, *intentio*, *conceptus*, *species*. *Notio*: in the concept lies a definite "acquaintedness" with the matter meant — the concept transposes into a being-acquainted with it. *Intentio*: in the concept lies an "aiming-at," a meaning of something — the meaning of a matter is an essential structural moment of the concept ("matter" always used quite generally, in the sense of the mere something). *Conceptus*: the "grasping" — the matter is not only meant, not only acquainted, but meant and acquainted *in the manner of being-grasped*, so that what lies in it is embraced, taken together. *Species*: *eidos*, "look" — leads back to *notio*: when I am acquainted with the matter I know how it looks, how it takes itself out among others. These designations have won a quite worn-off meaning in their school-use, so that they are uniformly rendered "concept."

Definition is to be considered with respect to its origin: *logos ousias*. *Logos* is the "speaking" and at once, for the Greeks, the "spoken," speaking in the ground-function of *apophainesthai* or *dēloun*, "bringing-a-matter-to-show-itself" in speaking about something. This speaking-about-something is by its tendency a speaking-with-others, a speaking-oneself-out. In speaking with others and with myself I bring the addressed to givenness, in that in speaking I experience how the matter looks. Speaking is no mere process that now and then occurs; this speaking-about-something with others is at once a speaking-oneself-out — un-lifted-out structural moments of *logos*. The spoken "lies fixed," is a *keimenon*. The *keimena onomata* are, as fixed-down, available to others; they are *koina*, belong to everyone. Once a word is spoken, it no longer belongs to me; so language is something that belongs to everyone — and precisely in this common possession a ground-possibility of life itself is given living: mostly it is only *still spoken* — the going-up in mere words, without an explicit relation to the matters talked about. Therein lies an intelligibility common to all. Growing into a language, I grow into an intelligibility of world and of language that I have of myself, insofar as I live in language. A common intelligibility is given, of a peculiar character of *averageness*; it no longer has the character of belonging-to-the-individual, it is worn off, used, used up. Every spoken has the possibility of being used up, of moving into the common intelligibility.

This speaking is used by the Greeks themselves — not only in the explicit reflection on the human's life and *Dasein* as it lies in philosophy, but also in natural findability — to determine the human's being itself in its peculiarity. The human is determined as *zōon logon echon*, a "living thing" — no biological concept in modern coinage. Life is a *how*, a category of being, and not something wild, deep, and mystical; it is characteristic that "philosophy of life" never came to ask what is really meant *categorially* by the concept "life" as being. Life is a being-in-a-world; animal and human are present not *beside* others but *with* others, and (in the human's case) they speak themselves out to one another. Speaking-oneself-out as speaking-about is the ground-mode of the being of life, i.e. of being-in-a-world. Where there is no speaking, where speaking ceases, where the living thing no longer speaks, we speak of "death." From this ground-possibility of life the being of life in general is at last to be understood. Speaking is referred into the being-nexus of life as a specific being. The *echon* in *zōon logon echon* is to be understood in a quite fundamental sense: *echein* is determined (*Met.* Δ 23) as *agein*, to "carry on" a matter, to be in a manner according to an "impulse" (*hormē*) that comes from this being. Language is *had*, it is spoken, so that speaking belongs to the human's proper being-impulse. The human's life means speaking. This refers the preliminary clarification of *logos* into a being-nexus provisionally designated as the human's life.

### § 7. *ousia* as the Ground-Concept Simply of Aristotelian Philosophy

The ground-function of *logos* is the bringing-to-show-itself of the being in its being, of the *ousia* as the "being" of the being, or as "being-ness." Therewith is meant that the being of a being itself still has determination-moments, that of the being, in the *how* of its being, something more can be made out. But *ousia* as "the being in the how of its being" is, in Aristotle himself, *many-meaninged*, has various meanings, and is at once the title for the factual nexuses that make up the theme of Aristotle's fundamental research. *Ousia* is the expression for the ground-concept simply of Aristotelian philosophy. Along the thread of *ousia* we shall learn not only what *horismos* is but win a ground on which to place the other ground-concepts.

#### a) The Various Kinds of Many-Meaningness of Concepts and of the Becoming of Termini

*Ousia* is many-meaninged in Aristotle. That might seem to damage the use of the expression, since a many-meaningness of the ground-concept of a research could be dangerous. But not every many-meaningness is of the same character. There is: first, the *many-meaningness of confusion* — arising when a word stands in a definite use with various, already-clarified meanings that then, from lack of factual competence, get thrown together; it sets in afterward and effaces what was once set out in an explicit research. Second, the many-meaningness that grows from an *incapacity* to see definite factual nexuses with respect to their possible differences, from a difference-insensitivity in conceptual grasping. Third, many-meaningness can be the *index* that the word-region has grown in its many-meaningness from a *genuine* relation to, a genuine familiarity with, the matters — that the manifoldness of meaning is *demanded by the matter*, an *articulated manifold of distinguished meaning*, so that the matter itself demands the same expression but in various meanings. And so it is in Aristotle (example: Book Δ). Precisely that Aristotle does not trouble to remove this many-meaningness, to level it in some fantastic systematics, shows his instinct for the matters: he lets the meaning stand *in the face of the matters*. So manifoldness of meaning can be index of various things; one will do well not to confuse one's own confusion with the manifoldness of meaning in Aristotle — one must see whether the many-meaningness in fact comes from the matters.

To these many-meaninged ground-concepts belongs *ousia*. Such expressions of the character of emphatic expressing one calls *termini*, and the meaning that expressly belongs to them within a scientific question-nexus is the *terminological* meaning. There are various possibilities of the becoming of termini. First: a definite factual nexus is discovered, newly seen for the first time — the word is lacking, and is coined together with the matter; the expression may later fall off into the general currency of speaking. Second: the terminus attaches to an existing word, so that a meaning-moment co-meant in the current meaning, but not explicitly, now becomes thematic in the terminological meaning.

#### b) The Current Meaning of *ousia*

The expression *ousia* as fundamental terminus of Aristotelian research stems from an expression that in natural language has a current meaning. The current meaning is that which a word has in natural speaking — the speaking that plays itself out mostly and first and always, even where another manner of speaking-with-the-world, the scientific, is at hand; it moves in the averageness of understanding, is apt to go about as self-evident, is understood "without further ado." "*One*" (*man*) understands without further ado an expression of the current character; it is there in the common possession of language into which each new human grows. But with *ousia* it is not that the terminological meaning grew out of the current one and the current then vanished; in Aristotle the current meaning stands constantly *alongside* the terminological. And *ousia* in its current meaning says: "capacity," "state of possession," "goods and chattels," "estate" (*Anwesen*). We have the remarkable fact that a definite being — beings such as state-of-possession, household-gear, and so on — is addressed by the Greeks as *properly being*. So looking into this current meaning we may perhaps get behind what is meant by "being" among the Greeks at all. But we must beware of deducing the terminological from the current meaning; it can only be a matter of understanding the current meaning so that we take *directives* from it toward the terminological.

The current meaning of *ousia* designates a definite being, not mountains or other humans. *Ousia* is terminologically "the being in the how of its being" (otherwise translated "substance" — it is left open whether one can present more to oneself under "substance" than under "the being in the how of its being"). In the current meaning this "in the how of its being" is not emphasized. But we too have in our German expressions certain meanings that mean not only a being but also mean it in the how of its being: *Anwesen* (estate), *Vermögen* (capacity/means), *Hab und Gut* (goods and chattels). *Ousia* is such a being that is there for me in an emphatic manner, so that I can use it, that stands at my disposal, that I daily have to do with — the being there in my daily dealing with the world, even when I do science: a preferred, fundamental being *as* being in its being, in the how of its being. Even in the current meaning the *how* of being is co-meant. The how of being means being-there in the manner of *being-available*. This gives a finger-pointing that *being*, for the Greeks, from the outset means *being-there* (*Dasein*). The further clarification of the being in its being has to move in the direction of the question: *what does "Da" mean?* From the clarification of the *Da*-character of the being, its being becomes visible.

So we see how the terminological meaning of *ousia* stems from the current. Currently, *ousia* is a definite being in the how of its being, the *how* only co-meant. The terminological meaning gives, thematically, the *how* of being, hitherto only inexplicitly meant — and not only the how of *this* being but of *every* being. *Ousia* can thus mean, first, directly the being (the how co-meant); second, directly the *how* of the being of a being (this being itself co-meant). So *ousia* means: a being, and the how of being, being, being-ness, being in the sense of *being-there*; and *ousia* in the sense of being-there encloses a doubleness: the being-there-thing, and the being of the being-there-thing. No accident that the Greek designation for the things as they first encounter is *pragmata*, "beings one constantly has to do with," and *chrēmata*, "what is taken into use" — they point to the ground-meaning of *ousia*. Aristotle says the old question *ti to on?*, what is the being, is really the question after the being of the being, *tis hē ousia?* — and brings scientific research for the first time onto this ground, a ground Plato himself did not once suspect.

#### c) The Terminological Meaning of *ousia*

*Ousia* is the title for the object of the proper fundamental research of Aristotelian, and Greek, philosophy. The terminus *ousia* has grown from an expression ruling in everyday language and there meaning a definite being — the being of the character of capacity, possession, estate. We take this current meaning as thread, asking whether even in the terminological meaning meaning-moments of the current are still contained — only as thread; no deducing. The characteristic is that not only a being is expressed but a *being in the how of its being*. By "household-estate" I mean a being that is there in an emphatic way: the being first and mostly there in life, within which life factically mostly moves, out of which, as it were, life ekes its being-there. So in the current meaning of *ousia* lies a *doubling*: a being, but at once in the how of its being. The terminological meaning is distinguished by fixing just this *how* of being, so that *ousia* does not primarily designate a being but the *how* of the being of this being, a definite being co-meant. When one uses the terminus *ousia*, even in its current meaning, a definite being-concept is already meant. *Ousia* as *einai*, "being," has its quite definite being-meaning, grown from the primary understanding the Greeks have of the being that first encounters them; and this primary sense of being still swings along in the terminological meaning. So *ousia* means: first, a being in the how of its being; second, the *how* of the being of the being. Should there be a research that has *being* for its theme, it will somehow be held to make present to itself the *being* too, since in the end the character of its being is to be read off only *at* the being itself — so that the being must necessarily be reckoned in. Within such research every being-concept has a specific *double character* of its meaning.

**α) *ousia* as being.** Of the two ground-directions of the meaning of *ousia*, we take first the one meaning the being itself. Here the expression itself occurs; one speaks of *ousiai*, various "beings," because they have various being-characters. The being itself is always primarily experienced *before* the being. *Metaphysics* Z 2: *dokei d' hē ousia hyparchein phanerōtata men tois sōmasin* — "the being of the being shows itself most evidently at the *sōmata*." If we render *sōma* by "body," note that bodiliness for the Greek does not mean stuff-liness or materiality; *sōma* means a peculiar *obtrusiveness* of a being, a being-there, so that later *to son sōma*, "your *sōma*," equals *you*, and *sōma* later means "slave," "prisoner" — a being that belongs to me, stands at my disposal, is there for me in this obtrusiveness and self-evidence. Such *sōmata* are accordingly not only body-things but animals, trees, earth, water, air, *ta phusika*, and also the *ouranos* — not only dead things, but the being first and mostly there in the everydayness of life. Of this being Aristotle says the *ousia* shows itself *phanerōtata*, directly and first. Whether there is still other being that satisfies this is an open question. *Ousiai homologoumenai*: everyone says, agreeing with the other without further ado, the same — namely that these beings *are*. These beings are addressed, in the self-evidence of natural *Dasein*, as being in the proper sense. Hence it is self-evident for Aristotle, and for every research that investigates being and wants ground under its feet, that it starts from the consideration of the being (and being-structure) that is first there in this way, from a sense of being that naturalness understands without further ado. Life moves in a natural intelligibility of what in its speaking it means, without further ado, by "being" and "being-thing." *Metaphysics* Z 3 (end): *homologountai d' ousiai einai tōn aisthētōn tines* — "one agrees that being in the proper sense is something of what is taken in by *aisthēsis*." When Aristotle speaks of *aisthēton*, he never means an objectivity of the character of sense-data, made present through "sensations"; by *aisthēsis* he means the *taking-in* of the being in the natural way, a taking-in distinguished by the senses' being co-engaged, co-mediating the access — the natural way we see trees, the moon, and speak of them. Agreement rules that the being accessible through *aisthēsis* has the character of *ousia*; hence in the field of this being the investigation is in the first line to be undertaken: the investigation after the structure of *ousia* itself.

**β) *ousia* as being. The being-characters (Met. Δ 8).** What such a research of the being of the being sets out as being-characters we cannot follow in detail. For orientation we make present some being-characters, and the manifoldness of the meanings of *ousia* where it means the being of a being, taking as basis *Metaphysics* Δ 8, and considering at once whether and how, in these being-characters, the sense of being co-speaks that we found in the current meaning of *ousia* ("household-estate" *Hausstand*) — whether the being-characters are also characters in the sense of the *Da*. Aristotle introduces the chapter with the enumeration of the *sōmata*, to indicate the ground from which he sets the whole investigation of being to work. (1) As first being-character: the *hypokeimenon*. Such beings as animals, plants, humans, mountains, sun are of such a kind that they already lie there *in advance*. When I speak about them, say something of an animal, describe a plant, then what I speak about, the spoken-of I have there in speaking, is present-at-hand, already lying there in advance. The being of this being has the character of *being-present-at-hand* (*Vorhandensein*). (2) *Aition enyparchon*, "what is co-present-at-hand" in such a being, in the function of the *aition tou einai*. Such a being-character is the *psychē*. That the soul is *ousia* means: it is a being-character present in a being of the first-named sense, co-present-at-hand, in such a way that it co-makes-up the specific being of what we call living. It is "to blame for," makes up, the specific being of a living thing, i.e. of a being in the sense of being-in-a-world. The two ground-moments are *krinein* and *kinein*. A living thing is not simply present-at-hand (accessible for everyone) but is, in an emphatic way, still there in its present-at-handness: it can see, do, move itself. *Krinein*, the "setting-off" against something other, the getting-oriented in a world; *kinein*, the "moving-oneself-therein," the having-to-do-therein, the going-about-and-around-therein. One must therefore, in discussing Greek philosophy, be somewhat more cautious with the famous "substantiality" of the soul: *ousia* means a manner of being, and if the soul is called *ousia*, that means a *distinguished* manner of being, namely the being of the living thing.

(3) *Morion enyparchon*: represented, e.g., by the *surface* at a body. If I take the surface at a body away from the *Da*, the body is thereby cancelled — no longer there. The surface makes up the being-there and the possible being-there of a body, just as the line the possible being-there of a surface. Hence the surface as moment of a body is a being-character Aristotle also calls *horizon*, "that which limits": the body is limited by the being-character of the *morion enyparchon*, i.e. the being is determined in its being. This is possible only because for the Greeks the *limit* is a quite fundamental character of the being-there of the being. Limit-hood is a fundamental character of the *Da*. This *horizon* is *sēmainon tode ti*, it "designates" the being in which it is present as a "that there," so that this "that there" becomes visible, determinable, graspable in its being-ness. Because delimitation plays so peculiar a role that it determines a being in its being, some came to designate "generally" the limit, in the widest sense *number*, as the *ousia*: Pythagoreans and Platonists saw in number the proper *ousia*, numbers as *ousiai*. The number-like limits the being as such; they are no *substances*, demons, existing about. (4) *To ti ēn einai*: Aristotle did not coin this terminus but had it handed to him. It is a character of being, and indeed the one with a view to which the *logos* as *horismos* addresses the being; the *to ti ēn einai* is precisely the *theme of horismos*. It means "being," and namely the "what-being, as it already was." It means a being at itself, with respect to what it *already was*, whence it stems in its being, with respect to its *provenance*, its having-come into the being-there-thing. Hence *to ti ēn einai* is the "being of a respective," *ousia hekastou*. This is not "each" or "individual." *Hekas* means "far," *hekaston* "respective," insofar as I tarry at it, see it in a certain distance. The respective is precisely *not* seen immediately and directly; it first becomes accessible when I take a certain distance from it and so present it to myself in this distance. *Ta kath' hekasta* are the moments that make up the respectiveness of a being; they become present only when I have a distance from them. In natural dealing the familiar objects are not properly there for me; I see past them, they have not the character of presence, are far too everyday, vanish, as it were, from my everyday *Dasein*. Only at some unusual event can something I daily deal with suddenly become present in its presence. The respectiveness is not first and directly given; it needs a taking-of-distance to see everydayness in its being-there, to have it present — and the being-characters that explicitly show the being-there-thing in its being-there, that make up the *Da*-character of being, are enclosed in Aristotle's *to ti ēn einai*.

So Aristotle distinguishes two *tropoi*, "ground-modes," in which *ousia* is used: the *hypokeimenon eschaton*, already there for every dealing with it; and the being of the character of *tode ti on*, of which I say "that there," *chōriston*, standing "at its own place," being present "self-standingly." (5) This self-standingness is expressed by the *eidos*, "what is seen, sighted," the "look," the "taking-itself-out" of a being. What I here see and establish as being-there self-standingly takes itself out as a chair — is, for the Greek, a chair.

**γ) *ousia* as being-there. The being-characters as characters of the *Da*.** We have moved only in an enumeration of being-characters; it matters to see how in these various characters of being a definite conception of the *Da* comes to expression — how these various being-characters are quite definite characters of the *sense of the Da*, how the Greek understands the *Da*. For this we already have a thread in the current meaning of *ousia* as the "available," "present," the present-at-hand in the sense of "estate," "state-of-possession." *Ousia* means "being-there" and has *not* an indifferent sense of being, which at bottom does not exist at all. *Ousia* is the abbreviation of *parousia*, "being-present." More frequent is its opposite *apousia*, "absence" — i.e. not simply nothing, but something *is* there, there in a *lack*. Squinting is a seeing in the *apousia*. The *apousia* is the ontological foundation for the ground-category of *sterēsis*. We try to bring the named characters of the *Da* to a ground-orientation.

(1) *Hypokeimenon*, "being-present-at-hand," the "present-at-handness" of something. This being-character hangs with being in the current sense: it means the being-there-thing not only as being-there but, e.g., the being-there-thing in the sense of that on which the estate stands — the land, earth, heaven, nature, trees, what is present-at-hand as the being wherein concrete life ekes its being-there. *Ousia* thus as present-at-handness, without my needing to do anything to the being of this being-there-thing. (2) *Aition (tou einai) enyparchon*, example *psychē*. The "soul" is *ousia* in the sense that it makes up the being-there of a being of the character of a living thing. A living thing has a quite characteristic being-there: (a) it is there in the sense of the *hypokeimenon*, present-at-hand like stones, tables; (b) but the human does not stand by the way like a stone — he goes for a walk under trees. I meet him somewhere, but this his being-there as occurrence, as "world," is characterized by its being in the manner of being-in-the-world: it is by having an orientation. The human is there so that he is in the world in the manner of *having* his world; he has his world by dealing with it. *Psychē* as being-character is a distinguished one that encloses in itself the being as *hypokeimenon*. (3) *Morion enyparchon*, which makes up the possible being of something: the point, the line, number as proper being-character, because number is delimitation — but number, point, and so on, are being-characters only if it is demonstrable that for the Greek the *limit* and being-limited is the proper being-character. (4) *To ti ēn einai*: already the composition points to a whole complex of being-determinations, to be detached later. The being of the character of *to ti ēn einai* is the proper theme of the *logos* we discuss as *horismos*; it is the character of the *hekaston*: every being-there-thing in its respectiveness is determined by the *to ti ēn einai*. In the summary a fifth appears: *eidos*, already in Aristotle in the meaning "species." Why *eidos* means "species" and *genos* "genus" one does not understand unless one knows that *eidos* is a quite definite being-character: first meant is the being-there-thing in its "taking-itself-out." When a builder builds a house he lives and moves first in the *eidos* of the house, in how it looks.

The *to ti ēn einai* has in itself the determination of the *ēn* (the "was"): the being-there of a being seen with a view to what it *was*, to its *provenance*. When the human is determined as *zōon logon echon*, the speaking comes from his *zōon*-, "living-thing"-being; this is his *genos*. I see a being-there-thing with respect to its being as coming-from... I see a being-there-thing properly in its being when I see it in its *history*, the so-being-there-thing come into being out of its history. This being-there-thing as so there is *finished*, come to its end, to its finishedness, just as the house in its *eidos* as *poioumenon* is finished. The *hypokeimenon* is already finished; I need not first produce it. The body has, through the surface, its finishedness. So *being-there* means, summarily: first, primarily *presentness*, present; second, *being-finished*, finishedness — the two characters of the *Da* for the Greeks. In these two all beings are to be interpreted with respect to their being.

### § 8. *horismos* as a Definite Mode of Being-in-the-World. The Task of Understanding the Ground-Concepts, in Their Conceptuality, into *Dasein* as Being-in-the-World

*Logos* as *horismos* is such a "speaking," "addressing" of the world that in it the being is addressed with respect to its finishedness and this as present. *Horismos* is *logos ousias* in the sense that *ousia* designates the *to ti ēn einai*. The *logos* as *horismos* is thus a quite distinguished *legein*, a definite possibility within *legein*. *Legein* is not primarily enacted as a *horizein*; primarily the world is given in the *proximally* (*Zunächst*), as *synkechymenon*, "blurred," "buried," "unarticulated." It needs a special arrangement, a special opening of the eyes, to see the being-there-thing in its being. Aristotle had an explicit consciousness that *logos* as *horismos* is *not* everyday. He formulates this at the entry to the investigation of *ousia*, *Metaphysics* Z 3: "learning, the making-oneself-acquainted with something, is enacted for all so that it goes over what is *less familiar by nature* to what is *more familiar*." When I learn something, I have something pre-given, and this pre-given is known only in the averageness of *Dasein*; I am oriented about it in a superficial way. Going through this, I work my way in learning forward to what can properly be known. "As it shows in all concern, that one proceeds from what respectively appears first as good to the proper good and appropriates this proper good for oneself as good, so it is too with the becoming-acquainted with being that is respectively first known" — it is "often unsharply (*ērema*) known." I have not the time, the occasion, to look at the being-there-thing more sharply. This being-there-thing "has little or nothing of being." It is so self-evidently there that I see past it, do not heed it — and precisely in this not-being-heeded shows the self-evidence of the being-there of world. But I must start precisely from what is *inauthentically* there, to reach what is now properly to be brought into acquaintedness. These sentences are programmatic, the proper counter-thrust against Platonic philosophy: I must have ground under my feet, a ground that is there in a first self-evidence, to trouble myself about being — I cannot fantastically hold to a definite being-concept and then speculate.

The methodical stance shows quite in principle at the entry of the *Physics* (one of the earliest investigations, probably worked out when Aristotle still worked with Plato in the Academy). What is first known, from which I start, is the *katholou*, "something I have there in overview." I am oriented offhand in my *Umwelt* without being able, asked point-blank what it is, to give an answer. It matters to see, through the *katholou*, the proper being. This shows at the relation between speaking as current and the terminus. The word, used in natural speaking, points to a being-there-thing closed in itself, but without this so-addressed being *delimited-through*. When, on the contrary, the meaning and word-use is enacted in a *logos* that is *horismos*, then it takes the so-being-there-thing apart into what makes up the proper "respectiveness" of such an object: *ta kath' hekasta* are the moments that bring the at-first superficially-meant into the needed distance, so that I properly see it in its articulatedness. "Children [who as such live in a quite distinguished sense in their world, which they have unarticulated] are wont to address all men as fathers and all women as mothers; only later do they come to the *diorizein*." Father and mother is the very first and average interpretation of the being-there of humans for the child; it is first accessible, and it applies to every man and every woman. This indeterminacy of the pervasive gives the child precisely the possibility of orienting itself among the being-there-things, the humans. From this, from what is first, one is to start, and to see this ground explicitly. Out of this basis of natural being in everydayness grows the characteristic possibility of a peculiar speaking that now addresses the being-there in its proper presentness, in the character of its *peras*, so addresses it that being-there is addressed *in its limitedness*.

This addressing of being-there in its limitedness is a *logos* as *horismos*. A limiting speaking means, for the Greeks, an addressing of the proper being-there. That limit, limitedness makes up the proper *Da*-character, we see from *Metaphysics* Δ 17: *peras* is the *eschaton*, "the utmost of a respective being-there-thing, outside which as the first nothing further of the matter in question is to be met, within which the whole of the being in question is to be seen." This character of *peras* is at once determined as *eidos*: limit-hood is the proper "look of a being that has some extension." But *peras* is not only *eidos* but also *telos*. *Telos* means "end" in the sense of finishedness, not "goal" or even "purpose." Finishedness is such a *peras* "toward which the movement and the action go," *kinēsis* and *praxis*, the being-busy-with-something, where a movement or action finds its end (no purpose-idea!). There is also being where both have this limit-character. The character of *peras* has too the *hou heneka*, the "for-the-sake-of-which" something happens. The proper, last character of being in the *eidos* and *telos* is the character of *peras*. Limit for *knowing* is limit-hood only because it is limit-hood *of the matter*, determines the *pragma* in its limit.

From here is to be taken what for Aristotle and the Greeks the maxim within theoretical research means: *mē eis apeiron ienai*, not to go to the unlimited. *Eis apeiron ienai* is a going to something that no longer *is* at all, because the limit is lacking. This maxim of avoiding the *regressus ad infinitum* has for the Greeks a quite definite sense and weight, and is *not* to be transferred to today's investigations, because a wholly other sense of being-there lies at the base. If one needs it often, one has to justify oneself about what one calls being. The being-characters bear in themselves the *Da*-moment of *peras*. Presentness of a being in its finishedness determines a being in its *Da*, i.e. characterizes it simply. This sense of being is not found somewhere by the Greeks but grown from a quite definite experience of being, insofar as the human lives in a world and insofar as the world is over-vaulted by the *ouranos*, "heaven," insofar as the world *is* the *ouranos*, closed in itself and finished in itself. Being is interpreted by the Greeks *from being-there* — the only possible way. A definite experience of the world is the thread for the explication of being among the Greeks.

You see that what at the outset of our consideration we met as a technical affair of thinking and thought-cleanliness reveals itself as *horismos*. The *horismos* is a *logos*, a definite being-in-the-world that meets the being-there world in its proper *Da*-character, addresses it in its proper being. We have a concrete referral to where the proper rootedness of the concept is to be sought. Conceptuality is no arbitrary matter but an affair of *Dasein* in the decisive sense, insofar as it has resolved to speak radically with the world, i.e. to question and research. The *logos*, "speaking," will point out the being at itself precisely when this speaking has the character that it shows the being in its limitedness, that it delimits the being in its being. That *logos* which is *horismos* is the proper access-mode to the being; speaking as *horismos* is the proper addressing of the world. One can designate this *logos* as the proper access-mode to the being, insofar as *peras* is the ground-character of the *Da*. *Horismos* is the speaking with the being that is in the manner of presentness and limited in it, insofar as it meets it as limited.

When one later translated *ousia* by "essence" (*Wesen*) — and does so today in special measure, more or less clearly appealing back to this — one must be clear about what one understands thereby, whether in using "essence," "intuition of essence," "essence-nexuses" one means the being one wants to point out in the same sense of being as the Greeks. If not, one must show what one means by being. Insofar as this does not happen, all intuition of essence hangs in the air — which is indeed the case. Among the Greeks the being-concept did not fall from heaven but has its definite ground. When we ask after ground-concepts in their conceptuality, we see that *horismos* is an affair of *Dasein*, of being-in-the-world. Into *concrete Dasein* we must understand Aristotle's ground-concepts, and into his ground-possibilities of speaking with his world, in which *Dasein* is. That all clarification of concepts in their conceptuality goes so is demonstrated by the consideration of the concept *ousia*: what happened in that we went back to the current meaning, to fetch thence a directive for the meaning of *ousia*, *Da*, "being-there"? This return is nothing but the *listening-off* of the speaking of natural *Dasein* with its world — how the understanding-of-*Dasein*-with-itself speaks about the being-there-thing, what in this natural intelligibility "being" means. When we now set ourselves more explicitly to the task of grasping ground-concepts in their conceptuality, we are held to reach a closer understanding of what Aristotle understands by the being-there, the being of the human in the world — how he experiences *Dasein*, in what sense of being he addresses it, interprets it. Only when we are assured of this shall we have the possibility of understanding the ground-concepts in their original growth (*Urwüchsigkeit*). This closes the Erstes Kapitel and prepares the Zweites Kapitel's turn to the Aristotelian determination of the human's being.

## Chapter 2 — Aristotle's Determination of the Being of the Human as *zōē praktikē* in the Sense of a *psychēs energeia*

Aristotle fixes the human being's being as a *zōē praktikē tis tou logon echontos*: a life, and a practical one, belonging to a being that has language. The chapter's task is to make that formula concrete, and it runs in two opposed directions. Because the practical life is determined as an *energeia* of the soul, Heidegger tracks first what *energeia* means and second what *psychē* means. *Energeia* is perhaps the most fundamental character of being in Aristotle's doctrine; it contains the root *ergon*, work. So the analysis descends from *energeia* to the *ergon* of the human — the proper accomplishment in which a human lives as human — and reads off from that *ergon* the human's manner of being, since every *ergon* has its determinate limit (*peras*). What makes up that limit is the *agathon* — and Heidegger flags at once: not value. From the *agathon* as *peras* the analysis is led to the distinguished limit of a being whose being is *kinēsis*; such a limit is *telos*; and the *telos* of human life is *eudaimonia*, the ground-possibility a living thing carries within itself.

The opening prose already lays down the ontological grammar that governs the whole chapter. Living is, first, a mode of being marked by being-in-a-world; second, a being for which, in its being, that very being is at issue — a being that is concerned about its being. The being of life is somehow placed into its *ergon* as into a *telos*. Within the concrete possibilities among which each concrete existence decides, Aristotle seeks ground-possibilities; the ultimate one, in which *Dasein* is authentically, Heidegger names *Existenz*. For the Greek, the radical ground-possibility is the *bios theōrētikos*, the life that tarries in pure contemplation. Heidegger thus reads the ethical hierarchy as an ontology of *Dasein*'s possibilities, and marks the *bios theōrētikos* as the extreme case of authentic existence — a claim he will complicate but not retract.

The second direction runs ontologically counter to the first, clarifying the *psychē*. The soul is an *ousia* whose basic moments are *krinein* and *kinein* — discriminating-and-determining and moving-oneself in the world, dealing-with-the-world. These ground the further concrete distinction of being-in-the-world, and eventually the possibility of *hermēneuein*, interpreting. Hearing (*akouein*), the correlate of speaking, is the fundamental mode of *vernehmen* (taking-in) and the proper possibility of *aisthēsis*. In hearing I am in communication with others, since to be human is to speak. Heidegger notes the oddity that Aristotle stresses *hearing*, when for the Greeks the ground-possibility as existence otherwise lies in *theōrein*, in seeing (*horan*) — a tension he defers. In both directions the course pursues the disclosure of the structure of the human being's being, which in Aristotle only comes explicitly to completion because it was already alive in the history of Greek self-interpretation. Aristotle's sole tendency is to say what is *endoxon* — what lies in the natural being of *Dasein* itself, what is self-evident — and precisely that is often hardest to say.

### § 9. The Being of the Human as *psychē*: Being-Speaking (*logon echein*) and Being-With-One-Another (*koinōnia*) (Pol. A 2, Rhet. A 6 and 11)

The section opens by demanding clarity about *legein*, speaking, insofar as it makes up the human's proper being. Heidegger has no settled account of speech yet, and the whole section is the acquisition of one.

#### a) The Determination of the Human as *zōon logon echon*. The Task of Setting Off *logos* Against *phōnē*

The passage in view is *Politics* A 2. There the determination of the human as *zōon logon echon* serves a definite purpose: proving that the *polis* is a mode of human being that exists *phusei*, by nature. Heidegger blocks the modern misreading immediately: *phusis* is not "nature" opposed to "culture," a reading that then lets one polemicize against Aristotle. A *phusei on* is a being that is what it is from itself, on the ground of its own possibilities. So the possibility of being-in-the-*polis* lies in the human's own being — and in being-in-the-*polis* Aristotle sees the human's proper life. To show this, he shows that the human's being is *logon echein*. And enclosed in that determination is a peculiar, fundamental mode of the human's being: being-with-one-another, *koinōnia*. The being that speaks with the world is one that is in being-with-others. The move that matters: Aristotle does not add sociality to a self-sufficient rational animal; the having of *logos* already carries being-with within it.

Heidegger then renders the core *Politics* passage and glosses it. *Phōnē*, vocal utterance, is a *sēmeion*, an indication, of the pleasant (*hēdy*) and the distressing (*lypēron*) — of what lifts and what depresses *Dasein* — and animals have it too. But *logos* is *epi tō dēloun*, is set toward *making manifest*: not merely pointing but bringing what is pointed to itself into speech. It makes manifest the advantageous and the harmful, and thereby too the fitting and unfitting, good and bad. This is the human's *idion*, its own: to have *aisthēsis* — to take in — good and bad, just and unjust. And the *koinōnia* of these builds household and *polis*. What becomes visible in *logon echon*, then, is a fundamental character of human *Dasein*: being-with-one-another — not in the sense of being set side by side, but in the sense of speaking-with-one-another in the modes of communicating, refuting, contesting.

Heidegger sets up the double consideration the section will pursue. In both *phōnē* and *logos* a determinateness of being-in-the-world shows itself, a definite way the world comes to meet life: in the first case as *hēdy* and *lypēron*, in the second as advantageous and harmful (*sympheron*, *blaberon*). This yields a fundamental point about world. In natural *Dasein* the world is not a state of affairs I take cognizance of, not a reality; the world is mostly there in the manner of the beneficial-and-detrimental, of what lifts and depresses *Dasein*. These characters of access are present once in utterance, once in speech — and both are ways in which living beings *are with one another*, ways in which *koinōnia* constitutes itself. The world that is there for several who live together Heidegger names the *Umwelt*, the surrounding world in which I first and mostly go about my dealings.

He then presses the character of *hēdy* itself, borrowing the definition of *hēdonē* from *Rhetoric* A 11: pleasure is a *katastasis athroa* of the being of the living thing, an all-at-once transposing of it *eis tēn hyparchousan phusin*, into the possibility actually available to that *Dasein*, such that this is taken in. Being-pleased is a specific lightness of being-in-the-world; *lypē* is its opposite. The load-bearing point is that the *hēdy* is a *poiētikon* — it can *produce* a *diathesis*, a determinate way of finding-oneself-disposed. World in the mode of the pleasant is not a neutral object registered; it is what can bring the living thing into a disposition. Heidegger thus reads *hēdonē* as an ontological structure of attunement, not a psychological feeling-state — the seed of what *Being and Time* will develop as *Befindlichkeit*.

#### b) The *logos* of the Human and the *phōnē* of the Animal as Peculiar Modes of Being-in-the-World and Being-With-One-Another

Heidegger restates the strategy. The search is for the *milieu of conceptuality*, which has led back to the human's being, characterized as a life that speaks. He must secure the phenomenon of speaking to see which determinations of the human's being are contained in *logos*. Aristotle wants to show that the *polis* is not brought *to* the human from outside but is the possibility of being — *phusei* — already sketched out in the human's own being; the *polis* springs from a definite being-with-one-another, which in turn is grounded in a having-something-in-common, specifically a *koinōnia* of the advantageous and the good. The *koinōnia* that forms the household is possible only on the ground of *legein* — on the basic fact that the human's being is speaking-with-the-world: speaking-oneself-out, speaking with others.

The decisive thesis: speaking is not first a process to which other humans are later added so that it *becomes* speaking-with-others. Speaking is in itself, as such, a speaking-oneself-out, a speaking-with-one-another with other speakers, and *therefore* the ontological foundation of *koinōnia*. Heidegger corrects the natural picture in which the social is superadded to a private mental act. To make this intelligible he turns to Aristotle's comparison of the *zōon logon echon* with a *zōon* that has only *phōnē*, and to the claim that even animals living by *phōnē* have a being fundamentally determined as being-with-one-another — that animals too are in a sense *zōa politika*, the human being only *mallon*, more so, than, say, the bee. The delimitation against animal being is meant to sharpen the peculiar being determined by *logos*.

**α) Orientation Concerning the Phenomena Underlying the Distinction of *logos* Against *phōnē*.** Both cases put a living being under observation, life as being-in-a-world. World is there for this being-in-it not now and then but constantly; the only question is how this being-there of world is primarily determined. World is there in life in such a way that it always somehow *concerns* the living thing — *the world I find myself in bears on me*. This bearing-on, this being-concerned by the world one is in, Heidegger calls a definite mode of the world's *coming-to-meet* (*Begegnen*) in life. He then makes the sharp move: the character of coming-to-meet is being-concerned, but mostly much comes to meet me that does *not* concern me; in daily life the world is largely there as without import for me — *Belanglosigkeit*, inconsequentiality, is itself a character of the surrounding world's being-there. And it is a specific character of concern, not its absence: "that's none of my business" does not deny the thing is there; it concedes the thing is there while placing it out of import. This is the specific character of everydayness. Precisely in declaring something inconsequential, *Dasein* interprets the world as something that *could* concern it — so the concerning-character is the more original.

How far the world is there in a living thing hangs on how far that life is closed off in itself or, conversely, awakened, its being-in-the-world uncovered, bearing the character of the disclosed *Da*. There are degrees and stages. Aristotle sees exactly this when he says the animal's being has come only so far as to *have aisthēsis of the painful and the pleasant*. *Aisthēsis* here is emphatically not "sensation" but simply the *taking-in* of world, the manner of having-it-there. This disclosedness (*Erschlossenheit*), and its manner of being formed and made known, is in animals characterized by *phōnē*, in humans by *logos*; and for Aristotle the disclosedness of the being of world has its proper ground-possibility in *logos*, in the sense that in *logos* the living-thing-in-a-world appropriates world, has it there, and in that having-there authentically is and moves. Heidegger sets two tasks: to see how, in *phōnē*-life, world comes to meet the animal, and how, in *logos*, world is there for the human.

**β) The Coming-to-Meet Characters of the Animal's World: *hēdy* and *lypēron*. *phōnē* as Indicating, Luring, and Warning.** *Hēdy* and *lypēron* are *poiētika hēdonēs kai lypēs* — what can *form* pleasure and pain. What can form the pleasant need not be directly present: it can announce itself, and the *lypēron* can *threaten*. This "can" further determines the being-there of world. The *hēdy* comes to meet in the direction of a *diathesis*, forming a determinate disposition, *eis tēn hyparchousan phusin* — toward the disposition in which the being is at its ownmost, by itself, at what belongs to the animal according to its being; *Dasein* is lifted, light, is authentically itself.

Heidegger insists on a doubling in *katastasis*: the animal that the *hēdy* meets is *already* in the mode of finding-itself-disposed in the world, so the forming of a disposition by the *hēdy* means that a finding-oneself already there is transposed into a new disposition. Hence *katastasis* means both the transposing into a disposition and that disposition itself. He grounds the double translation in a structure of life as such: every mode of life is a finding-oneself in the manner of bringing-oneself-into-a-disposition-and-being-therein. I first find myself, in the proper sense, *through* bringing-myself-there: I come into joy only by rejoicing — however much that is delightful stands around me, only my rejoicing wins the actual joyfulness. The *hēdy* met by the animal is always something in the animal's *Umwelt* — a good feeding-place, not a symphony. This being is *indicated*: the animal gives a *sēmeion*. But the cry files no report about the presence of something pleasant out there in nature; the indicating is in itself a *luring* or a *warning*, and luring and warning are in themselves a self-addressing-to. Luring brings another animal into the same disposition; warning drives it out. Both have being-with-one-another lying at their base: already in luring and warning the animal shows itself to be with another. Being-with-one-another becomes manifest precisely in the animal's specific being-character as *phōnē*. Animals do not get so far as to *state* that something is there as such; they only indicate it within the circle of their animal dealings — and in indicating the world as *hēdy* the animal at once makes known its own being in it.

**γ) The Coming-to-Meet Characters of the Human's World: *sympheron*, *blaberon*, and *agathon*. *logos* as Speaking-Oneself-Out with Others About the Advantageous Toward the End of Concern.** Heidegger carries the doubling — indication of world that is at once disclosure of being-in-it — over into *logos*. Before the analysis he clears away a prejudice we are more inclined than ever to import: that in *phōnē* and *logos* reality is grasped under a certain "aspect" relative to a "subject," so that world is there only in a subjective aspect, not itself. This subject/object orientation must be dropped in principle — not merely because those concepts do not occur in Greek philosophy, but because the orientation is *senseless* here, since what is at issue is not a manner of *apprehending* world but *being in it*. Nor may one suppose a world-in-itself from which animal and human each take a slice, each seeing it under its own aspect; it is even wrong to speak of "the world of the animal" and "the world of the human" as apprehension-modes. The animal and the human are in their world because world comes-to-meet into a determinate disposition of the living thing. Apprehending world at all presupposes being-in-world; grasping is one possibility of being-in-it. With the subject/object split one never reaches the phenomenon of being-in-the-world at all. Heidegger's ban is doctrinal, and everything downstream in the course rests on it.

Now to *logos* proper. The human's *idion* is *to monon agathou kai kakou kai dikaiou kai adikou kai tōn allōn aisthēsin echein* — to live, as human, in the taken-in-ness of good and bad, fitting and unfitting, of *sympheron* and *blaberon*. Heidegger unfolds the *sympheronta* by three marks Aristotle gives: they are *ta pros to telos*, what is of themselves toward the end; *kata tas praxeis*, within the circle of view proper to *praxis*; and *skopos prokeitai tō symbouleuonti*, that on which one looks lies before the one who deliberates. Taking these in order: the *sympheron*, the advantageous, is a being whose very advantageousness *is* its reference-toward-something; that toward which it refers is the *telos*. *Praxis* is concern, and as such means bringing-something-to-its-end; concern has its end in itself, the end toward which it goes as concern. So the *sympheron* refers to the end of a concern, contributes to bringing something to completion. And the *sympheron* is *skopos*: deliberating (*symbouleuesthai*) Aristotle characterizes (*Eth. Nic.* Z 10) as *zētein ti kai logizesthai*, a seeking-of-something in the manner of reckoning — where *logizesthai* means *bringing to speech* what one looks toward in deliberating.

The account of *logizesthai* is the heart of the section. Deliberation runs in the structure *if–then*: if such-and-such is the end of a concern, then this-and-that must be undertaken, brought to speech. Its mode of enactment is the *syllogismos* — *logoi* clasped into one another. What gets brought to speech is *to ōphelimon* (= *to sympheron*), and specifically *kata to ōphelimon kai hou dei kai hōs kai hote*: the advantageous in view of what is needed for bringing a concern to its end, and *how* and *when*. In this bringing-to-speech of the *sympheron* the world, as it concretely is there, is first brought properly into the *Da*. The now-and-here of the human's being becomes explicit in a determinate deliberation; through it the human stands — in modern terms — in the concrete situation, in the proper *kairos*. Heidegger's example: I want to give my friend a joy — that is the *telos*, anticipated in *proairesis*; the deliberation then works out *how*, arriving at "I will give him a book," and my *Dasein* orients itself in this *proairesis*, so that I go to *this* bookseller to get the book quickly, i.e., so the concern reaches its end. Deliberation does not make the bookshop a bookshop; the world is already there for the being who is in it *praxis meta logou* in the character of the *sympheron*. The stick I take, the hat I put on, are *sympheronta*; the stick is not primarily a piece of wood but *the stick*. The world holds explicitly to its primary character of "as beneficial-to…" because *legein* in its primary manner addresses world *as* something: *legein ti kata tinos*, saying-something-of-something, bringing it into the *Da as* this or that, in the character of the *as*. This is the primary function of *logos*, and it is why speaking in the human's world is *dēloun to sympheron*, making the advantageous manifest.

This deliberating speaking is *symbouleuesthai*, bringing-to-speech-with-oneself — but taking-counsel-with-oneself is only a definite possibility of a far more original counsel *with others*. Bringing-to-speech is speaking-oneself-out, speaking-with-another about something, talking it through. Speech is a pointing-out self-expression *toward* — not a stating but a *talking-through* of the *sympheron*. In this function *logos* has the character of a definite *communicating* (*Mitteilen*): I have, with the other, and the other with me, insofar as we talk something through, the world there — *koinōnia* of world. Speech is in itself communicating, and as communication nothing other than *koinōnia*. Heidegger concedes the reader may feel a leap here — why should speaking be speaking-with-others? — and answers that the Greeks saw *logos* originally, whereas we have a primitive or no conception of language; the concrete proof of that originality is the whole *Rhetoric*. Speaking is deliberating speech about the advantageous, speaking-with-one-another; *logos* is the human's mode of being in its world such that this being is in itself being-with-another. And this *koinōnia* is determined not only by *logos* itself but by *logos* being a deliberating within the circumspective view of concern: concern is *meta logou*, where *meta* means "in the midst of" — *logos* belongs to concern, concern is in itself a speaking, a talking-through.

Heidegger has so far suppressed the *agathon*, though Aristotle finally calls the *sympheron* an *agathon*, and now supplies it from *Rhetoric* A 6. *Agathon* is: *auto heautou heneka haireton*, graspable at itself for its own sake — the *agathon* as *hou heneka*, the for-the-sake-of-which; *kai hou heneka allo*, so that the reference now runs in the reverse direction, from *telos* to *sympheron* (only because the *agathon* primarily has the character of end can it be a for-the-sake-of-which, and indeed a for-the-sake-of-another); *hou ephietai panta*, that toward which all holds itself, toward which all is under way; and *hou parontos*, such that when it is present the concerned one *eu diakeitai*, is disposed *eu*, well — the *eu* being a definite how of finding-oneself that is formed once the thing to be taken care of is settled, and dependent on the *manner* of that taking-care. All these run together in this: the *agathon* is primarily end, *telos*, more precisely *peras* — a fundamental determination of being already met earlier.

#### c) The *Man* as the How of the Everydayness of Being-With-One-Another. The Equiprimordiality of Being-With-One-Another and Being-Speaking

The new being-character is being-with-one-another, and it shows itself in the concrete structure of *logos* itself as it comes alive in everydayness — the speaking that is the enactment-mode of deliberating, of taking-counsel-with-oneself, of concern. As deliberating, a going-about-in-the-world is enacted, in the world that is there in the character of *agathon* or *sympheron*; and it is *logos* that points out the beneficial-as-such and, conversely, the *hou heneka*: *legein ti kata tinos*, world had-there in the character of the *as*, placed into a definite regard. Hence Aristotle can also say *aisthēsin echein tou agathou*. This sight of concern Heidegger names *Umsicht*, circumspection: in deliberating I look around me. And what is there in that circumspection is pointed out by *logos*, which is *apophainesthai*; the "as such-and-such" characters are explicitly brought into the *Da*. So *logos* here enacts its ground-function, *epi tō dēloun*, making world manifest — and this making-manifest, as enacted through speech, is a *communicating*, a making-manifest-to-another, the manner of having-world-there-explicitly-with-one-another, a ground-determination of the human's being in the world.

Heidegger then sharpens the determination decisively. Being-with-one-another does *not* state a fact — that the human is not alone but several are together. It states a *how* of being: the human *is* in the mode of being-with-one-another. So the most primary self-statement I make as a living human in my world — "I am" — is strictly false. One must say: "*I am one*" (*ich bin man*). "One" undertakes this and that; "one" sees things thus and so. This *Man* is the proper *how* of everydayness, of average concrete being-with-one-another, and its proper bearer is *language*: the *Man* has its dominion in language. Heidegger adds, importantly, that the *Man* is at the same time the possibility out of which an *authentic* being-with-one-another can grow — the *Man* is not merely fallen. He then connects the *Man* forward to *theōrein*: when later the course treats looking-at the world, the *eidos*, the "look" of world *as one usually sees it*, a so-called universality and average validity lies in the *eidos* — and *here*, in the *Man*, is the root of the determination of the universal that one so gladly takes as the ground-determination of the Greek concept of science. Heidegger thus derives the Greek "universal" from the anonymity of average understanding, an ontological genealogy of the *katholou*.

The section closes on equiprimordiality. Being-with-one-another is equiprimordial with being-speaking; it would be wholly wrong to *deduce* one statement from the other. The phenomenon of the human's *Dasein* has, equiprimordially, being-speaking and being-with-one-another, and these characters must, if they are to hit the phenomenon, be held together as a unity — the methodological demand that governs the rest of the course.


### § 10. The Being of the Human as *energeia*: The *agathon* (Eth. Nic. A 1–4)

Heidegger recalls why the human's being interests the course at all: earlier it was settled that *conceptuality* points back into the human's *Dasein*. To grasp Greek conceptuality we must make the *Dasein* in its Greek, Aristotelian interpretation intelligible. Pursuing the unfolding of the human's *Dasein*, the course has met several being-determinations and found the new one, being-with-one-another; it will now press on until it meets the proper being-character, the *peras*. Since the human's being is determined as concern, every concern has a *telos*; since the *telos* of every *praxis*, as *peras*, is the *agathon*, the *agathon* is the human's proper being-character — a determination of the human's being in the world, to be traced back to the *peras*, the proper being-character itself.

The *agathon* is investigated by four questions Heidegger poses in Aristotle's own order. Where is the *agathon* as such explicitly visible — in what mode of being-related-to-world is it explicitly there? Where do we find the *agathon* of *praxis*, the *anthrōpinon agathon*, the good for the being determined as *zōon politikon*? What are the general determinations of the *agathon* as such? And which being, which possibility of being of the human, *satisfies* the structure of the *agathon* thus set out? Before answering, Heidegger inventories what the course has won about the human's being: *zōē*, being-in-a-world; this being-in-a-world characterized by *logos*; this speaking itself the enactment-mode of a *concern*, going-about-in-the-world concernfully, so that being-in-a-world is equiprimordially concern; this concern always having an end fixed in advance, toward which it deliberates the beneficial, and in a definite anticipation having that on which it is bent — *aisthēsin echei*, concern characterized as circumspection, out of which grows in everydayness the possibility of mere looking-at, of *theōrein*; and this being as in itself being-with-one-another, being-in-the-*polis* taken explicitly. This is the structure to be held fast.

#### a) The Explicitness of the *agathon*

**α) The Explicitness of the *agathon* as Such in *technē*.** The *Nicomachean Ethics* opens: every *technē* and every *methodos*, likewise every *praxis* and *proairesis*, seems to be after some good. *Technē* is knowing-one's-way-about in some concern (the cobbler understands how a shoe is made); *methodos* is a going-after a matter, being under way after something; *praxis* and *proairesis* the concernful taking-up of something as to-be-completed. All these seem to be *after* (*ephiesthai*) a good, and this being-after belongs to their being itself: in each, as knowing-one's-way and concern, an *agathon* is explicitly present. Concern is not something else that then also, on occasion, is a being-after. The four — *technē*, *praxis*, *methodos*, *proairesis* — return later in the pairings *technē*/*praxis* and *proairesis*/*gnōsis*: doublings that concern being-in-the-world in the manner of concern that is circumspective, that sees something, that knows its way in what it is about. *Technē* — knowing-one's-way in the given concern — is the mode of being-in-the-world in which the *agathon*, and thereby the *telos*, becomes explicitly visible. The first question is answered.

**β) The Explicitness of the *anthrōpinon agathon* in the *politikē*.** It follows that, to find the *agathon* of the human as *zōon politikon*, we must make present the knowing-one's-way proper to a being so determined — the knowing-one's-way that makes the human's being *as being-with-one-another* explicit in its *telos*, and more precisely makes this being-with-one-another explicit *as* this concrete being in its *polis*. That *technē* is the *politikē*. Heidegger draws the sharp consequence: the *politikē*, this knowing-one's-way in the human's being as being-with-one-another, is where we learn about being-*telos*; and the human's self-comportment, the "holding-oneself" in the world, the "bearing," is *ēthos*. So *politikē* as the knowing-one's-way about the human's being in its authenticity — *hē peri ta ēthē politikē* — *is* ethics. To make ethics a *part* of politics is a misunderstanding; Aristotle expressly says this inquiry is one that moves toward forming the knowing-one's-way about the human's being in its authenticity, and insofar as it is *politikē*, a ground-determination of being lies enclosed in all considerations of the *agathon*.

#### b) The Ground-Determinations of the *agathon*

We ask now after the determinations of the *agathon* as such. We know: *agathon* is *telos*. So the question will presumably be after the character of being-end, of finishedness, of *teleiotēs* — how far the *agathon* is *teleion*, makes up being-finished. The last question is which being of the human satisfies the *teleion akrotaton*. The *agathon* is nothing objectively buzzing around, but a *how* of *Dasein* itself. Heidegger re-lists the four stations: where the *agathon* becomes visible at all; where correspondingly the *anthrōpinon agathon* is found; the general determinations; and what satisfies the *agathon* so characterized — what makes up the human's *Dasein* in its finishedness. On the first: the *agathon* is met in a knowing-one's-way about something, *technē* belonging to the very sense of concern. "I take care of something" already says that I know my way within the circle of what I set myself, that the *what* of the concern stands in a sight, is explicitly there, that I know my way in the beneficial.

**a) Manifoldness and Guidance-Nexus of the *telē* and the Necessity of a *telos di' auto*.** Describing the human's *Dasein* placed into this field of view, one can make out that a manifold of concerns is given — not a heap but a *multiplicity with a nexus* determined by the character of being-with-one-another; and correspondingly a manifold of *telē*, of where concern comes to its end. Among the *telē* Aristotle makes fundamental distinctions: some *telē* are *energeiai*, others are, beyond the concernings themselves, *erga*, works. *Energeia* is the distinguished, perhaps the fundamental character of being, a *how* of being in an eminent sense — being-at-work itself; were "actuality" (*Wirklichkeit*) not so worn out it would be an excellent rendering. Some *telē* are *energeiai*, the how of the concern; others are *par' autas*, alongside the concern, and indeed *erga*, works — *telē* that fall off from a concern, mostly of the beneficial character (the shoe falls off the cobbling). The *para* marks that such a *telos* is something self-standing for itself. Against this, a walk is at its *telos* precisely in the walking — not in reaching a place but in *being out in the air*, in *having walked*; the *telos* lies in the *praxis*. Two modes of concern are distinguished by the being-character of what makes up the *telos*.

Since there is a manifold of concerns and of *technai* and *epistēmai*, there is a manifold of *telē*: health for medicine, ship for shipbuilding, victory for generalship, wealth for household-management. But the concrete aspect of the human's being-with-one-another shows a *guidance* among these concerns: one leads the others. Horse-care for military service is authoritative for the saddler, who is authoritative for the tanner; *hippikē* is guided by the conduct of war, *stratēgikē*, so that generalship is in a certain respect authoritative even for tanning. *Stratēgikē* is thus a *dynamis* that reaches guidingly through a whole manifold of concerns, and it is subordinated to a characteristic interest in the human's being-with-one-another, in the *polis*. A guidance-nexus obtains: a whole manifold of concerns that are *di' heteron*, for the sake of another. Aristotle then argues there must be *a* concern, or a *telos*, that is *di' auto*: it is impossible that in the whole circle of possible concerns we grasp "one for the sake of another" without end, for so one goes to the unlimited and wins no *peras*, so that *orexis*, being-out-after, becomes *kenē kai mataia*, empty and vain. *Peras* determines the being-there of the concerned; the enactment of concern is possible only because what is taken care of is there, because concern does not grasp into the void, because concern has the character of *peras*. In this sense of being it is meant in advance: *Dasein is being-limited*. So the manifold of concerns that makes up the human's being-with-one-another must have a *peras*, and since the concerns stand in a guidance-nexus, that *peras* is constituted by a *telos di' auto*, a *telos* taken care of for its own sake. From this general consideration: the *anthrōpinon agathon* is what, in contemplating the human's *Dasein*, is there as the *telos di' auto*, that which *periechoi an ta tōn allōn* — would embrace and enclose the others. The consideration gives, as yet, no content: only that from the being-structure of being-with-one-another it follows that a *telos di' auto* must be, and it is necessarily the theme of *politikē*.

**β) The *bioi* as *telē di' auta*. The Criteria for the *telos di' auto*: *oikeion*, *dysaphaireton*, *teleion*, and *autarkes*.** This seemingly formal consideration keeps its eye on concrete *Dasein* as being-with-one-another. The setting-out of the *agathon*'s determinations, and of what satisfies it, takes its orientation from concrete experience — and not only present *Dasein* but the *opinions* present *Dasein* has of itself about what the *agathon* is. Aristotle stands within this: concrete *Dasein* does not first get an interpretation *through* him; to *Dasein* itself belongs a self-interpretation it always already carries in some measure. The intelligibility in which *Dasein* moves, the *Man*, is grounded ultimately in *doxa*, in what one on average opines about things and oneself. This self-evident opinion is the first source from which Aristotle orients himself. Hence his method: he examines the *logoi* *ek [tōn kata ton bion praxeōn] kai peri toutōn* — he draws what he makes out about *Dasein* from how *Dasein* speaks about itself, in view of the *conduct of life*. *Bios* is a new concept of "life," not the same as *zōē*: modern biology precisely does not mean Greek *bios*. *Bios* is the conduct, the way, the specific temporality of a life from birth to death, "course of life," so that *bios* also means "biography" — the *how* of a *zōē* is its *bios*, the history of a life. What life makes out about itself is drawn from the concerns in the very conduct of life, and this conception is at once *peri tōn praxeōn*, an interpretation of the concerns from which it is drawn. Aristotle lays out three *bioi*: the *bios apolaustikos*, life in enjoyment; the *bios politikos*, the way of experiencing life that goes up in concern within concrete *Dasein*; the *bios theōrētikos*, the way characterized by contemplation.

From these Aristotle sets out various *telē*, shows they are pursued *di' auto*, and asks critically whether they satisfy the sense of the *di' auto* as the *telos* of being-with-one-another. The *kritērion* must become visible. The *telos* must be: *oikeion* and *dysaphaireton* — "at home" in *Dasein* itself, not carried in from outside, belonging to it "unsnatchably." But even *dysaphaireton* is insufficient; it must be *teleion*, what in the proper sense makes up finishedness. And it must be *autarkes*, self-sufficient — and in the interpretation of *autarkes* it becomes visible that the *telos* is one that determines a *Dasein as being-with-one-another*: it must be self-sufficient *in the determination of being-with-one-another*. The formally *di' auto* *telos* is what one commonly calls *eudaimonia*, commonly "blessedness." Aristotle says one has taken this *ouk alogōs* from the *bioi* — not so that nothing is shown, but so that something comes to light: the determination is on the right path because something factual is exhibited.

Heidegger then follows Aristotle's critique of the candidate *telē*. The *bios apolaustikos* sets the *telos* in *hēdonē*; those who decide for it "come into talk," and it has the assent of the many. The educated and practical set the *telos* in *timē*, honor: in concrete being-with-one-another what finally matters is that one stand in *repute* with others. But Aristotle answers that in *timē* the *agathon* lies not with the one bent on standing in repute but with those who *esteem* — they are the ones who have appropriated the *agathon* — whereas the honor-seeker is after *timē* to secure and convince himself that his *Dasein* is an *agathon*. So *timē* is no *oikeion agathon*; I have honor by grace of others. This is even plainer with *hēdonē*. Neither is a *dysaphaireton*. The further candidate, *aretē*, excellence, is *kata toutous* — but one can be an excellent fellow and yet sleep one's *Dasein* away, or have bad luck and nothing succeeds: two determinations, being-awake and succeeding. So if *aretē* is brought into play, it needs the further determination that *aretē* be *energeia*, a matter that shows itself in deed, that has its being in the proper, concrete being-there-in-every-situation. The good-running, *eutychia*, belongs to the authenticity of a concern; *eutychia* lies with *eudaimonia*. Aristotle includes it because the Greeks hold the full concrete sense of *Dasein* as being-in-a-world, seen in the liveliness of the enactment of concern — this ethics is no fantastic one but seeks the *anthrōpinon agathon* in its *possibility*.

What life has concretely said about itself carries its own grounding. Aristotle says (end of *Eth. Nic.* A 2): if I am sufficiently oriented about what life says of itself, no appeal to the *dioti*, the "because," is needed. Life has so spoken; Aristotle takes *Dasein*'s self-interpretation positively — the *hoti*, the "that" it has so spoken, once grasped, needs no further *dioti*. Life has appropriated and made explicit its possibilities in the three *bioi*. The treatment of the *bios theōrētikos* Aristotle expressly defers (to *Eth. Nic.* K), setting it out as the proper possibility of human existence; the other two yield two kinds of *telē*: *hēdonē* and *timē*. On *hēdonē* the treatment is short because it is at once plain that such an *agathon* draws *Dasein* away from itself into the world: in *hēdonē* *Dasein* does not come to itself; life is lived *by* the world it moves in, wholly dependent on it — its own being is not lived. On *timē*: the second setting has more for it, since in *timē* lies a distinguished possibility of being-with-one-another, of finding-oneself-among-others — standing in repute is a distinguished disposition, but one dependent on others, who can lend the repute or withhold it. The others have the *agathon* and bestow it; it does not belong to my being as such. So *timē* is no *oikeion*, no *dysaphaireton*; an *agathon* that is in the proper sense the *Dasein*'s own must be at home in that being and not snatchable.

Such an *agathon* seems to be *aretē*, the manner of *Dasein* by which we say someone is a capable fellow — the "excellence," the disposing over a wholly determinate possibility of one's own being (the flute-player's *aretē* is his disposing, in the distinguished sense, over flute-playing). But such a being can, so to speak, *sleep itself away*; one can be excellent and sleep one's life away. If disposing over one's own being-possibility is to be an *agathon*, it must be in the mode of being-awake and must *enact* the disposing, the *praxis*. Hence in the end the proper *agathon* of human *Dasein* is *eupraxia* or *euzōia*: the *eu* is no worldly outside but a *how of life itself*. From the determination that the *agathon* is a *how* of concern, a series of moments determines the *agathon* and prefigures what alone can satisfy it — among them that whoever is in *euzōia* has *eutychia*: concrete *Dasein* can so enact itself, but can also have bad luck. This *eutychia*, a further moment of the *eudaimonia* of the *ariston*, marks that *euzōia* is being-in-a-world with its conditions and possibilities, and that its inclusion shows the ethics is no fantasy but seeks the human good in its real possibility.

Aristotle concludes (chapter 4) that there can be no good *in itself*. The *agathon* is at itself always *peras* of a *praxis*, and *praxis* is now-and-here, goes to the now-and-here being, *peri ta eschata kai ta kath' hekasta* — to the utmost, the sharpened now-and-here, to the individual as such in its determinateness. Hence the idea of an *agathon katholou* is senseless; it mistakes the being-character of the *agathon* itself. Against this, to bring the *agathon kath' auto* into sharper determination, Aristotle strikes a new path and investigates the being-character of the *agathon* directly: the *agathon* is *peras* or *telos*, end in the sense of making-up-a-finishedness. He defines it (chapter 5) as *telos*, more precisely *teleion*; and to prepare that, Heidegger interprets *Metaphysics* Δ 16.

### § 11. The *teleion* (Met. Δ 16)

*Teleion*, like *on* and correspondingly *agathon*, is double in meaning. *Agathon* means, like *ousia*: a good, a being that is good; and good-being, goodness. So *teleion* means: a finished-being, and what makes up being-finished — the determinate being in which a finished thing must stand to be finished, the being-mode of the finished-being. Heidegger blocks the standard error at the outset: *teleion* and *telos* do not mean "goal" or "purpose." The primary ground-determination is *being-end*; it is expressly formulated as *tōn eschatōn ti*, having the character of "the utmost." That one translates *telos* by "purpose" or "goal" has its ground and is not baseless — but the question is whether those renderings are the *primary* ones, and whether at this level of the inquiry into being one may throw primary and derived meanings together. Purpose is the what-for; goal the toward-which. The end *can* step into the character of purpose or goal, but only because *telos* is *end* can it also, on occasion, be goal or purpose — and only in view of a definite looking-toward. Purposive activity or goal-directedness, at the level of this inquiry, is a complete misinterpretation, and leads to the picture of Aristotle as one of the primitive men as they were imagined to live in the nineteenth century.

#### a) Translation of the Chapter

Heidegger works through Δ 16 clause by clause. First: *teleion* is said of that outside which not even a single part can be found that still makes up the being in question — thus time is finished for each thing such that outside it there is no further time belonging to it. "Everything has its time": a definite delimitation of time, outside which nothing of that being is temporal; when something has *had* its time it is *teleion* (compare the analysis of time in *Physics* Δ 10–14). Second, *teleion* is said *kata aretēn kai to eu*, of what, in the circle of disposing over its own being-possibility in its genuine provenance, has no "beyond": we speak of a finished physician and a finished flute-player when, in view of how their own disposing-over-their-being is there, they fall short in nothing. And by *transferring* (*metapherontes*) this how-of-being-meant, we speak even of a finished sycophant or a finished thief — since we also call them "good," a good thief, a good sycophant — and *aretē* is a certain *teleiōsis*. Third: each thing is then *teleion*, and each *ousia* then finished, when in view of the *eidos* of its proper *aretē* nothing is lacking of the measure of its possible being-able-to-be. Fourth: those in which the *telos* is present *spoudaion*, in earnest, are called *teleia*, in view of *having* the end; and since *telos* belongs to the utmost, we transfer this too to the bad — *teleiōs apolōlenai*, *teleiōs ephtharthai*, utterly destroyed, when nothing is lacking to the ruin and it stands at the utmost. Fifth: hence *teleutē*, death, is by transference called *telos*, since both are utmost; death makes life finished. Sixth: *telos* is also the *hou heneka*, the for-the-sake-of-which as utmost.

#### b) Structuring of the Chapter

**α) The First Two Points. The Method of Transference.** *Chronos teleios* characterizes *teleion* as that beyond which nothing is there that co-constitutes the being of the being whose character is *teleion* — here *teleion* (*peras*) said first of a being understood in its mere presence. *To kat' aretēn* gives the being already in a peculiar character: as one that disposes over its ownmost being-possibility, where *teleion* then means: beyond which nothing is there that would make the being still more properly itself. For the finished flute-player there is no "beyond" (*hyperbolē*) in the sense of the possibility of his ownmost being. On this ground rests the possibility of "transferring," of *metapherein*, as in speaking of a "good thief." A "good thief" does not mean he is a good man but a finished thief: one who in his being has come to his right being-possibility, has brought that possibility to its end. Aristotle expressly names the *metapherein* and adduces the transference for a purpose. In a transference of speech we take, from the nearest and original addressing, from the original meaning of *teleion*, a meaning *along* (*meta*) and carry it over onto a new thing addressed; and in this carrying-over precisely what we carry along becomes visible — and therein becomes visible what, in the ground-meaning from which we carry, we had already meant. With the finished physician it is not moral goodness that matters but, in this *teleios*, the bringing-to-an-end. The *metapherein* makes visible what *teleion* properly means; the physician is *agathos*, and the thief too *agathos qua* thief, in another sense *kakos*. It is no accident that Aristotle conducts a whole series of analyses in this manner of transference.

**β) Recalling the Nexus of the Treatment of the *teleion*.** *Teleion* is a determination of the *agathon*, and so *aretē* too — later a fundamental determination of the being of life — has a peculiar bearing on being-finished. In disposing over something, over a determinate possibility of one's being, this being is already held in its end; I already have my proper being-possibility in grip and possession. On this phenomenon of *echein to telos* Aristotle expressly comes to speak: *teleiōsis*. The nexus is that, after discussing the several *bioi* as *agatha di' auta* and *telē*, and setting out characteristic moments of the *agathon*, Aristotle tries to clarify the character of the *agathon* as *teleion*; and this treatment of *telos* as *teleion* is preceded by the discussion with Plato (deferred). What follows for the *anthrōpinon agathon* taken as *telos*? It has bearing on the human's being, known as being-with-one-another, determined by a manifold of *praxeis* in a guidance-nexus, so that among them an *akrotaton agathon*, a highest good, is findable — an *agathon di' auto*. Such *agatha di' auta* Aristotle exhibited in the *bioi*; under the *bios politikos*, two possibilities — *timē* and *aretē*, with *aretē* a forward-pointer to what Aristotle himself sets as the *agathon*. With the discussion of the *teleion* we gain a foundation for the fundamental concept of Aristotle's doctrine of being, *entelecheia*. *Telos* is not "goal" but *eschaton*, the character of limit, the "utmost"; goal and purpose are definite modes in which *telos* is as end, but not primary — they are founded in *telos* as end, the original meaning.

**γ) Renewed Structuring of the Chapter into Eight Points.** Heidegger re-articulates Δ 16 in eight points. First: the character of *teleion* as that beyond which nothing else is there. Second: a beyond-which-nothing that, as a determinate being-possibility of a being, determines it in its being properly — that beyond the *telos* there is no further being-possibility, that a being has, as to its being-possibilities, come to its end. Third: in this being-come-to-its-end lies the possibility of transference — in "good thief" becomes visible what *teleion* properly means in "good physician." The predicate "good" carries the side-meaning of distinguished, valuable; *teleion* does not mean *this*, since we also speak of a "perfect thief," so that *teleion* names a being-character not bound to the specific meaning of *agathon* as expressing a certain property. Fourth: *teleion* has a bearing on *aretē* — since *aretē* means disposing over a determinate being-possibility, the determination of *telos* or *teleion* already lies in it; a being with an *aretē* already has, in a definite way, its end in that *aretē*. *Aretē* is a being directed in itself toward the *telos*, a being-able-to-dispose that need not have explicitly arrived at its *telos*. Fifth: the further determination is prefigured in *aretē* as *teleiōsis*, insofar as there is a being that in the proper sense *has* its *telos*, such that its *telos* is *present* in it — *hyparchei telos* — that it has its end in a first way. Here *echein*, having, is meant in an eminent sense. One speaks of "having" in two senses: (a) as occurring at something, something is so-and-so constituted, has this or that determinateness — "the table has cracks," what is had is something present-at the being; (b) "having" can mean the direct concern with something in its explicitness, having the had present, having to do with it. There are transitions: "the tree has blossoms." This having-nexus is not simply identical with the being-nexus of "the table has cracks," and "the human has boredom, toothache" is again another having, since this had and having is *conscious*: "the human has the thought of running away." The latter having is meant when Aristotle speaks of *hyparchein telos spoudaion*: the *telos* is there "in earnest," had in earnest — where *spoudaios* does not mean someone who makes a grim face, but the mode of *Dasein* in which I have a matter in earnest, do not play with it: being *at* a matter, staking everything on one card in having-grasped-it. The matter I have in earnest need not be extraordinary; the less extraordinary, the less possibility of deception about the earnestness. *Spoudaion* is a determination of the *how*: earnest must be made with a possibility of one's own being.

Sixth: in the further determination it first becomes clear in what sense *teleion* is a *being-character*. It is spoken of *teleiōs ephtharthai*; and the *teleutē*, death, is called *telos*. What becomes visible in this transference? We say of a human: "he is quite finished, done for, used up." Being-finished is *being-away-from-Dasein*. What is the sense of the transference when *teleutē* is called *telos*? With death, life is at an end; death makes life finished by taking being away from the *Da*, life vanishes. In this transference *teleion*, *telos* shows itself as a *Dasein*-character, insofar as *to telos*, *teleion*, designates the *Dasein* we designate as no-longer-*Dasein*, as being-away. Being-away is a distinguished mode of *Dasein*. Precisely in the transference of *telos* and *teleion* onto death, the distinguished function of *teleion* shows itself — the character of being *Dasein* in the distinguished possibility of vanishing. Heidegger reads death here as a *mode of Dasein*, not the mere cessation of an organism, and grounds it in the thesis that not-being can only be interpreted from a positively laid-out *Da*: one cannot make the being of a being intelligible by saying it also is when it is not grasped; that is a merely negative determination. Being-away is the extremest mode of *Dasein*, so the interpretation of being is thrown back onto the laying-out of the *Da*. Seventh: the *teleion* is ascribed to the *hou heneka*, the *hou charin*, and this is *teleion* when it is *eschaton*. The *hou heneka* is the being that stands in a willing, that I willingly have to do with, that on which I am somehow out in *orexis*, characterized as end, last, ultimately *telos*; it gets *telos*-character from *eschaton*. The *hou heneka* is not the goal I look toward — that is *skopos*. Eighth: in the summary Aristotle divides the meanings into two groups — those in which *telos* is properly said of something, and those that have their meaning as *teleion* by being with regard to something named *teleion* in the first way. *Teleion* is thus brought onto the schema of the categories, which indicates that, insofar as *teleion* admits such a division, it is in itself a fundamental being-character — a distinguished character of being in the sense of *Dasein*.

#### c) The *teleion* as Limit in the Sense of the Proper *Da* of a Being

Summarizing: *telos* has the determination of *limit*. This limit-character is to be taken as: that beyond which nothing further is there, end, that at which something stops. But here caution is needed. A path through a meadow stops at a garden fence. The fence is *not* the *teleion*. The path's being-a-path is not determined by the fence; what the path stops *at* is itself a being that is in the same manner as what stops at it. Presumably *teleion* is *not* a being, a piece of the being whose end it makes up, but a *being*, a mode of being itself. *Teleion* is limit *not* as one being to another whose limit it is. In this sense the thief is finished, the limit *not* lying outside him: the how of his being, the stealing itself, has come to its determinate possibility. He is not a good thief because he stumbles onto a large sum of money. *Teleion* is a determination of the *being* of the being, not some property like white or black. The beyond-which-nothing has the character of limit in the sense of a determination of *being*.

This limit-character becomes plain in the further transference: death, a mode of *Dasein*, the no-longer-being-*Dasein*, being-away, *apousia*. The no-longer-there is a character of the *Da*, insofar as *teleutē* is addressed as *telos* — though by a transference. That is, in addressing death as *telos*, the proper meaning of *telos* and *teleion* has in a sense been lost, insofar as *telos* means an end such that this end does *not* simply let the thing vanish, does not take it from the *Da*, but *holds it in the Da*, determines it in its proper *Da*. *Telos* originally means: to be at an end *such that this end makes up the proper Da*, determines a being in its presentness, properly. Because that is the ground-meaning of *telos*, one can by transference speak of *telos* in the sense of death. Here lies a fundamental nexus: not-being or not-being-there can be interpreted only if one has properly, positively laid out *Dasein* itself. Being-away is the extremest mode of *Dasein*, so the interpretation of being is thrown back onto the laying-out of the *Da*. *Telos*, *teleion* has the character of limit — limit in the sense of *being*, such that this limit determines the being in its *Da*: end in the sense that this *telos* reaches *back* onto that whose end it is and determines it in its *Da* — the character of a *back-reaching embracing*. A finished violinist is, through his being-finished, in his proper being.

From here Heidegger measures what *entelecheia* fundamentally means. A being determined by *entelecheia* is one that holds itself in its proper being-possibility, so that the possibility is finished. When the being is such that it *can* have its *telos* — the *telos* standing in sight, so that it can be spoken about — the most fundamental character of the *Da* comes to expression in *entelecheia*. This determination of *telos* can become fundamental insofar as the being of a being can become *explicit for that being itself*; and this possibility of the explicitness of proper being exists for a being we characterize as *life*, being-in-a-world. Hence for Aristotle the soul is "the first *entelecheia* of a body that carries in itself the possibility of life." Here lies the ground of the extensive discussion of *teleion*: it prepares the concept of the soul, and thereby the ontological reading of *psychē* toward which the whole chapter has been building.

### § 12. Continuation of the Consideration of the *agathon* (Eth. Nic. A 5–6)

In *Eth. Nic.* A 5 Aristotle first recapitulates the course up to the confrontation with Plato: a manifold of concerns, not a heap but a guided multiplicity, and a manifold of *telē*. Since concrete looking at the human's *Dasein* shows *telē* that are *di' hetera*, it is co-given that not all can be *teleia*: not every *telos* is already *teleion*, proper end for the being that holds itself in the concern. Though individual concerns are *telē*, they are not the proper end of *Dasein*. Tools are *telē* at which a definite *praxis* ends — but a *praxis* whose *ergon* is *para*, alongside. The shoe is the *telos* in the sense that, once finished, it has *beside* the cobbler's being a *Dasein* of its own in the world. Such *telē* have in themselves the character of beneficialness. The hammer is a *telos* of the smith in that it does not occur like a stone but is that with which I can drive a nail: its usability makes up its very *Dasein*. It is *telos* but no *teleion*; it points, in itself, away from itself into another concern it makes possible. So there is in the world a manifold of *telē* that are not themselves *teleia*.

#### a) Continuation of the Discussion of the Ground-Determinations of the *agathon*. The *anthrōpinon agathon* as the *haplōs teleion*

The *anthrōpinon agathon* was said to be *di' auto* and the *ariston*, *akrotaton agathon*, the good at which it goes no further — so presumably a *teleion*. But the *bioi* yielded a *manifold* of *telē di' auta*; and if there is a manifold of *teleia*, there must among them be a *teleiotaton*, and if that, then also a *teleioteron*. This shows how the interpretation of the *agathon* aims in advance at a radical carrying-through of the idea of *telos*, *peras*: for the being-determination of the human's *Dasein* the Greek ground-determination of being is radically claimed, so that the *agathon* is *telos* in the sense that it is *haplōs teleion*, simply finished.

The reach of this formal structural consideration is not at once apparent. But precisely from here Aristotle's determination of the human's ground-possibility, *theōrein*, becomes intelligible. *Theōrein* is the ownmost possibility of *Dasein* because in it *Dasein* comes to its end in such a way that it is transposed into its ownmost possibility, its ownmost *Da* — because *theōrein* makes up the ownmost *entelecheia* of the human's being. Aristotle gives to what lay in Greek *Dasein* concretely as an existence-tendency its proper expression, making this existence intelligible out of, and grounding it in, the proper sense of being and *Dasein*. The most general determination of *teleion* — that beyond which nothing is — is to be grasped so that a being-character comes to expression: *teleion* is not being *as* being but *as* being. Shoes, tools, *hypokeimena* are *telē* only when their being-character has become explicit, when what a definite craft ends at properly *is*. The beyond-which-nothing is not negatively being-at-an-end but is taken positively, as making up the proper *Da*: the *telos* holds the being in its presentness. The sense of being is determined by this being-present.

With this clarified concept Heidegger returns to Aristotle on the *anthrōpinon agathon*. The good of human *Dasein* must be a *peras*, since every being is determined as being-limit; the question is what character the *anthrōpinon agathon* qua *telos* has. The treatment proceeds on the concrete ground of the human's *Dasein* as seen in natural experience — human *Dasein* as being-with-one-another in the *polis*, being-with-one-another in concern. Human concerns run in a guidance-nexus; the *telē* refer to one another, are *di' hetera*. This is a being-determination of the *telē*: the *telos* is not something lying present that afterward finds a use; already what the instrument-maker is out for has in itself the character of usability-toward. "Not all *telē* are *teleia*," as they come to meet in concrete *Dasein*. Only the *anthrōpinon agathon* will be *teleion* in the proper sense; it is said not to go *eis apeiron*, so that the guidedness of the *telē* of the *praxeis* does not lose itself in the infinite. Since there is a manifold of *telē kath' hauta*, the *ariston* must be the *teleiotaton* or *teleioteron* among them — what more, and more properly, has the character of end.

What belongs to a *haplōs teleion*? Aristotle first gives the *teleioteron*: *teleioteron* is the *kath' hauto* that is *diōkton tou di' heteron*, pursued and grasped rather than something that is for the sake of another. This is necessary but not sufficient. In view of the *kath' hauto*, the *teleioteron* is that which is *mēdepote di' allo* and *aei kath' hauto haireton*, a *di' auto* that constantly, always, is what it is. The *telē kath' hauta* — *hēdonē*, *timē*, *aretē* — can also, in the end and mostly, be appropriated *for the sake of eudaimonia*: we choose honor, pleasure, and excellence also for their own sake, but also for the sake of *eudaimonia*. So these *telē* can have, in the background, a further *telos* on which it really turns for the human — and in the end this *telos* is *Dasein itself*. If the *haplōs teleion* is to be something constantly and always *kath' hauto*, then for the human's *Dasein* only what belongs to that *Dasein as such* comes into question. The *aei* is not meant in the Platonic sense but related to the being of the human. The being on which it finally turns can, for *Dasein*, only be *its own being*. Here shows a fundamental determination of *Dasein*: a being in whose being, explicitly or inexplicitly, that being is at issue — so that the *haplōs teleion* is what simply makes up the being-finished of *Dasein*, the simple being-possibility of *Dasein* itself. If *Dasein* as being-in-the-world was determined from the *telē* (*hēdonē*, *timē*) as a disposition, then this being-possibility names a disposition, the mode of *Dasein* as *diagōgē*, a tarrying in a world; and this *Dasein* in the ownmost sense has its enactment-possibility in *theōrein*.

Heidegger hears this determination — that finally *Dasein*'s being is what simply makes up *Dasein* in its *Da* — echoed in Kant's determination of the human: the rational being exists as an end in itself. That is at once the ontological condition of the possibility of the categorical imperative. Insofar as the rational being is disclosed for the law in the ground-determination of respect and at the same time is such that its being is, so to speak, at its end in itself, having no further "why," this law is the last in itself: the ought is categorical, not hypothetical. For the human's being there is no *if* but an ultimate *so*. Heidegger notes the oddity that Kant takes the concept of natural law in the wider, almost Aristotelian sense: "nature" here is the mode of *Dasein*, *phusis* in Aristotle's sense; the maxim is to become not an explicit law of nature but a mode of *Dasein* simply. The reading turns Kant's autonomy into an ontological point about a being whose being is its own end.

So the human's being is what in the end makes up the *haplōs teleion* of a being, and this is what *eudaimonia* means. Aristotle gives the popular term a specifically philosophical sense by determining *eudaimonia* out of *Dasein* itself. In clarifying it as *haplōs teleion* he supplements the *teleion* with a determination showing it is the *teleion of the human's Dasein*: *autarkes*. The good that finishes the human's *Dasein* seems to be self-sufficient — but since by its own being-possibility the human is a *zōon politikon*, the self-sufficiency of the *teleion* cannot be referred to the individual, cannot hold primarily for the hermit; co-enclosed in *Dasein* itself is being-with parents, children, wife, friends, and fellow citizens. From this being-with-one-another a *horos*, a limit, must be won — for extended to friends of friends and relatives of relatives it loses itself, goes *eis apeiron*. Proper being-with-one-another loses itself if it is a wild being-with-all-humans; it is genuine when it has in itself its definite *limit*. So the *autarkes*-determination shows how from the outset the *teleion agathon* is seen, and what role being-with-one-another plays in the *teleion* as *autarkes*.

A further determination: *eudaimonia* is not *synarithmoumenē*, not a summed-up thing, no sum. If, as *autarkes* suggests, a manifold of relations makes up the being-finished of *Dasein*, it is not a matter of a sum, a how-much; the manifold must be understood not as a sum but from the being whose *teleion eudaimonia* is — from *praxis*. The *teleion* of *Dasein* is not a summative *what* one could gather (*mē synarithmoumenē*) but a *how* of the *eu*, the *euzōia* that makes up the proper *teleion* of *Dasein*. So a whole series of characters of the *agathon* stands assembled: the *oikeion* and *dysaphaireton* as ground-determinations; the being of *Dasein* itself as *haplōs teleion*, what is in the ownmost sense at home in *Dasein*; and the *autarkes*.

#### b) The *psychēs energeia kat' aretēn* as the Being-Possibility of the Human that Satisfies the Sense of the *anthrōpinon agathon*

Aristotle now asks what this *agathon* actually is — what at the human's being satisfies this sense of *anthrōpinon agathon*. One wishes a clearer account of what the *agathon* is. The guiding rule: I find the *agathon* of a being by looking at its *ergon*. Every *praxis* has an *ergon*; in the working itself the *agathon* seems findable. So for the *anthrōpinon agathon* I must look to what, at the human's *Dasein*, is the *anthrōpinon ergon* — the concern of human *Dasein* that makes up the human's being as such. Is there such an *ergon anthrōpinon* at all?

Looking at concrete human *Dasein* we see definite occupations — builder, cobbler — determinations that do not accrue to every human as human. In these the human works with hands, goes with feet, sees, grasps, particular parts of this being having their task. The question is whether, beside the cobbler's or carpenter's *ergon* and walking and so on, there is an *ergon* of the human that would be *idion* to the human as human, his own. Aristotle decides this not fantastically but by opening his eyes: the *idion* is to be seen by *bounding off* (*aphorizesthai*) everything this human life has *in common* with other living things. All that concrete presentification of human life shows as also found in other living beings is set aside. The field is being in the manner of life: living seems common even to plants, but the *idion* is sought — so the nutritive and growing life is bounded off. In taking nourishment a living thing is in a definite way in its world; this being-in-the-world traces back to bringing-to-the-world, generating and bearing (we have the expression "to come to the world"). Nutrition and growth are only definite being-possibilities of life in which the ground-possibility, *gennan*, is formed — but nothing specifically human. In the being of animals as being-in-the-world we observe *aisthēsis*: animals take in the world within definite limits, have the *Umwelt* there, have an orientation in it. But this being-oriented, this somehow-explicitly-having-it-there, is likewise not proper to the human as such. Keeping the eye concretely on the human, and with other modes of life co-seen: there remains only *praktikē tis tou logon echontos* — one mode of being-in-the-world such that it can *take care* of something in it, a concern of a being that *speaks*. The *idion ergon*, the human's proper mode, is *praxis*, determined as a mode of being-in-the-world, and speaking — *meta logou*, *kata logon*.

We know that what is at stake is the *proper* being-possibility. But concern can rest; the human can sleep his *Dasein* away. It turns on the proper mode of *Dasein*, such that the to-be-taken-care-of is itself present, the *ergon* there, the human *in* the *ergon*, *kat' energeian*: the human is determined in view of living-and-going-up in concern properly. And this *kat' energeian* admits a further determination. The human's being is determined by *aretē*, the mode of being in which the *telos* is had, *telos* as the beyond-which-nothing. So the *energeia* is *prostithemenēs tēs kat' aretēn hyperochēs*, in view of the *ergon* being taken in its ownmost being-possibility, enacting itself in *aretē*, really there. With a violinist we distinguish violinist from violinist: a bad one differs from a *spoudaios kitharistēs* who has made earnest with his being-possibility, who has really set to work the disposing over what he is.

So the human's *ergon* shows itself as *praktikē zōē*. Since the human's *telos* lies not outside himself but in himself as his being-possibility, the *anthrōpinon agathon* is *zōē* itself, "life" itself. The *ergon* is life itself, grasped as being-in-the-world *meta logou*, such that speaking goes with it. The *anthrōpinon agathon* is thus *psychēs energeia kat' aretēn*. The *psychē* is determined as making up the being of the living thing. This being-in-the-world as *energeia* is a definite possibility of concern, of *praxis*, as set-to-work, and this setting-to-work as *eu*, grasped in earnest (*spoudaios*), so that the ultimate being-possibility is grasped at its end.

Heidegger declines to pursue Aristotle's more concrete elaboration (*Eth. Nic.* A 7–12, on the nexus of the *anthrōpinon agathon* with the possibilities of *Dasein* in the *polis*), since the course's interest is to see, more sharply, the ground of conceptuality — *Dasein* itself. Following the discussion of the *agathon*, the course has reached a determination of *Dasein* one moment of which is the *psychē*, the ontological ground-determination of life, *psychē* as *praktikē energeia*. The inquiry gives itself: Aristotle himself supplies the thread in saying the knowing-one's-way that makes up the human's being as being-with-one-another must be instructed *peri psychēs*. This does not mean a politician must also be a psychologist, but that he has to do with the proper being of the living thing in its ground-structure. Psychology has nothing to do with "consciousness" and "lived experiences"; it is only the doctrine of the being of the living thing, the ontology of the being characterized by life. To be oriented *peri psychēs* is nothing other than to be clear about the proper being-determinations of life — and for the *politikos* this task has a definite limit. Aristotle carries out the closer orientation about the *psychē* on the thread of average opinions of life about itself; one such is the determination of the human as *zōon logon echon*, and in its direction the further determinations move. This closes the chapter and hands the thread to the *Rhetoric*-analysis of Chapter 3.

## Chapter 3 — The Interpretation of the Human's *Dasein* with Respect to the Ground-Possibility of Speaking-With-One-Another, on the Guiding-Thread of Rhetoric

### § 13. Being-Speaking as Being-Able-to-Hear and as the Possibility of Falling. The Double Sense of *alogon* (Eth. Nic. A 13, De an. B 4)

The prior consideration reached a preliminary end: the ground-determinations of the human's being were set out, and the being of the human's *zōē* fixed as *psychēs energeia kat' aretēn teleian*. *Energeia* is a character of the ensouled being, the being that is in the manner of being-in-a-world; *zōē* is a life that is there actively, so that this *Dasein* properly lives in concern and has its *telos* so as to bring the human's *Dasein* to its proper end. The concrete elaboration would now treat the *aretai* — and *Eth. Nic.* Books following A give the extended account. But Heidegger will not follow that; the course's interest is not the concrete elaboration of the *Dasein*-interpretation but one moment enclosed in it: that the human's being, *energeia kat' aretēn*, has the character of *speaking* — *praxis meta logou*. The stress falls on *meta logou*, because the search is for *logos*, the speaking and addressing of world in which concept and conceptuality are at home. The course seeks the *Bodenständigkeit* — the rootedness — of concept-formation in *Dasein* itself; concept-formation is no chance affair but a ground-possibility of *Dasein* insofar as it has decided for science.

The preliminary determination *zōon logon echon* already disclosed a ground-character of *Dasein* as *zōon politikon*: the human is in the manner of being-with-one-another, whose ground-possibility is speaking-with-one-another, speaking as speaking-oneself-out in speaking-about-something. *Logos* comes into function not only there but exactly where Aristotle asks after the possible *aretai*; the inquiry is articulated on the thread Aristotle himself runs through the *logon echon*. And the *logon echon* was clarified only superficially — a whole series of determinations lies enclosed in it. Aristotle determines *Dasein* as *logon echon* more precisely: in the human, *being-speaking* itself plays a fundamental role such that, in being-with-one-another, one can be the speaker, the other the hearer. *Akouein*, hearing, is the proper *aisthēsis*. Though seeing, in connection with *theōrein*, discloses world in the proper sense, it is *hearing* that matters here, because hearing is the taking-in of speech, the possibility of being-with-one-another. And the human is not only a speaker and hearer but, for himself, a being that *hears itself*: speaking, as speaking-oneself-out-about-something, is at once a speaking-to-oneself. So *logon echon* encloses further: the human has *logos* also in the manner of *hearing his own speech* — a being-possibility Heidegger characterizes as *hypakouein*, hearkening.

The demonstration Aristotle draws from concrete *Dasein*-nexuses treated in *Eth. Nic.* A 13: *paraklēsis* (encouragement), *nouthetēsis* (drawing-attention), *epitimēsis* (rebuke). All these modes of natural speaking-with-one-another carry the claim that the other not merely take note but *take up*, follow, bethink himself, repeat what is spoken, and in repeating *hear* it. So in the human's being as a concernful being lies the possibility of *hearing one's own speech*. This possibility of hearing, the *akoustikon*, lies more precisely together with the mode of being fundamentally enclosed in *praxis*: *orexis*, striving. Every concern has a tendency, is out for something, an *agathon* always there as *legomenon*, as something addressed. This being-out hears the spoken, the pre-given of what and how something is to be taken care of. So the concernful life in which speaking is co-present speaks such that it *hears itself*. This concernful hearing-of-speech is, as *orexis*, not properly speaking; it is speech only insofar as it *hears* speech. Insofar as it is not properly speech, Aristotle calls it *alogon* — which does not mean without any relation to speech but only not *kyriōs*, not primarily speech.

The *alogon* has, for the full determination of the human's being, a double sense: first, being-not-speaking in the sense of *hearing* speech; second, standing in no relation to speech at all, like the *threptikē* — nourishing, reproducing — which is unrelated to *logos*, and in a wholly fundamental sense: the function of the gastric juices has no relation to the human's speaking. *Alogon* is thus determined once with respect to the *threptikē*, and once as a being-possibility characterized by *being-able-to-hear* speech itself; proper speaking is being *logon echon kyriōs*. This is the thread for dividing the possible *aretai*. There are *aretai* oriented on proper speaking, deliberating, grasping the matter; and there are modes of being-able-to-dispose-over one's being in which *logos* is co-present but the decisive thing lies in the *grip*, in *proairesis*. The first are the *aretai dianoētikai*, the second the *aretai ēthikai*. *Dianoeisthai* is thinking-through, deliberating-through. *Ēthikos* does not mean "moral"; with the "ethical virtues" one must not cling externally to the word. *Ēthos* means the human's *bearing* — how the human is there, gives himself, comports himself in being-with-one-another, how an orator speaks and holds himself in it, how he stands to the matters he speaks of. Heidegger will later take up the *aretai dianoētikai*, because their field holds the ground-possibility of contemplation, of scientific research, the *bios theōrētikos*, and thereby the ground-possibility of human existence.

Crucially, the human as the being that speaks is *not* Aristotle's invention. Aristotle expressly gives back an *endoxon*, a *doxa* with dominion in Greek *Dasein* itself; even the distinction *logon echon*/*alogon* goes back to the *exōterikoi logoi*. Heidegger corrects the old riddle about that phrase: it does not mean Aristotle's published dialogues (a view he rejects), but — following Diels (1883) and Jaeger — the manner of speaking *outside science*, "how one talks," and what settles in such talk. Aristotle expressly appeals to it in taking the *alogon* as a ground-determination of the human. This gives the essential hint that the determination *zōon logon echon*, being so fundamental, must have a real ground: it is no accident that the Greeks in their natural self-interpretation determined the human as *zōon logon echon*.

We have no corresponding definition; the nearest would be, at most: the human is a living thing *that reads the newspaper*. That sounds odd but captures the Greek definition. When the Greeks say the human is a living thing that speaks, they do not mean physiologically that he emits certain sounds, but that the human has his proper *Dasein* in conversation and speech — *the Greeks existed in speech*. The orator is the one who has proper power over *Dasein*: *rhētorikē peithous dēmiourgos*, the ability-to-speak is the possibility in which I have proper dominion over the convictions of humans as they are with one another. Even when the Greek reads, he *hears*; it is no accident that all the texts we have from Aristotle are lectures, spoken word.

From this Heidegger draws the pivotal point about *Verfallen*, falling. If speech is the proper possibility of *Dasein* in which it plays out, concretely and mostly, then this very speaking is also the possibility into which *Dasein* gets caught — the tendency to go up in what lies nearest, in fashion, in idle talk, and let itself be led by it. This process of going up in the world, in what is customary, of *falling to* the world one lives in, became for the Greeks, through language, the *ground-danger* of their *Dasein*. The proof of this is the existence of *sophistic*. In sophistic, earnest is made with this predominant possibility of speaking: Protagoras' phrase, *ton hēttō logon kreittō poiein* — to discuss geometry with a geometer though one understands nothing of geometry, to conduct the conversation so as to overpower the other without competence in the matter. Sophistic is the proof that the Greeks had fallen to language — the "most speakable of all languages," as Nietzsche called it, who had to know what Greekness is. In the fourth century the Greeks had come wholly under the dominion of language.

One must measure what it meant to retrieve speaking out of this externalization of Greek *Dasein*, out of talk and idle talk, and bring it to where Aristotle can say: *logos* is *logos ousias*, speaking about the matter, what it is. Aristotle stood in the extremest counter-position to what lived around him. One must not imagine science fell into the Greeks' lap; the Greeks went up entirely in the external, *Dasein* was so laden with chatter that it took the whole effort of Plato and Aristotle to make earnest with the very possibility of science. The decisive thing: they drew the new existence-possibility not from outside — not, say, from India — but *out of Greek life itself*; they made earnest with the possibilities of speaking. This is the origin of logic, the doctrine of *logos*; and the present-day interpretation of logic is unfit to grasp it.

The received treatment of rhetoric is likewise an obstacle to understanding Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, which the Berlin Academy edition placed at the end — proof of complete helplessness; the tradition had long lost the understanding of rhetoric once it became a mere school-discipline (already in Hellenism and the early Middle Ages). Heidegger's thesis, load-bearing for the whole chapter: *rhetoric is nothing other than the interpretation of concrete Dasein, the hermeneutic of Dasein itself*. That is Aristotle's intended sense of rhetoric. Speaking in the mode of speech-in-oration — in the assembly, before the court, on solemn occasions — these are exposed cases of the ordinary speaking as it speaks in *Dasein* itself. In interpreting the *Rhetoric* one must attend to how it already explicates ground-possibilities of *Dasein*'s speaking. Only against this ground of Greek *Dasein* do we understand that the definition *zōon logon echon* is no invention, no accident, but renders how the Greek primarily sees his *Dasein*. Hence the task is to grasp the main determinations Aristotle gives of *logos as speech*, to understand more sharply the definition *zōon logon echon*, and thereby where the *logos ousias*, the *horismos*, the theoretical speaking with the matters themselves, has its ground.

*Eudaimonia* is a definite being-actual of life in view of *aretē*, of the being-able-to-dispose over the being-possibility of the relevant being. Since there is a manifold of such possibilities, the question arises how it is to be articulated, with reference to what these various *aretai* are being-possibilities of the human. The articulation needs a ground taken from the human's being, and Aristotle takes it, again, from *logon echon* — which must show itself in its *widening*: *logon echon* not only in the proper sense but also, the human is a being that *says something to others* and, in one with that, *lets others say something to it* — the primary sense of speaking as *letting-oneself-be-told-something-by-others*. Insofar as the human is the speaker, he can say something to himself, has the possibility of *letting-himself-be-told-something-by-himself*. This shows in humans' being-with-one-another as encouraging, urging, admonishing. Insofar as the human lets himself be told something, he is, in a new respect, *logon echon*: he hears — not to *learn* something but to have a directive for concrete practical concern. This being-able-to-hear is a determination of *orexis*. *Logon echon* in this second sense Aristotle also calls *alogon*: *orexis* is not straightforwardly speaking but hearing. *Alogon* is claimed, first, for *logon echon* in the manner of hearing; second, for a how of the living thing's being that has no relation to speech. And the *threptikon* and *auxētikon* are fundamental being-determinations just like *aisthēsis*: nourishing too would be seen wrongly if taken as a physiological process. Reproduction is bringing-to-the-world, nourishing is holding-oneself-in-the-world.

How alive the being-character of the *threptikon* and *auxētikon* is, *De anima* B 4 shows: the nutritive soul is present in the others too, is the first and most common *dynamis* of soul, in view of which living belongs to all; its *erga* are to generate and to *use nourishment* (*trophē chrēsthai*) — where *chrēsthai* expresses the relation to world, as the things of the world are addressed as *chrēmata*. Most natural of the *erga* for living things — those that are *teleia* and not maimed — is to make another such as itself, animal an animal, plant a plant, that they may partake of the *aei* and the divine so far as their being-possibility allows. Bringing-to-the-world is a definite mode of being, oriented on the Greek ground-idea of being: in reproduction, by bringing another of its kind to the world, a living thing holds *itself* in its being; reproduction is the manner of a living thing's *ever-being-there*, since being for the Greek means being-present, ever-being-present. From this passage it follows that *metechein tou theiou* does not mean standing in some religious relation to God; *theion* has nothing to do with religion but is a circumscription of the concept of being in the manner of ever-being. To translate *theion* by "religiosity" is pure invention. Heidegger adduces the passage to make clear that what we call physiological processes are *modes of being* that give the possibility of being properly, being ever-there. These being-determinations — *threptikon*, *gennētikon*, *aisthētikon*, *noētikon*, *orektikon* — are divided by *logon echon*/*alogon*. The definition *zōon logon echon* thus shows a far greater reach than first appeared: in the definition itself, *zōē praktikē meta logou*; in that the human's disposable being-possibilities are divided on the thread of this determination; and in that *logos* names the *horismos*, the proper speaking with the world. And the *logon echon* is itself twofold: *epistēmonikon* and *logistikon* — speaking as theoretical contemplation and speaking as *logizesthai*, deliberating (*Eth. Nic.* Z 1–2).

### § 14. The Ground-Determination of Rhetoric and *logos* Itself as *pistis* (Rhet. A 1–3)

How can we get from Aristotle himself a conception that, and how, being-speaking was for the Greek the ground-phenomenon of their *Dasein*? We are fortunate to have a *Rhetoric*, which surveys the phenomena set as tasks in speaking. Rhetoric as reflection on speaking is older than Aristotle's — the *Rhetoric ad Alexandrum* transmitted among his works is not his (most likely pre-Aristotelian, ascribed by Spengel to Anaximenes); the reflection traces back to the Sicilians Teisias and Corax. The first carrying-through is Aristotle's — no accident, since he has for *legein* itself, and all the phenomena that come to speech in it, the right factual view and a developed conceptuality. The question: in what manner is *legein* the ground-determination of *Dasein* itself in the concrete manner of its everydayness? Heidegger will make present a few characteristic chapters of the *Rhetoric* and ask back what follows for *Dasein* that does *not* expressly dwell in oration — since these exposed modes of speech are only definite possibilities already prefigured in *Dasein*'s everydayness.

#### a) The Ground-Determination of Rhetoric as the Possibility of Seeing What in Each Case Speaks for a Matter

What does rhetoric mean at all? Aristotle determines it (Rhet. A 2) as a *dynamis* — and this is to be held fast against the fact that he often calls it a *technē*; that designation is improper, *dynamis* the proper definition. Rhetoric is the *possibility of seeing, at what is in each case given, what speaks for a matter that is the theme of the speech*. As a *dynamis* it does not have the task of *peisai*, of forming a definite conviction and setting it to work in others; it only presents a *possibility* of speaking for the speaker who is resolved to speak with the intention of *peisai*. The *rhētōr* is a *dynamenos*, and indeed *dynamenos theōrein* — able to *see*, not to persuade — *peri hekaston to pithanon*. Just as the thief is one who *can* take secretly; but proper thievery includes *boulesthai*, that he has put stealing into his choosing. The *dynamis* of rhetoric differs from *sophistikē*: sophistic too knows its way about speaking, but it lies not *en tē dynamei* but *en tē proairesei* — to the sense of sophistic belongs *unconditionally* to persuade others, whereas rhetoric holds itself *en dynamei*, forming a possibility for one who wants to persuade, a possibility that develops the being-able-to-see what speaks for a matter. Against the old definition *peithous dēmiourgos*, this is far more cautious: the reaching of the *telos* of speaking is not included. Medicine too does not as such make healthy; it produces a possibility for one who decides to heal, up to a limit — one can even treat the hopelessly sick according to the rules of medicine.

This comparison with *iatrikē* grounds the distinction of rhetoric from all *technai*. Medicine encloses a definite competence and, imparted, is a *didaskalikē*: it teaches within a definite subject-domain given in advance as its theme. Medicine treats of the healthy and sick human, arithmetic of numbers; every knowing-one's-way treats a definite subject-domain. Rhetoric has *no* subject-domain that could be delimited — and because it has none, it need not be called *technē*. Rhetoric is not *technē* but yet *technikon*: it gives an orientation *peri hekaston*, about what is just given. And it treats of this not by *description* — not the things as such, but the situation, insofar as something is to be drawn from it that *speaks for* something, for the conviction the speaker wants to form in others; not the matter itself but the circumstances with respect to a definite beneficialness, insofar as they can speak for something, for the *pisteuein*. To grasp what rhetoric treats, we must ask what can come into question *for* a matter at all. Aristotle distinguishes three kinds of *pisteis*, among which *logos* is one; *legein* itself, rightly understood, is a *pithanon*.

The prior consideration made clear the ground-function of *logos*: as determination of *zōē praktikē*; as character of the *aretai*; and as the way the being becomes accessible in its being — *logos ousias* as *horismos*. Concept-formation is characterized by the being's becoming determined, set off, graspable in its being. Heidegger wants to understand this possibility as one grounded in *Dasein* itself. What makes up conceptuality? For that we have a guide in *logos* itself: insofar as *legein* is a ground-mode of the human's being in the world, it makes possible that world holds itself graspable, determinable in concepts. We thereby strike a ground-phenomenon of *Dasein* — "phenomenon" in the trivial sense: something that shows itself in a definite kind of seeing and access — *logos* as ground-phenomenon of *Dasein* such that *through it* a still more original mode of the human's life becomes visible. That we have Aristotle's *Rhetoric* is better than if we had a philosophy of language: in it we have something that treats of speaking as a ground-mode of being as being-with-one-another, so that an understanding of this *legein* also presents the being-constitution of being-with-one-another in new aspects.

*Rhētorikē* is a *dynamis tou theōrēsai*, the possibility of seeing, *peri hekaston*, in what a definite situation of being-with-one-another just offers, that which speaks for a matter under negotiation. Through speaking a definite opinion is to be formed in others. Whoever appropriates rhetoric sets himself into the possibility of seeing, in each case, what speaks for a matter. Rhetoric gives a certain knowing-one's-way, but not about a definite subject-domain like arithmetic; it has no underlying matter, no *hypokeimenon* it is to bring to knowledge. It has the *technikon*, the possibility of giving a knowing-one's-way, but not about a delimited region of beings; in it various matters come to speech as the case may be, to form the *pisteuein* in the hearers. The determination of rhetoric: to see what speaks for a matter; in speaking to form the *pisteuein* in those addressed, over an affair under debate — to form a *doxa*. The *pisteuein* is a *view*, *doxa*, on which speaking turns, presumably something that in *Dasein*'s everydayness, in humans' being-with-one-another, leads and dominates them. Being-with-one-another moves in definite, again modifiable views about things; it is not insight but *view*, *doxa* — a *doxa* about things not in the sense that the things brought to speech are themselves thematically investigated. This *pisteuein*, holding-oneself-in-a-view within being-with-one-another, is what matters in the speech itself.

Rhetoric has to set out a possibility that enables one to see the *pithanon*, what contributes to forming a *pisteuein*; Aristotle also calls it *pistis*. *Pistis* here is not "belief" or "holding-to-be-so" but that which speaks for a definite matter, with respect to which a *pisteuein* is to be won. The relation of *pisteuein* to *pithanon* is analogous to that of *alētheuein* to *alēthes*: the un-coverable being-there that has the possibility of contributing to *alētheuein*. *Alētheuein* is a mode of being-in-the-world such that one has it uncovered, as it is; this *alētheuein* is the ground-phenomenon toward which the course steers, and it also underlies *legein*, insofar as *doxa* is a definite manner of appropriating the being as it shows itself. *Pistis* is the beneficial toward forming a *pisteuein*; the *pithanon* one should be able to see and learn in rhetoric. So the first task is an orientation about *pistis*.

#### b) The Three *pisteis entechnoi*: *ēthos*, *pathos*, and *logos* Itself

Aristotle divides the *pisteis* into *atechnoi* and *entechnoi*. The *entechnoi* are what speaks for a matter that we ourselves can dispose over, bring about through ourselves: we ourselves have the possibility of being such a thing as speaks for a matter. The *atechnoi* are what speaks for a matter that cannot be brought about by us but is there in advance and can be taken into use: witnesses (*martyres*), tortures (*basanoi*), documents (*syngraphai*) — treated in Rhet. A 15, where there are five: *nomoi*, *martyres*, *basanoi*, *synthēkai* (agreements), *horkos* (oath), bound to a definite kind of speech, speaking about the just in the court proceeding. Against these stand the *entechnoi*: of the ways of speaking-for-something that can be brought about through speech itself, there are three, according to a threefold possibility of taking the *logoi*. First, *en tō ēthei tou legontos*, in the *bearing* of the speaker, in how the speaker gives and holds himself in his speech — the speaker in his *ēthos* is himself a *pistis*. Second, *en tō ton akroatēn diatheinai pōs*, in the bringing of the hearer into a definite disposition — how the hearer stands to the matter, the manner of bringing-the-hearer-into-a-disposition, holds a *pistis*; the *diathesis* of the hearer determines his *krisis*, the view he finally forms. Third, *en autō tō logō*: *legein* itself is *pistis* as ground-function of *Dasein*; by how it is spoken, information is given about the matter itself, *dia to deiknynai*.

On the first: the *logos* must be so held that it makes the speaker himself credible, one who gives the impression that the matter is so. Through the speaking itself, through *how* the speaker speaks, the *ēthos* must become visible; from the speaking itself the *pistis* must grow. Even when we hold firm views, we trust the decent who make a good impression more and more quickly, generally in everything, and above all where the matter is in dispute, where it can be talked back and forth, where it hangs in the balance. Earlier treatises held that *ēthos* contributes nothing to the *pithanon* — a claim before Aristotle, and a point against sophistic. For Aristotle bearing is the *most excellent pistis*. On the second: how the hearer to whom one speaks is disposed, how attuned — Aristotle notes that we do not deliver judgments alike when sad or glad, whether we stand sympathetically or not, *ē philountes kai misountes*. The *diathesis* of the hearer is decisive; the speaker must aim in speaking to put the *akroatēs* into a definite *pathos*, to enthuse the hearers for a matter. This *pistis* on the hearer's side Aristotle treats extensively in Rhet. B 2–20 — an inquiry into the *pathē* that became historically potent (its influence on the Stoa and the whole doctrine of the affects as handed down). These *pathē*, the "affects," are *not states of the psychical*; they are a *disposition of the living thing in its world*, the manner of how it is placed toward something, lets a matter concern it. The affects play a fundamental role in determining being-in-the-world, being-with-and-toward-others. On the third: *pistis* as speaking about the matter itself; in speaking, the *alēthes*, the un-covered, is to be pointed out as the matter lies, free of all determinations — and shown *from the occurrences and circumstances that speak for the matter*, an *alēthes* opened not through a *theōrein* but one that makes the true visible *in the probable*. The *atechnoi* have their sense as *pisteis* only insofar as they are oriented on the *telos* of a definite *legein*, the court-speech, taken into use as the case requires.

The *dynamis*-determination shows that rhetoric gives no information about every concrete situation, as little as medicine informs about the medical treatment of Socrates or Callias. Rhetoric does not know its way about a definite case but about cases of such-and-such a kind, of such-and-such a look. It treats of what one ordinarily talks over in life, and the manner of talking-through; its orientation is on the definite *pressing matters* of everyday being-with-one-another — not every case, but what has a definite standing: court proceeding, popular assembly, the glorification of a hero, and the like.

#### c) *logos* Itself as *pistis*

**α) The Three Forms of Hearer and the Three Kinds of *logos* to Be Determined from Them: Deliberative (*symbouleutikos*), Judicial (*dikanikos*), and Epideictic (*epideiktikos*) Speech.** In Rhet. A 3 Aristotle reaches the ground-determination of *logos*: speaking has its *telos* at the *hearer*, the *akroatēs* — so speaking is *communication*; a speech is at its end only when taken up as communication. From the various ways a hearer can be, Aristotle determines three kinds of *logos*. The general structure of *logos*: speech consists of three moments — the speaker; the *about-which*, what the speaker points out; and the *pros hon*, the hearer to whom he speaks; the *telos* is at the hearer. From the ways the human in the *polis* can be a hearer, the *logoi* are distinguished. What does the *pisteuein* mean for humans' being-with-one-another? The nexus must be kept in view: the ground-determination of the human's being is being-with-one-another, carried by *logos*; and the question is how *logos* as *horismos*, scientific concept-formation, stands within *Dasein* — for which the *Rhetoric* is the thread.

There must necessarily be three forms of hearer. The *theōros* — the technical term for one attending a festival, the "spectator," but not the dull spectator, rather one who at once, with respect to what he sees, is *kritēs*, forms an opinion. A *kritēs* forms an opinion about what has happened or what is to come: the one judging about what is to come is the *ekklēsiastēs*, the participant in the popular assembly — where the *about-which* of deliberating has the character of the "not yet," but at once of a being-able-to-be, not a pure possibility but within the concrete possibilities of the deliberators and the circumstances. About what has happened the judge forms a view; about what now *is*, the *theōros* (about the *dynamis*). Hence three *logoi*: *symbouleutikos*, deliberative speaking, the back-and-forth in the assembly; *dikanikos*, court-speech, of the accused and the defender; *epideiktikos*, a speech of praise, a "pointing-out" that lets the human be seen in his life, where it turns not on a judgment in the sense of a court-verdict but where the seeing itself has the tendency to point out. All three *logoi* strike out to two sides: deliberative can be *protropē* or *apotropē*, urging-toward or urging-away, for or against, in both private and public counsel; judicial is *katēgoria* or *apologia*, ascribing a matter to someone, saying he has it on his conscience, accusing, or speaking-oneself-away-from, defending; and the pointing-out *logos* is *epainos* or *psogos*, praise or blame. The three *eidē*, each with its two-sided possibilities, are then characterized together with respect to their *about-which* and their temporal orientation, which Heidegger takes up next.

The three *logoi* differ further in the *time-character* of what they speak about. The *chronos* of deliberating is *ho mellōn*, the coming time, what will be — on which counsel is directed; the *chronos* of the one at law is *ho genomenos*, what has happened; and *ho parōn*, the present, for the epideictic. Correspondingly the being-character of the *about-which* differs: for the deliberative it is the *sympheron*/*blaberon*, for the judicial the *dikaion*/*adikon*, for the epideictic the *kalon*/*aischron*. Each *logos* has the three *pisteis* in a different way; Aristotle begins the closer explication with the *enthymēma*, the pointing-out, and gathers the characters: one must be able to *syllogisasthai* and to *theōrēsai* about the *ēthē*, the *aretai*, and, third, the *pathē*. The *syllogisasthai* stresses a possibility beside *theōrēsai*: coherent speaking demands a *seeing*, an understanding of what speaks for the matter.

**β) Rhetorical Speaking with *paradeigma* and *enthymēma* in Its Parallel to Dialectical Speaking with *epagōgē* and *syllogismos*.** Because the *logoi* are oriented on being-with-one-another, rhetoric can be viewed as a *paraphues* of *dialektikē* and of the *pragmateia peri ta ēthē*, an inquiry rightly called *politikē*. Rhetoric is *paraphues*, what grows up alongside the treatise on the *ēthē* that one may fittingly call *politikē* — ethics belongs *in* politics. Setting aside modern concepts of ethics and politics, the inquiry is to be understood as one primarily oriented on being-together, in particular on the individual's being-placed toward the other. "Together with" this inquiry, *paraphues*, is *dialegesthai*, since being-with-one-another is determined by speaking-with-one-another. And there is a connection with *dialektikē*, the discipline that develops the possibility of *dialegesthai*, of running-through the *logoi*, seeing what they mean and how they should look. Rhetoric is *antistrophos tē dialektikē*, turned-against dialectic: it is, unlike dialectic, related to *praxis*, concern — but neither is a knowing-one's-way about a definite subject-domain; neither rhetoric nor dialectic is an *epistēmē*; both are possibilities of bringing-forth the rightly-demanded speech.

Aristotle goes into the third *pistis*, *legein* as pointing-out. In *legein* he distinguishes: I can point something out by adducing an example or by grounding a thesis. The *deiknynai* through *logos* is twofold: *paradeigma* and *syllogismos rhētorikos* as *enthymēma*. This corresponds in *dialektikē* to *epagōgē* and *apodeixis* (*syllogismos*). To *paradeigma* corresponds *epagōgē*, to *enthymēma* *apodeixis*. *Enthymeisthai* is to take something to heart, weigh it in oneself, think it over; *enthymēma* is applied to a *legein* that of itself aims at concern, a speaking with others about something in which, by its own tendency, concern is at stake. *Apodeixis* means not merely talking-through states of affairs in what they are but speaking so that the *pisteuein* grows through the speaking. These are the two possibilities in *logos* insofar as it has the task of letting-see. *Paradeigma* is leading-toward-something by drawing in a concrete case; *para* means the present, what stands before one, what is directly adduced, demonstrated on an example.

Heidegger clarifies the *syllogismos* itself via the *Topics*. What one speaks *from* in a *syllogizesthai* we call the "major premise" — a designation that, orienting everything on the sentence (*Satz*), loses the proper sense of speaking. In a science what one speaks from must have the character of *alēthes*, lie so free in its being-so that one cannot ask further after the why; it must be evident in itself, only so the possible ground from which to go on and prove something. Aristotle determines the *syllogismos* as a *logos*, an *apophainesthai*, a speaking letting-see, in which something *other* comes in, is seen, than what lies before from the outset — precisely "on the way over what one starts from." In scientific speaking, *apodeixis*, the *hypothesis*, what one starts from, has the character of *alēthes* and at once of a *prōton*: needing no further speaking-out. It speaks in itself, for itself; it has the *pistis* through itself, so that to bring a *pistis* about it makes no sense.

The *syllogizesthai* of dialectic differs from scientific speaking in that what one starts from has the character of *endoxon*, is "in the *doxa*" — what appears so to all, or most, or the understanding among them, the most known and reputed. The *enthymēma* not only starts from an *endoxon* but returns to one, just as the scientific inference starts from what is in itself transparent and returns to a state of affairs with the same evidence. Rhetoric is akin to the *syllogismos* of dialectic insofar as its *endoxa* are quite definite: they concern the future, the past, the present, the beneficial, the fitting and unfitting, the beautiful and ugly. One has definite views, definite *doxai*, from which the assembly-speaker speaks, and he speaks so that a definite *doxa* arises in others. For this task — to start from an *endoxon* and reach an *endoxon* — the two ways are *paradeigma* and *enthymēma*. One must be cautious with *deiknynai*: it is not proof but a definite manner of enacting speech, bringing the matter into view.

The matters the *logos* deals with — *sympheron*, *dikaion*, *kalon* — show themselves as characteristic moments of the being as it is daily brought to speech, each in a peculiar oppositeness (beneficial/detrimental, and so on); speaking moves in an opposition. And in these moments a definite moment of *temporality* shows itself: the deliberator concerns himself with the *mellōn chronos*, what is not yet there but to be made available in the next everyday; what has happened comes to speech (that someone did a wrong); what now is comes to speech. The characters of the surrounding-world's being-there, as it comes to speech in everydayness, are at once characterized with respect to temporality — everydayness reveals itself in a fundamental ground-structure: *its temporality*. Being as concern and concernful speaking is temporal: it takes care of the not-yet-present, speaks about the already-happened, contemplates the now-there.

Aristotle then grasps these characters ontologically more sharply, more formally. What is spoken about has the character of *more-or-less*, a *megethos*, an extension marked by indeterminacy; this more-or-less is a ground-character of the being's being — now so, now so. With it goes the moment of *dynaton* and *adynaton*: what has such being that it can also *be otherwise*, *endechomenon allōs echein*, what the next moment is already other. According to this being-structure of everydayness, *legein* too is peculiar. It cannot be scientific proof, *apodeixis*; the being that daily happens does not stand under theoretical axioms — there are ground-opinions, views not arisen from theoretical contemplation but formed by life in everydayness itself. From this *endoxon* and about it one negotiates. Hence conditions on how *logos* must be with respect to its pointing-out character: it must be able to show this everydayness simply, without elaborateness, needing no extended proofs — through *epagōgē*, leading-toward; and, since a conviction is to be spoken out, the *syllogismos* must be an *abbreviated* kind of inference, because the assembly-hearer is *haplous*, simple, cannot infer from far off, has a short-breathed thinking, cannot gather a train of thought coming from far, "cannot take much together," so the pointing-out must be *enthymēma*, going more to his heart.

The difference between *epagōgē* and *syllogismos* Aristotle set out in the *Topics*, showing *epagōgē*'s advantage. *Epagōgē*, leading-toward, is an *ephodos*, a way-to, *apo tōn kath' hekaston*, through the individual just there, toward the *katholou* — not general validity but simply the "so, on the whole." Its advantages: it is *pithanōteron*, speaks more for itself; *saphesteron*, clearer, making no special demands about how the connections run, demonstrated on the example; *kata tēn aisthēsin gnōrimōteron*, more familiar with respect to direct taking-in; and *tois pollois koinon*, common and more accessible to most. The *syllogismos* too has advantages: *biastikōteron*, more forceful, persuading in the end more than mere pointing to a case; and *pros tous analogikous enargesteron*, more suited when one must speak with and question those who speak against, for whom no example first helps. Both have their positive possibilities.

Rhetoric is thus no purely formal discipline; it has a relation to the being of humans' being-with-one-another. The explicit stress on the connection of politics and rhetoric is intelligible only against the historical background: rhetoric is no self-standing *technē* but stands within *politikē*. The distinguished way to be in being-with-one-another is speaking-with-one-another; to set out the possibilities of being-with-one-another is the *ergon* of rhetoric, and because it treats *legein* in the mode of *enthymēma* and *paradeigma*, of the *syllogismos* and *epagōgē* of dialectic, it comes into dialectic's neighborhood. These assignments show its unselfstandingness and the peculiar being-character in which it moves. In Aristotle's time and before, rhetoric stood in quite another esteem within being-with-one-another: it "slips under, cloaks itself in the shape of politics." Rhetoric makes the claim to *be* politics — as do those who speak against politics, wanting to set rhetoric in place of *politikē*, "partly from lack of education, partly from boastfulness," holding the proper business of *politikē*, giving laws, unnecessary. Against this Aristotle (*Eth. Nic.* K 10) stresses that the whole can be carried through only on the ground of a concrete experience of *Dasein* itself, that one does not come near this *Dasein* by passing off the formal discipline of rhetoric as decisive. Knowing-one's-way in everyday *Dasein* is needed for those who would act within the *polis*. The sophists, who also pass themselves off as wanting to determine the possibility of the right *Dasein* of the *polis*, "show themselves far from teaching such a thing" — they do not even know what politics is about, else they would not equate rhetoric with politics or rank it higher, nor suppose law-giving can be done by simply gathering what seems good to most. Here the striving was alive to give rhetoric the ground-function of the proper understanding about *Dasein* itself; hence the sophists stand in confrontation with the philosophers — the philosophers are the *right* sophists, as Plato's *Sophist* would show. Rhetoric's peculiar standing to politics and dialectic betokens the peculiar thing it treats, ungraspable as a discipline or *technē*: a going-about, a *what-we-deliberate-about*. The *about-which* of rhetoric is deliberative speaking-with-one-another, of which there is no *technē*. What happens to everyone, everyday and ordinary, is not divisible into a craft or profession: everyone comes to speak in the assembly, everyone is liable to be haled before court, everyone has occasion to hear a speech of praise, at the Olympic games. This peculiar domain, the everydayness of *Dasein*, becomes manifest through the right interpretation of the *Rhetoric* — and manifest as already largely conceptually explicated.

### § 15. The *doxa* (Eth. Nic. Z 10 and Γ 4)

To understand the ground-phenomenon of everydayness that still underlies this speaking, one must first come to terms with the sense of *endoxon*, of *doxa*. *Doxa* means first the "view of something" but at once mostly "to have a view."

#### a) Delimiting *doxa* Against Seeking (*zētēsis*), Knowing (*epistēmē*), and Making-Present (*phantasia*)

First, *doxa* is *ou zētēsis*, no seeking, but *phasis tis ēdē*: I *already* have a view, I do not first seek, am not first under way to establishing a thing's constitution, but am placed thus-and-so toward it. *Phasis* is a certain *legein*, a Yes-saying to what I have a view about. Since *doxa* is a certain Yes-saying and no investigating, reflecting, first-coming-to-a-view, it stands in connection with *epistēmē*: when I know about something, know my way exactly, can say something about the matter even without it before my eyes. This *epistēmē* has the character of not being a *zētēsis*: one knows — a Yes. *Doxa* too is a certain Yes, a being-placed toward the matter, but differs from *epistēmē* in that to *doxa* belongs, second, *orthotēs*. When I know definitively, the known cannot be *pseudes*, false, else it would be no *epistēmē*. *Doxa* must have *orthotēs*, the *direction* toward, the being-aimed at *alētheia*. For having-a-view is *only* a view; it could also be otherwise. In itself *doxa* is true *and* false: it could be so, could be otherwise. The being-aimed at *alētheia* is constitutive for *doxa*, and hence the possibility of *pseudos* belongs to it. Plato (*Theaetetus*, *Sophist*, *Philebus*) did not yet have the possibility of seeing this. The "it could be otherwise" belongs to the view itself: I do not absolutely assert "so it stands" but, within a certain *phasis*, "we assume." Third, *doxa* differs from *phantasia*, the having-present of something without directly perceiving it, mere making-present — which can be true and false like *doxa*, but has both possibilities, as it were, only *from outside*, whereas *doxa* has them *in itself*. In the sense of opining lies the "can" — true or false, *dynaton*/*adynaton*.

The *endoxon* is the kind of being-oriented in which one is oriented about a being that can also be otherwise; the view can be revised. About the being that always is as it is, in *epistēmē*, there is no revision; to *doxa* belongs, from itself, revisability. *Doxa* is the way in which the world of being-with-one-another is there. Thereby the possibility is brought into being-with-one-another of being a *being-against-one-another*: one has this view, another that, because the being can be otherwise — the ground-possibility of speaking-against-one-another. *Doxa* is the way we have life in its everydayness there. Life knows of itself not scientifically, theoretically — that is only a distinguished possibility; *doxa* is the way life knows of itself. The goal of the *logoi rhētorikoi*, forming the *pisteuein*, is nothing but forming a *doxa*, the right view about a matter. For to *doxa* belongs the peculiar moment of *pisteuein*; with it goes a certain *pistis*, a *phasis*. Hence animals have no *doxa*, because they have no *logos*; a *phasis* is impossible for them. The being as *Da* is, for them, other.

#### b) Recalling the Nexus of the Treatment of *doxa*

Keeping the nexus in view: human *Dasein* is to be made visible from the ground-structure of its being, to see from it the possibility of concept-formation. *Dasein* was determined as being-in-a-world, more precisely being-with-one-another, having-the-world-in-common in which one is. This having-in-common is a dealing-with the world as concern of the world, whose character is that in this being-in-the-world *the being itself is at issue*: *eudaimonia* is taken care of — in taking care of what life deals with, it takes care of its own being. Being-with-one-another as concern has the ground-determination of speaking-with-one-another; *logos* is a ground-phenomenon of *koinōnia*, with the ground-function of making manifest that within which life holds itself: *dēloun*. Being-in-the-world is a being that has *uncovered* the world; being-in-it is oriented, the wherein uncovered. The in-being holds itself in a definite *acquaintedness* from which it draws its orientation. The phenomenon of in-being is finally to be set out, and from it conceptuality understood as a ground-possibility. Being-in-the-world is the ground-character of *Dasein* with respect to its uncoveredness: the world as what life deals with concernfully and speakingly in a certain acquaintedness. This acquaintedness is carried by speaking as the peculiar pointing-out of what one is oriented about, and is at once the way views and orientations are formed — formed, renewed, fixed, hardened in speaking. Speaking-with-one-another is thus the thread for uncovering the ground-phenomenon of *Dasein*'s uncoveredness as being-in-a-world, and the concrete thread is rhetoric, since it is nothing but the interpretation of *Dasein* with respect to the ground-possibility of speaking-with-one-another.

Rhetoric's task is to set out what speaks for a matter, the *endechomenon pithanon*. There are three moments as speaking-for-something, three *pisteis*, analogous to the structure of speaking: speaking about something (*peri tinos dēloun*, *deiknynai*); speaking to someone (*pros tina*), the *akouōn* (*pathos*); and being-speaking itself (*legōn*, *ēthos*). In all this what is spoken about shows itself under the ground-determination that it can also be otherwise, is each time different. The being at issue in everydayness is not the *aei on* but the *endechomenon kai allōs echein*, at once such as can *more or less* be what it just is. The *telos* of *praxis* Aristotle determines as a *telos kata ton kairon*: one looks to the moment — how, where, when, toward whom, in fixing the being-appropriate dealing. With respect to *kairos* a character of concern shows: it always wavers, does not stand fast. About this being one must bring forward definite views, form them in others, bring *Dasein* into the *doxa*, supply an *endoxon* about the world. So we strike the ground-mode in which the concerned world is had: *doxa*, translated "to have a view about something," "I am of the view," "I am for it that…". Aristotle treated *doxa* often and in manifold ways; it is the phenomenon discussed at length in the later Platonic dialogues (*Theaetetus*, *Philebus*, *Sophist*). It is a self-evident hermeneutic rule to approach these Platonically-discussed phenomena *from Aristotle*, where they are carried further and clarified — opening the perspective on what Plato did not see, of fundamental import for a core piece of Platonic philosophy.

#### c) Repetition and Continuation of the Delimitation of *doxa*: *doxa* and Being-Resolved (*proairesis*)

Three moments of *doxa* are set off against three kindred phenomena. First, against *bouleuesthai*: a seeking, a *zētēsis*, a being-out for a definite view I want to win; through deliberating I first come to the *telos* of a *doxa* — it is no Yes. I only want to appropriate a definite view of a state of affairs, whereas *doxa* is no longer a seeking but stands at the *end* of seeking, is a *phasis*. But though a Yes, it is no knowing. Second, against *epistēmē*: knowing-one's-way about a matter is characterized by the knower being placed so that he has an orientation about it even when it is not there — I know my way, I am *securely* placed toward the matter. Hence I have *epistēmē* only of beings with the character of *aei*. The ground-presupposition for knowing is a presupposition about the being of which there is knowing: that it is always as it is, cannot change; what is not *aei* can change. In *epistēmē* I need not have the matter actually present. Of the *endechomenon* there is no *epistēmē* but only *doxa*. Yet *doxa* is akin to *epistēmē* as a Yes-saying, a *phasis*; in contrast to *epistēmē* it is determined by *orthotēs*. In *doxa* I do not have the being itself but an orientation about it, aimed at the *alēthes*. The view tends to mean the being uncovered at itself, but it lies in *doxa* that it is only an apprehension, as such presumably false; with *doxa* the matter can also be false — no absolute assertion. In the being of being-of-a-view lies the addition: it can be so or so, can also be otherwise. Third, thereby *doxa* with its *orthotēs* differs from *phantasia*, a making-present that can also be true or false, but in another sense than *doxa* (treated *De an.* Γ 3). *Phantasia* can be false — hence akin to *doxa*. But *how* false? By its turning out *afterward* that it was in fact true or false, whereas with *doxa* the being-able-to-be-true-or-false is contained *already* in the view-formation. *Phantasia* is bare having-present; hence *phantasia* belongs also to animals, while *doxa* is only where *logos* is. In every view-formation something *speaks for* the view (*akolouthein*, *hepesthai*, as belonging to the phenomenon). With *doxa* the being-oriented is on the *alēthes*: it could be so, it looks as though it must be so — the speaking-for-something of what *doxa* speaks about. To opining belongs a being-convinced, a *pepeisthai*; and to being-convinced belongs *logos*, a speaking-out of what I have a view about. *Doxa* is characterized so that something is present to me in the character of "as so and so," i.e., *talked-over*; and *doxa* is of nothing else than that of which there is also *aisthēsis*. Here Aristotle confronts Plato (*Sophist*, *Philebus*): how *phantasia* differs from *doxa* lies in how the *alēthes* and *pseudes* are themselves co-meant.

Fourth, and decisively, *doxa* is delimited against *proairesis* (*Eth. Nic.* Γ 4). This pairing is surprising at first: one does not see what being-resolved-to-something and having-a-view-about-something have to do with each other. But the preceding philosophy, Plato, interpreted *proairesis* as a certain *doxa* — so there must be moments in *proairesis* that allow this. It becomes clear when *doxa* is rightly rendered: "I am for it that the matter stands so and so." But being-for can also mean: I am for it that a matter be *done* so and so — and being *resolved* that a matter be done so is *proairesis*. Aristotle delimits *proairesis* in four directions: against *epithymia*, being-inclined toward something, having-a-stirring for it; against *thymos*, being-in-excitement, having-passion for; against *boulēsis*, wish, wishing-something; and against *doxa*. The first three are characterized briefly, only to bring *proairesis* to understanding, since *proairesis* is a determination of *aretē*. *Aretē* as disposing over a being-possibility is further explicated as *hexis*, a being-at-oneself — the transition to §17, which Heidegger takes up after §16 on *ēthos* and *pathos*.

#### d) The Characters of *doxa* as the Orientedness of Average Being-With-One-Another-in-the-World

Heidegger gathers the analysis and turns it on the question that really interests the course: the peculiar phenomenon of being-oriented in the world — how human *Dasein* first has its world *averagely*, how orientedness lies in the having-of-world. What does the *doxa*-analysis teach about this phenomenon of uncoveredness? *Doxa* is the proper uncoveredness of being-with-one-another-in-the-world. The world is there for us, as being-with-one-another, in uncoveredness, insofar as we live in *doxa*. To live in a *doxa* means to have it *with others*; to opinion belongs that others too have it.

First, the domain of *doxa* is *panta*. Being-oriented in the world, even in everydayness, is not directed only on the *prakta*; uncoveredness does not obtain only with respect to the *prakta* — I do not merely know my way about my concrete task, what I have to do in my nearest surroundings, but also have a definite view of how the world, nature, in which the *prakta* are, is: of the moon, the stars, the *aei* of the Greeks. *Doxa* extends to the whole world, for the *prakton* I deal with is not a delimited region of beings; what I have to do with as a being is itself in the world, in the being of nature. There are definite being-relations between the *prakton* and nature, the *aei on*. The manner in which this world is had as uncovered to a certain degree is this being-for-it that it is so. In this being-for lies the *going-along with how the world first shows itself*, the moment of *trust in the nearest aspect*. Thales' opinion that water is the *prōton*, that the *archē* of being is water, is nothing else: intelligible from the dominion of a pervasive trust in what first shows itself, taken as what the world first is in the opinion of it.

To the determination of *doxa* belongs necessarily *the one who has the doxa*. With an *epistēmē* it is indifferent who has it; with a valid proposition it is indifferent who I am — it contributes nothing to the truth of the known. But the one having the view is co-decisive for the *doxa*; it weighs *who* has it. The matter itself cannot speak purely for itself, it is covered; I have a *view* of it. In *doxa* not only the matter speaks for the matter, so far as uncovered, but there speaks along the one who has the view, and those with whom the Yes, the *phasis* of the *doxa*, stands. Accordingly the firmness of a *doxa* is grounded not exclusively in the factual content it conveys but in those who hold it. In this structure lies the possibility that *doxa* comes to a peculiar dominion and stubbornness. One speaks an opinion *after* others; in this repeating it does not matter to investigate what the person says — decisive is not the said but *that he* said it. Behind the dominion of *doxa* stand *the others*, peculiarly indeterminate, ungraspable — "one is of the view": a peculiar dominion, stubbornness, and compulsion lying in *doxa* itself. This is Heidegger's ontological reading of the tyranny of the *Man* over opinion, and the point where *doxa* and *Verfallen* meet.

*Doxa* is thus the proper orientedness of average being-with-one-another-in-the-world. Average: the task of *investigating* the world is not set; as one everyday lives along in the world and deals with things, one deals with it *in and out of doxa*. One need not investigate everything as to its factual content; one holds to what the others say. Thereby *doxa* is set out both as the ground *and* as the drive to speaking-with-one-another, to negotiating-with-one-another. For though *doxa* has a certain firmness, it lies in it that about what one is of a view, one can speak further.

#### e) *doxa* as the Ground for Theoretical Negotiation

*Doxa* is not restricted to what can be otherwise; it is also the ground for the mode of grasping the being we call *epistēmē*, *theōrein*. Even the being about which I do not negotiate in the sense of concern, but in the sense of setting out the states of affairs as they are, is first there in a *doxa*. Hence Aristotle deliberately reaches back to the history of philosophy, discussing each fundamental problem first with a view to how one thought about it — from the positive understanding that in such a *doxa* the matter must somehow have come into view. *Doxa* is the peculiar trust in what first shows itself, and what first shows itself is the ground for the investigation of the matter itself.

**α) Pre-Giving (*protasis*) and the Cast-Forward (*problēma*) as the Whence and About-Which of Theoretical Negotiation (Top. A 4 and A 10–11).** To see that *doxa* also dominates theoretical *legein* — *logos* as treatise, theoretical explicating, not practical negotiation like a court proceeding — Heidegger turns to *Topics* A 1. There Aristotle shows about what, in its extent and quality, and from where, speaking-with-one-another in *dialegesthai* arises: what one speaks *from* and what one speaks *about* are equal in number and the same. What is spoken about are the *problēmata*; what the speaking is from is the *protasis*. Both spring from *doxa*, are *endoxa*, stand in some connection with *doxa*. They differ by the *tropos*, the manner. *Protasis* is a putting-forward, a pre-giving; *problēma*, from *proballō*, to cast forward: the cast-forward, insofar as it is a matter of throwing an opinion into discussion against the ruling opinion, showing the uncertainty, the problematic, that one has not yet come to a result. In *protasis* lies the character of *dialegesthai*, the pre-giving in the sense that *dialegesthai* appeals to a fixed opinion, claims the ground for itself, pre-gives something not to be further discussed but requested as the common ground from the others. *Problēma* the *pro*, *protasis* the *ex*. Example of *protasis*: "the statement, the human is a two-footed living thing, is surely the definition of the human! Living thing is surely the genus of the human!" — *ara ge*: you too are of the view that this is so; let us lay it down! Example of *problēma*: "Is the determination — the human is a two-footed living thing — the definition of the human, or not?" — beginning with *poteron*, demanding a quite definite decision. A peculiar *problēma* is the *thesis*.

*Doxa* is the specific orientation of being-in-the-world, in it the world is present; a structural moment (that in *doxa* a *phainomenon* is pre-given) Heidegger passes over here, deferring it to a later consideration of *alētheia*. What matters now is how from this *doxa* the individual possibilities of negotiating about the world spring. It lies in the sense of *doxa* to make discussion possible: what a view rules over is such as still lets itself be talked about. The possibility of negotiating-with-one-another lies enclosed in *doxa*; *koinōnia* is enacted this way. All coming-to-an-understanding in being-with-one-another is a coming-to-an-understanding on a certain ground of acquaintedness with something, which ground is itself not discussed for the discussion. This acquaintedness is what one speaks from and into, insofar as the result of the negotiation again has the character of *endoxon*. What one speaks from is not explicitly there; insofar as it is explicit, the phenomenon of *protasis* arises — the pre-giving of what one speaks from, which does not itself come to speech in the discussion. This "whence of the speech" is theoretically fixed as *archē*, in a sharply-pointed speaking of theoretical pointing-out and proof, where the phenomenon of speaking-to-one-another, though not explicit, is yet there — the treatise too addresses an addressee. Here *protasis* is called *archē*: the principles presupposed, from which the proof starts, are a definite case of the original nexus that one speaks from something known.

Aristotle treats *protasis* (*Top.* A 10) and *problēma* (A 11). *Protasis dialektikē* is characterized as *erōtēsis endoxos*, a questioning that holds within the circle of what a fixed view exists about — *mē paradoxos*, not against the general opinion. Questions introduced with *ara ge*, "it is surely so…," ask by their sense for *assent*. *Erōtēsis* is *apokriseōs aitēsis*, the requesting of an answer; in *protasis* one requests assent to what is said, the concession that for the further one then stands on common ground, directed at what seems so to all, most, or the understanding. The content of a *protasis dialektikē* can also be what belongs to *doxa*, or what one competent in his field says from his experience, a scientist from his discipline, without its being proved — this has the character of *endoxon*, *mē paradoxon*. With *problēma* it is not a matter of pre-giving as ground; Aristotle calls it *theōrēma*, something to be looked into, to become the object of speaking. The question-character of *protasis* requests assent, whereas *theōrēma* means something about which inquiry is to be made in negotiating, something *synteinon* — either toward *hairesis* and *phygē* (that I resolve for it or take distance) or toward *alētheia* and *gnōsis* (that something be uncovered and brought to knowledge). Something is cast up, discussion set going, and what is thrown into discussion has in itself the claim to be discussed. The relation of *problēma* to *endoxon*: something is cast up about which no agreement exists, about which one has come to no definite view on either side, which is disputed, open, or about which most think otherwise than the understanding, or both are at odds among themselves. What is to be investigated has the character of the disputed. A special form of *problēma* is the *thesis*: not every *problēma* is a *thesis*, but every *thesis* is a *problēma*. A *thesis* is an *hypolēpsis paradoxos* of one of those known in philosophy — a taking-for (*hypolēpsis* another word for *doxa*) that stands *outside*, beside *doxa*; such an *hypolēpsis* not as an arbitrary single case thought up by just anyone, since to *doxa* belongs constitutively the one who has it. The *thesis* is such a *doxa* as one has who belongs to those familiar with *philosophia*, in the field of the proper contemplation of beings; *kata philosophian*, a view cast up by one who moves factually in the domain of research — *philosophia* opposed to sophistic. Examples: Antisthenes' *doxa* that there is no contradiction; Heraclitus' "all is in movement"; Melissus' "one is the being" — not the *enantion tou tychontos*, of just anyone, but of a reputed one with competence. The *thesis* differs from the *problēma* in speaking expressly against the ruling opinion, whereas many *problēmata* do not, in pointed form, speak against the ruling opinion, though disputed and leaving something open.

**β) The Being-Unable-to-Get-Through (*aporia*) as the Theme of Theoretical Negotiation (Met. B 1).** From the characterization of the whence and about-which of *dialegesthai*, one can gather what can become a possible theme of negotiation at all, and set off the difference from the speech of rhetoric more sharply. Aristotle characterizes the theme of rhetoric as *ta ēdē bouleuesthai eiōthota* (what one is already accustomed to deliberate about), and what is treated in scientific discussion as *ta logou deomena* — *logos* meant as *dialegesthai*. In *dialegesthai* one treats what still *needs* (*deomai*) the speaking that has no further aim, that does not arise from the natural function of speaking in practical intent. Here *logos* is detached from the *prakton*; *logos* itself has become the *praxis*. Here *logos* as negotiation comes into its pure function as pointing-out what is negotiated in how and what it is. Discussion goes about what still *needs logos*, what is not straightforwardly clear, cannot be brought to understanding otherwise. A *logou deomenon* is what is not simply a matter of a *correction* (*kolasis*) or of *direct, plain taking-in*. Those who have difficulties whether one should honor the gods or love one's parents "need a correction" — we would say, a box on the ears; it makes no sense to set a treatise to work about it, nor about "whether snow is white or not," where it is simply a matter of opening one's eyes. A ground-requirement of conversation: that one has agreed about *whether the theme, by its factual sense, admits discussion at all* or lies outside all discussion. And of what does need a certain grounding, not every arbitrary thing is a *logou deomenon*: neither what the proof lies near to, where pointing-out is easily supplied, nor what the proof is too far-fetched for, falls under the possible theme; such has no *aporia*. The *aporia*, the being-unable-to-get-through, is the proper theme of theoretical negotiation: what genuinely blocks passage, where the matter neither lies open to plain sight nor is settled by an easy proof. This closes §15 and hands over to §16 on *ēthos* and *pathos* as *pisteis*.

### § 16. *ēthos* and *pathos* as *pisteis* (Rhet. B 1, Eth. Nic. B 4)

#### a) Theoretical and Practical Negotiation

Only when I already have the matter in a genuine way can I venture upon *aporiai*; not arbitrary difficulties and contradictions are seized. The *aporia* is the way to form a real, positive questioning in the positive intent of *poriein*. Against the *aporia*-bound theme of theoretical negotiation stands the theme of everyday speech in the assembly, before court, and so on: what is *already accustomed to be an object of deliberation*, what one has always talked over in being-with-one-another in the *polis*. Insofar as it is a matter of deliberating a *prakton*, and of an *endoxon* — speaking about general opinions against general views toward forming a definite view — this speaking does not place itself in the domain of *dialegesthai*. In this speaking, where it is a matter of such objects, the speaker and the one spoken to are fundamentally important. In *dialegesthai*, by contrast, it is to a degree indifferent to whom one speaks, and indifferent who I am, how I comport myself. In the first kind of speaking the *ēthos* of the speaker and the *pathos* of the one spoken to are relevant — both determinations ground how the *doxa* is had, how the one to whom the view is to be conveyed himself stands to it.

The *aporia*, ground-condition of a theme that aims at working out a problem, is intrinsically related to a *poriein*, a "running" — speaking in the sense of pointing-out, being-under-way in pointing-out. The *poriein* has as its goal the *euporein*, the getting-through in the right way to what is asked after; correspondingly *poriein*/*euporein* is a *proaporein* that goes ahead of a *euporein*. Related to *legein* it is *dēloun*, making manifest what is asked after; related to questioning itself it is a way of forming the question rightly. By pointing out definite factual characters of the matter asked about, the end of the investigation becomes manifest, and the possibility given to bring the investigation into the right track and to decide, at its end, whether what is sought has been found, whether the result is a factual one. The *aporia* in Aristotle is related and restricted: what falls into scientific investigation must have the character of *aporian echon*, of having difficulties — the ground-condition for something's being a *logou deomenon*, and then *protasis* and *problēma*.

The second kind of about-which is what one is accustomed to deliberate about, brought to speech in a definite being-nexus of life, not settled once for all by factual considerations but recurring ever again according to circumstances and situation. The theme here is not an identical state of affairs passed on within a science; it is something that changes with the circumstances of *Dasein* itself — affairs according to *Dasein*'s circumstances, change of mood and, accordingly, of view. This is what rhetoric treats: no thematic state of affairs but something that changes with the views; the respective situation of things and humans speaks along. Hence the "taking of the pre-givings," *lambanein tas protaseis*, is here another: at the pre-giving other things must be taken into view — the mood of those spoken to, the situation of things, and how one oneself stands to the matter. Hence concretely to be considered: the *ēthos*, the bearing of the speaker, and the *pathos*, the disposedness of the hearer.

Rhet. B 1 gathers Book I and gives Book II's theme. The treatment of this speaking as rhetoric takes the conversation as aiming in itself at view-formation: a *doxa* is to be formed. Since rhetoric has *krisis* — view-formation, a definite decision in the sense of *doxa* — before its eyes, one must look not only at the speaking as such, the function of pointing-out (*deiknynai*, *dēloun*), but the speaker must bring himself and those in whom the decision is to lie into a corresponding constitution — and indeed *through the speaking itself*. For it makes a great difference in conveying what speaks for something, most in deliberations, then also in adjudication, how the speaker shows himself and accordingly how the hearers think of his disposedness, and further whether they themselves attain the right disposedness (their stance to the discussed matter). The matter in question does not show itself as the same for those who — *philousin* — have a fondness for it and for those who — *misousin* — have an aversion; it shows itself as either wholly other or in great part other. To one well-disposed toward another in advance, that other will show himself as one who has not erred at all or only slightly; to one who has something against him, in the reverse way. And one who is out for a matter in advance, who sympathizes with it, will take what is to come as something that will indeed come and is at the same time beneficial — the optimist; to the indifferent and the ill-humored pessimist things show themselves in advance in another light, and he will stand otherwise to the deliberation.

#### b) *ēthos* as *pistis*

The *ēthos* and the *pathē* are constitutive for *legein* itself. First the *ēthos*, the bearing of the speaker: how the speaker gives himself to his hearers in speaking, how this self-giving contributes to forming the *pithanon*, how this *ēthos* gets to speak along, to weigh in. What is it in speaking that makes us, as hearers, take the speaker as one who himself bears witness for the matter he represents — apart from what he says, from the factual arguments? Three moments make up the *ēthos*: *phronēsis*, circumspection — the speaker must show himself as circumspect; *aretē*, earnestness (circumscribed earlier by *spoudaiōs*); and *eunoia*, good disposition, benevolence.

Aristotle shows the concrete meaning of these three by the opposite way, from the contrary, asking: what makes us get the opinion that a speaker deceives, leads astray? He asks after the conditions of the possibility of showing-oneself-as-one-who-deceives; what does the speaker lack in his self-giving such that we do not take him as one who in fact has the right *ēthos*? First, in his speaking the speaker can show himself as an *ouk orthōs doxazōn*, one who does not form his view rightly: he lacks the right horizon for the matter, does not fully survey it; the view he brings forward is not oriented on what the matter really is, it lacks *orthotēs*. As soon as the hearer notices the deficiency, the speaker loses *pistis*. Second, the speaker may have the right *phronēsis*, may show himself circumspect, but as one who *will not say* what he thinks; the hearer notices: he knows his way, but does not say everything, veils his own stance and view, is not in earnest with what he tells his hearers, knows more. As soon as the hearer notices this, he withdraws trust, does not take him seriously, for the speaker does not stand in earnest for what he says. Third, the speaker may show himself circumspect and in earnest, yet the hearer notices he lacks the needed benevolence: he can advise something, commend something as *sympheron* that he even believes is *sympheron*, but — despite fulfilling the first two moments — holds back the best counsel from lack of benevolence, because the people do not interest him; he can withhold the most decisive positive possibility he disposes over from his *phronēsis*, content to make the assembly an earnest proposal but not the best. Then too the hearer loses the right trust. Conversely, a speaker who shows himself circumspect, earnest, and speaking out of benevolence for the matter will have the right trust — he will himself, in his *logos*, be a *pistis*. *Phronēsis* and *aretē* Aristotle treated already in Rhet. A 9; the third moment, *eunoia*, he considers in connection with the analysis of the *pathē*.

#### c) *pathos* as *pistis*

The *pathos*, the second moment for *pistis*, Aristotle treats extensively in the following chapters of Book II. The expression *pathos* is highly manifold and has, within Aristotle's philosophy, fundamental significance. Three ground-meanings, hence three factual nexuses: first, the average, nearest meaning, "changeable constitution"; second, a specifically ontological meaning, important for understanding *kinēsis* — *pathos* in connection with *paschein*, mostly translated "suffering"; third, a pointed meaning: changeable constitution in relation to a definite factual nexus, in a definite being-region of life, "passion." In this last meaning *pathos* is the theme of the *Rhetoric* and *Poetics*.

The right nexus in which the phenomenon named *pathos* is discussed becomes visible in *Eth. Nic.* B 4, where Aristotle begins the investigation into what *aretē* really is — its *genesis*, the being-nexuses from which it comes. He introduces it with a remark important for us: since what comes-to-be in the soul is three — *pathē*, *dynameis*, *hexeis* — *aretē* would be one of these. *Pathos* belongs to what comes-to-be in the soul. *Psychē* is the *ousia* of a *zōon*, makes up the being of the being characterized as being-in-its-world; being has three modes of its becoming: *pathē*, *dynameis*, *hexeis*. *Pathē* are *epithymia*, *orgē*, *phobos*, *tharsos*, and so on — in general, what is accompanied by *hēdonē* and *lypē*, a definite finding-oneself, being-lifted, being-depressed. The *hexis*, by contrast, is first what characterizes the manner in which we *are* in such a *pathos*; it is that with respect to which we are praised or blamed. With respect to the passions — that we are in anger — we are neither praised nor blamed; the *manner* in which I am in anger, in which situation, on which occasion, against whom — that *pōs* is subject to praise or blame. The *hexis* concerns the *pōs echomen pros ta pathē*, how we hold ourselves, in what composure we are, in such a *pathos*. *Pathos* is a definite coming-out-of-composure. The *dynameis* concern those being-determinations of the living thing that Aristotle also characterizes as *phusei on*: given in the possibility of our factical *Dasein* are the possibilities of being angry, sad, glad, of hating, and so on; these *dynameis* too are *ginomena en tē psychē*.

Important is the being-given of the *pathē* as *ginomena*, modes of being itself, insofar as we live — modes of becoming, concerning being-in-a-world — and that the *pathē* have the possible relation to *hexis*. From the closer understanding of *hexis* we will understand the analysis of the *pathē*, see how what is named by *pathos* determines being-in-the-world in a fundamental sense, and how it comes into question, as such a ground-determination, in forming the *krisis*, the taking-of-a-stance, the deciding of a decisive question. With the demonstration of this fundamental role of the *pathē* in *krinein* itself, we at once get the possibility of seeing the ground of *logos* itself more concretely. The *ēthos* of the speaker must be quite definite for him to stand before the hearer as one who as a person speaks for the matter; it must satisfy the determinations of *aretē*, *phronēsis*, *eunoia*. The *ēthos* is nothing but the manner in which what the speaker *wills* — willing in the sense of *proairesis* — is made manifest. So Aristotle determines the role of *ēthos* in the *Poetics*: *ēthos* makes manifest the respective being-resolved of the speaker. In speeches that by their sense do not turn on being-resolved to something or bringing others to a definite resolve, there is no *ēthos*; there it turns on the *dianoia*, what is needed to point out something in its factual character. Heidegger notes this fixing of the conditions of speaking is not exhausted even today, for one can ask how far, in scientific and philosophical lecture, *logos* is to be taken as mere *deiknynai* and how far a *proairesthai* stands in it — remarking pointedly that it might be fitting for philosophers to resolve to consider what it means at all to speak *to others*.

The second condition is the hearer's disposedness, *pathos*. Rhetoric's task is to set out the possible situations in which the hearer can find himself disposed, his constitutions, in their various moments, to give the speaker a directive for what to consider when he chooses his *proairesis*. Under this intent stands the analysis of the *pathē* in the *Rhetoric*: to analyze the various possibilities of the hearer's finding-himself so as to give threads for what must be formed in him. The first determination: the *pathē* are that through which, *changing*, humans differ with respect to their *kriseis*. *Metaballontes*: something under way whereby a *turnabout* sets in in us, whereby we turn over from one disposedness into another. In one with this turnabout, *diapherousi pros tas kriseis*: we differ from ourselves before the turnabout in what is the hearer's task — taking a stance, forming a view; view-formation hangs on how we turn over. *Hois hepetai lypē kai hēdonē*: not "follow" but "co-given" in one with the *pathē* is a being-higher- or being-lower-attuned of the *Dasein* in question. These are the constitutive moments Aristotle fixes with respect to the *pathē* in the special aim of the *Rhetoric*'s analysis.

The manner of the constitution we stand in makes up also how we stand to things, how we see them, how far and in what respects. The coming-from-one-definite-constitution-into-another concerns primarily the mode of taking-a-stance to the world, of being-in-the-world. Herein lies the possibility and danger of the shifting of relations. The right constitution is nothing but the right being-in-the-world as disposing over it. The world is first and mostly there in *praxis*, in the character of *endechomenon allōs* and at once in the determinations of more-or-less; the world is there as *agathon* or *sympheron*, and that in more-or-less; so too our comportment to it is more-or-less, we comport ourselves at these swings more-or-less in an average way of moving in the world. The transparency of the world is more-or-less. From this one understands that "coming into the proper constitution" means coming into the *middle*, out of the swings into the middle — and the middle is nothing but the *kairos*, the totality of circumstances, the how, when, for-what, and about-what.

The question is how *pathos*, by its structure, is more precisely to be understood — after its being-determinations, how Aristotle defines *pathos* itself. *Pathos* characterized quite generally as *ginomenon tēs psychēs*, soul taken as *ousia*; *metabolē* and *genesis* used in the same meaning: *pathos* is a turning-over, hence a definite becoming-into from an earlier situation — but not a turning-over with its own course, rather a mode of finding-oneself in the world that at once stands in a possible relation to *hexis*. This turning-over into another constitution, and being in the new from out of the old, has in itself the possibility of being-seized, being-overcome. The manner of coming-out-of-composure, of being-brought-out-of-composure, is such by its sense that composure can be *regained*: I can compose myself again; I am, for a definite moment, in danger, in the moment of terror, in composure; I can relate the disposedness marked by terror to a possible composure-for-it. So *pathos* has in itself already the relation to *hexis*. These two concepts can be characterized, on Aristotle's basis, as *fundamental concepts of being*. Already thereby *pathos* is indicated as a being-concept, since *paschein*, in opposition to *poiein*, is a ground-moment for the analysis of *kinēsis*, of being in the sense of being-moved. *Hexis* goes back to *echein*, having — recognized by Aristotle as a mode of being; it is not so puzzling that among the ten categories *echein* too occurs. The task is to set out the being-structure in the two phenomena *pathos* and *hexis* — which §17 undertakes.

### § 17. The *hexis* (Met. Δ 23 and 20, Eth. Nic. B 1–5)

#### a) *echein* and the *hexis*

Aristotle treats *echein* in *Met.* Δ 23: *to echein legetai pollachōs* — various beings are addressed by the expression in various senses, not a random jumble but bearing on a ground-meaning brought to sight through the individual meanings. We must see wherein the manifoldness of the meanings of *echein* comes together, how far *echein* expresses being. First, *echein* as *agein*, proceeding according to one's own full-determinate possibility of *Dasein* (*phusis*) or according to the impulse (*hormē*) lying in the being as such: so one says the fever *has* the human (the illness has gone at him, "it's got him"), the tyrants *have* the cities (rule them), the clothed *have* their clothes on. Second, *echein* as being a being such that in it something is present-at, to which presence the being itself has the readiness (*dektikon*): the bronze *has* the look of a statue (is a statue), the body *has* the illness (is ill). The bronze is determined in its being so that it can become a statue; *hylē* here does not mean an indeterminate "matter" but a positive character of a mode of *Dasein* — being-in-readiness-for is a positive determination. Having means: to be, out of readiness, the wherein of a presence of something. Third, *echein* as the encompassing having the encompassed: the vessel *has* the water, the *polis* the humans, the ship the sailors; so too the whole *has* the parts. Being-part is always being-part-of-something, belonging to a whole; the whole is the whither of the part's definite belonging. Fourth, *echein* as what *hinders* another, according to its own *hormē*, from moving or acting: the pillars *have* (hold up) the weights on them, and, as the poets say, Atlas *has* the vault of heaven — having in the sense of holding, as *kōlyein*, holding-off another being from being as, by its own *hormē*, it would be. The weight's *hormē* is to fall down; the vault "has the tendency to fall to the earth." Having in this sense of holding-off, of the *synechon*, holding-together — a concept constitutive for understanding movement (the *syneches*, the continuum, is a ground-moment of the being of the moved). These four kinds of *echein* mark the being always in the being-character of being-out-for a definite being-possibility, or its negation, which in the sense of negation is the same: the holding-off from being properly as something would be. No accident that Aristotle closes by saying that *being-in-something* is said in the same manner and *following* on *echein*: the character of having-and-being-had, like that of being-in-something, is co-given. So it is justified that *echein*, beside *keisthai*, appears among the categories.

On this *echein*, taken as a mode of *Dasein*, the *hexis* is in turn related (Δ 20): *hexis* in one way is a certain *energeia* of the having and the had, like a certain *praxis* or *kinēsis*. *Hexis* is the *energeia*, the proper *Da*, the being-present of the having one and the had. Within this being-nexus *hexis* means the proper being-present of the having as such. When one thing does, the other is done, the doing between them is the *poiēsis*; so with wearing a garment, there is a *between*, the wearing on the one side, the garment worn on the other. The wearing as such is the *hexis*. This having is an ultimate; it cannot itself be further had — the having of this having is no new being-determination but simply the *Da*, the being-present. In the wearing of the garment as put on, it is properly there as put on; so too with the *Dasein* of the garment: a garment is not there when it hangs in the closet, but when it is worn it is at its *telos*. In being-worn the garment is what makes up the proper *Da* both of the put-on and the carried garment: the *hexis*.

This *hexis* Aristotle further characterizes as a *diathesis* according to which the disposed is disposed well or badly, and that either in itself or toward another — as health is a *hexis*, for it is such a *diathesis*. On *diakeisthai* the *metaballein* is related, which goes on through the *pathē*. *Diakeisthai* (Δ 19): having is a *taxis*, a distribution of parts in various respects, a distribution having the character of *thesis*, a *posited* distribution, not a chance falling-together but a being-set. The *hexis* as *diathesis*, as *taxis*, springs from *proairesis*: the right finding-oneself in the distributedness of the moment. *Hexis* is the determination of the *authenticity* of *Dasein* in a moment of composure for something; the various *hexeis* as the various modes of being-able-to-be-composed. *Hexis* is quite fundamentally the being-determination of proper being, here referred to the human: *praxis* is characterized by *aretē*, *aretē* characterized as *hexis proairetikē*. *Praxis* as a how of being-in-the-world shows itself here as the being-nexus we can also, in another sense, call *existence*. The composure is not arbitrary and indeterminate; in the *hexis* lies the primary orientation on the *kairos*: "I am here — let come what will!" This being-there, being-on-one's-post in one's situation, over against one's matter, characterizes the *hexis*. So the *hexis* is a being-possibility that is in itself related to another possibility — to the possibility of my being, that within my being something comes over me that brings me out of composure.

#### b) Recalling the Nexus of the Treatment of *hexis*

The *hexis* plays a fundamental role in the analysis of the human's being; but it also matters for another ground-determination, since in the counter-consideration against *hexis* Aristotle discusses *sterēsis*, extensively at *kinēsis*. The concept of *sterēsis* too has a ground-relation to being. The task is to understand the *pathē* as the determinations characterizing the hearer, who stands to the speaker in a definite situation, so that the situation co-determines how the hearer understands; in speaking-with and speaking-after he appropriates what the speaker wants to point out. The *pathē* are theme insofar as they are co-decisive for the manner of *legein*, how *logos* has its ground in the *pathē*. For the right grasp of the *pathē* Heidegger takes the detour over *hexis*, on a general hermeneutic thread: what is structurally more transparent illuminates the opaque. The *pathē* can be *had*; in having lies a relation to being. With the orientation of the *pathē* on *hexis*, the *pathē* themselves are oriented on *Dasein* as being. This ground-orientation is important against the traditional conception of the affects as "states of the soul," possibly with "bodily concomitants" — a splitting of the phenomenon into psychical and bodily states. Against this: Aristotle, treating the psychical as the mode of being of the living thing, stresses that the *pathē* express the being of the human, so that here a wholly other ground obtains from the outset. The original unity of the phenomenon of the *pathē* lies in the being of the human as such.

Aristotle's doctrine of the *pathē*, in its grounding and its selection of phenomena, worked powerfully on later philosophers and theologians (Thomas' doctrine of the affects); the *pathē* are a ground-question of theology. In the Middle Ages *fear* especially plays a special role, since the phenomenon of fear stands in special connection with sin, and sin is the counter-concept of faith. Luther confronted fear (early writings, the *Sermo de poenitentia*); the discussion of *phobos*, *timor*, connects with *timor servilis* and *timor castus*, then with repentance, where *attritio* and *contritio* are distinguished — *timor castus* the pure fear from God's presence, *timor servilis* the fear of punishment. These distinctions go back to Augustine, and the medieval treatment of the *pathē* also to John Damascene and Nemesius (whose treatise the Middle Ages cites under Gregory of Nyssa's name), who has the doctrine of the *pathē* from the Stoa. The whole development of the doctrine of the affects is still not philosophically analyzed; only Dilthey (in *Weltanschauung und Analyse des Menschen seit Renaissance und Reformation*) considered the *pathē* extensively and characterized their significance beyond psychological states.

The *pathē* characterize quite generally a disposedness of the human, a how of being-in-the-world. Accordingly Aristotle's thread for the analysis in Rhet. B considers the *affectus* in three respects. First, of each *pathos* the question arises, *pōs diakeimenoi eisi*: how do we find ourselves, what is our being-in-the-world, when in anger, in fear, in pity? Second, *tisin* (Aristotle: *tisin eiōthasin orgizesthai*): about what are we brought out of composure? Third, *epi poiois*: over against whom, which humans encountering us, who are there with us? Already in the ground-structure of the *pathē*, again, the orientation on the being-with-one-another of *Dasein* as being-in-the-world. Presumably it is just the manifoldness of these relations, expressed through the *pathē*, then seized through the *hexis*, over against which the *hexis* expresses a composure. To see the connection of the *pathē* as possibilities of finding-oneself and being-seized, we must bring the *hexis* itself nearer, as a ground-determination of the human's *Dasein*.

#### c) The *hexis* and the *aretē*

We consider the *hexis* as related to the *zōē anthrōpou*, to *praxis meta logou* — *hexis* as the *genos* of *aretē*: *praxis* has its proper how in *spoudaiōs*, the earnest expressed by *aretē*. From the connection of *hexis* and *aretē* we see the orientation of *hexis* on the human's *Dasein* in its concrete possibilities. From *Eth. Nic.* B 1–5 Heidegger draws only the most important, to demonstrate the *hexis* and clarify a ground-concept of the *Ethics*, the *mesotēs*. *Mesotēs* is not "mediocrity," no determination of human acting that would come out at mediocrity, no so-called "bourgeois morality," no principle of "value-rankings," but the ground-relation to *hexis*, thereby to the human's *Dasein*, to *praxis*, thereby to the *kairos*. The *Nicomachean Ethics* is anything but the ethics of a mediocre averageness and convention.

From the insight into the connection of *hexis* and *aretē*, four ground-moments of *Dasein* follow. First, that acting, *praxis*, concern, is in itself concern of the *Dasein* that concerns itself: in dealing about in the world, in dealing with other humans, the so-dealing *Dasein* takes care of itself, its being — *Dasein* as concern is care about itself, mostly inexplicit. This ground-phenomenon is hidden in the concept of *hexis* — *hexis* as having of something, in having a mode of being, of being-placed toward the had. Second, in *hexis* *Dasein* shows itself more sharply in its *respectiveness*: the human's being, human life as *Dasein*, is respective, in-the-moment; *hexis* is a composure of *Dasein* oriented on the moment. Third, this composure, this being-oriented on the moment, is a possibility *Dasein* has itself seized from its respective situation. *Dasein* holds itself average and mostly in swings, in more-or-less, in too-much and too-little. Fourth, from this it is at once evident that *hexis* is a ground-determination of *Dasein* itself, and that the *genesis* of this *hexis* — how *Dasein* comes to a composure over against itself, the occasion and manner of its formation — can have its place only again in *Dasein* itself. *Dasein* must take the occasion at itself to form this composure as a possibility.

**α) The *genesis* of *aretē*.** *Aretē* as *hexis* is no property, no possession brought to *Dasein* from outside, but a mode of *Dasein* itself. Again the peculiar category of the *how*: *aretē* is a how of *Dasein*, not a fixed property but a how determined by *Dasein*'s being, characterized by temporality, by the stretch in time. Hence *aretē* is and becomes *di' ethous*, through habituation. The possibilities of composure toward the various disposednesses in which I am or come out of composure are seizable only by living through various situations, dangerous ones; only by life's not withdrawing from its own possibilities and dangers is the occasion given to form this how of *Dasein*. In being present, according to our being, in the full presence of the situation, we seize the *hexis*. By making use of the possibilities of acting, of concern, in the manner of disposedness, we appropriate the *hexis* in the first place — not, conversely, having it as a possession to then use, but *chrēsamenoi eschomen*, having used, we came to have. This living-through, taking-or-seeking-occasion, is a dealing: by being with humans in dealing, we become composed and prudent; by bringing ourselves into dangerous situations, we have the possibility of learning courage, of losing cowardice — not in a fantastic reflection over *Dasein* but in venturing out into *Dasein* according to the possibilities of the relevant existence.

This must not be taken as though there were a *technē* for this taking-occasion and venturing into the *deina* of life: neither under a *technē* nor under any *parangelia* (a general army-order, an a-priori ethics by which humanity *eo ipso* becomes better) does it fall; each must for himself have his gaze directed on what is in the moment and concerns him. From this the how of composure over against *Dasein* is to be formed; thereby the *hexis* is oriented. But a difficulty arises: what should it mean to become just by acting justly? I must already *be* just to act justly. Aristotle (Eth. Nic. B 3) resolves it by pointing to the other kind of relations in *technē*. In *technē* it matters that the *gignomena* turn out rightly: in cobbling, that the shoe, the *telos*, the *ergon*, be a good, fitting shoe — nothing further comes into question. But the human's being in its *ergon* is *praxis*, which has the *telos* in itself, is at its end at itself. Hence for the human's *ergon* quite other ground-conditions are decisive: it turns on how the actor comports himself *as such*, on the *hexis*, the composure, and this *pōs echōn* of the actor is determined by three moments. First, *eidōs* — *phronēsis*: he must be knowing, act in the right circumspection, oriented on the *kairos*. Second, *proairoumenos*, from a real being-resolved, acting from himself. Third, so that he is thereby *bebaiōs kai ametakinētōs echōn*, firm and not to be brought out of composure — recalling the determination of *pathos* as that through which, *changing*, we come from one composure into another; characteristic is not the result, the having-come-into-another-composure, but the being-out-of-composure, the being-under-way, the peculiar unrest given with *pathos* itself, characterized with respect to *phobos* as *tarachē*, confusion.

These determinations, especially that the *proairesis* be *bebaiōs*, are "not reckoned in" with a *technē*, where only the right factual competence comes into question — what kind of fellow I myself am plays no role for cobbling. Against a certain commonality of *technē* and *praxis* through the determination of *eidenai*, Aristotle stresses the distinguished significance of *proairesis* and the *bebaiōs*. As fitting and composed *pragmata* (Heidegger: we lack the category — the new factual situation I have created through my *praxis*, the new being-placed toward something, which Aristotle calls *pragmata*) are addressed the *pragmata* a *sōphrōn* or *dikaios* could have done; but fitting and *sōphrōn* is not the one who, by some chance, does what is fitting and just, but the one who takes care of the situation in the same *how* of the just and composed. This is a blow against the sophists and the many, who believe that by talking over ethical conflicts, by moralizing, one gets something for ethical acting. The many do not take care of this (the being-*proairoumenos-bebaiōs*) but take refuge in chatter and believe they thereby philosophize and are in earnest; they resemble those who listen carefully to the physician (and discuss it) but apply nothing — as those do not become healthy by so taking care of themselves, neither do these, who only moralize, have proper existence at themselves, but only as appropriated in talk. Characteristic is the sharp opposition of *legein* about ethical problems and right philosophizing — a move against the misuse of the Socratic method, in the claim that Aristotle understood Socrates rightly, which one can hardly dispute.

**β) *aretē* as *mesotēs*.** The relation of *hexis* to *aretē* is to be brought nearer, to understand how the *hexis* can be the *how* of our comportment to the *pathē*. *Hexis* is nothing but a how of the *pathos*, of being-out-of-composure or composed toward something. Insofar as we can determine the *hexis* by its ground-structure, we thereby make transparent the possible structure of the *pathē*. The *hexis* is a ground-determination of *aretē*: by its being-provenance (*tō genei*) *aretē* is a *hexis*, a composure for something. *Hexis* is to be understood in relation to the concrete being of the human — it also has the wider meaning, identical with *dynamis* of any being; here it has the definite orientation on the human's being, related to *zōē praktikē meta logou*, a being-in-the-world in which the world is encountered in the character of *sympheron*, *blaberon*, *hēdy*, *lypēron*. Our being-in-the-world is always characterized by this disposedness of being-lifted and being-depressed, so that we find ourselves in the swings, carried by a bad or a lifted mood; within this being, the *hexis* is the definite composure. Thereby *aretē* is determined in its being-character.

Aristotle takes the being-determination of *aretē* still more sharply by taking it as *mesotēs*, the *ousia* of *aretē* as *mesotēs*. The term *mesotēs*, *meson* stems from *medicine*, which aimed to grasp the healthy state as a *meson* and orient its concept-formation on that; Aristotle carried this ground-concept over to ethics, with a concrete eye on the otherness of the ground-sense of being at issue there. In earlier ethical questions the concept of *mesotēs* does not appear. He brings the *meson* nearer via the determination of the middle at any *pragma*: in everything continuous and divisible, one can distinguish the more and the less and, accordingly, the equal — and these differences *kat' auto to pragma* (with respect to the matter itself) and *pros hēmas*. The equal is a *meson* of excess and deficiency; the *meson* is that equal we grasp as the equidistant from the ends — as *meson* of the matter itself is addressed the equidistant from each of the two ends. So one can determine the middle at a thing, geometrically. But insofar as the *meson* is to be used for the interpretation of the human's being, it concerns not a *pragma* at itself but insofar as it is *pros hēmas*, insofar as we stand to it, insofar as it concerns us such that the matter does us "not too much and not too little." With *mesotēs* the world too comes into question, but not alone; through *mesotēs* the mode of being-in-the-world as such is determined. Hence, according to this being, there is no *meson* that would be *hen kai tauton pasin*, one and the same for all — while with a *pragma kath' auto*, a line or two numbers, one and the same remains the *meson* (4 is always double 2, equidistant from 2 and 6). For the human's being there is no such *meson*, because every human *meson* is *pros hēmas*. For our being, characterized by *respectiveness*, no one-time, absolute norm can be given. It matters to form the human's being so that it is put into the fitness to *hold the middle* — which means nothing but to seize the moment. It matters *when* (one must), *and on what*, *and toward whom*, *and for the sake of what*, *and how* one must. Against this manifoldness of being-determinations, one must hold the middle — no arithmetical or geometrical middle, middle now taken in the sense of *hexis* as *taxis*: the distributedness of what comes into question for a decision, a distribution springing from the resolve itself. The middle here is no fixed property but a mode of comporting-oneself in the world. *Aretē* Aristotle calls *tou mesou stochastikē*, aiming as the middle-holding, being-oriented on the right distribution, the right seizing of the moment. *Mesotēs*: *hexis blepousa*, the composure that *sees* and is open for the situation.

Aristotle characterizes *aisthēsis* itself (*De an.* B 11) as a *mesotēs*, a *meson* of the character of *kritikon*, being-able-to-set-off against one another. Seeing colors is always setting-off a definite color against another; being-able-to-see must be a possibility not related to one object of the circle but able to see toward both ends, dark–light, and thereby toward the whole stretch of color-manifoldness — a being-placed toward the possible objects that is a *dynamis* in the sense of the *kritikē*. Taking-in stands to the objects in the peculiar situation of *being-free for them*; in this freedom is a definite being-oriented from the two ends. From this use of *meson* it becomes clear that it is no circumscribed property but primarily related to being-oriented in the world.

**γ) The Orientation of *aretē* on the Moment (*kairos*).** *Aretē* is a *hexis proairetikē*, standing in a *mesotēs pros hēmas*, delimited *logō*. In this sense *aretē* as *mesotēs* is one that is delimited, delimits itself, *through speaking* with the world, in the manner of deliberating the moment in advance, through the how of talking-through the circumstances — now, in this moment, over against this definite human, this comes into question — so that in this delimitation the right distributedness of the moment results. From *mesotēs* and *aretē* so understood, it is plain that it is mistaken to grasp *aretē* as a *skill* (*Fertigkeit*); that contradicts the sense of *aretē*.

What does it mean to come into a definite *hexis*? *Hexeis* are not properties brought by nature but have a definite *genesis*, *di' ethous*. Habituation is the way we come to *hexis*, to *aretē*. At the start of B 1 Aristotle distinguishes: the *dianoetic* *aretē* has its more from *teaching*, hence needs *experience and time*; the *ethical* comes to be *from habituation*. *Ethizein*: bringing-oneself-into-a-definite-possibility through *often-living-through*. The possibility is each time a definite one, e.g. for a *poiēsis* the appropriation of a technique; or the possibility for *praxis* — *praxis* not in the wider sense "action" but as a determination of the human's being. *Poiēsis* and *praxis* are two possibilities perhaps designating only two different modes of appropriation. Aristotle speaks of the *grammatikos*: one can write correctly, at first by chance or with another's help — but the chance writer cannot simply write; he must write as the *technē* demands, not by chance but by rule, and from himself. In practice, in often-living-through, it matters to *switch off* the orientation on the rule more and more; drill has the sense of bringing deliberation to lapse, since with skill it matters to reach a result. In *technē* the *ergon* is decisive; taking care of it aims at its turning out right, at the production going smoothly. But with an action — in the narrower sense against *poiēsis* — it matters by its sense *not* that it simply run off and a result arise, but the decisive thing is the *proairesis*, the manner of resolving. To action belongs that it springs each time from a resolve; the action has its *telos* in the *kairos*; it goes *through* deliberation and is enacted as such — in the *orthotēs* of the deliberation the action is enacted. In drill the possibility of acting, the deliberating and resolving, the how of acting, is switched off — just what matters. Bringing oneself into the possibility of right acting thus cannot mean appropriating a skill. The manner of habituation with action is not drill but *repetition*. Repetition does not mean bringing a fixed skill into play but acting anew in each moment out of the corresponding resolve. In forming the *hexis* it never comes to a routine, a *Betrieb*; in routine the moment is destroyed. Every skill as a fixed routine fails over against the moment. Appropriation and formation of the *hexis* through habituation means nothing but *right repetition*. Hence Aristotle sharply distinguishes *aretē* and acting from every *technē*, though he first sets both together in delimiting both against *epistēmē*. To appropriation *ek didaskalias* belong *empeiria* and *chronos*. *Epistēmē*, science, is for Aristotle a definite *hexis*, a definite being-placed toward the beings as such such that I know my way about them; this *hexis* carries in itself an extent of concrete knowledge, which by its content can be conveyed only bit by bit, needing a definite duration. But with *prattein*, acting, and already with *poiein*, producing, it matters to form the matter as such in *praxis* and *technē* — not to take up definite material but to form the *how* of the dealing itself. The difference: with *praxis* it turns on the *how*, appropriated only so that the human puts himself in the state of being composed for every moment — not routine but *keeping-oneself-free*, *dynamis* in the *mesotēs*. All human life cannot constantly be there; the possibilities a human existence disposes over are, in the stretch of *Dasein*, not constantly there — they lose themselves, fall away, and need ever new and constantly repeated appropriation. All acting, all not-acting, is oriented on the *mesotēs*, which Aristotle stresses is hard to find and very easy to miss — the swings lie near, the middle must be seized ever anew. This closes §17 and prepares §18's direct analysis of *pathos*.

### § 18. *pathos*. Its General Meanings and Its Role in Human *Dasein* (Met. Δ 21, De an. A 1)

#### a) The *hexis* as Thread for Grasping the Being-Structure of *pathos*

From the understanding of *hexis* and its *genesis*, we take that it cannot be grasped as a skill in the sense of routine. From there we already see more sharply what comes into question with the *pathē*: they too are characters that more closely determine, in their way, being-in-the-world, being-in-the-moment. Not "psychical states" with "bodily concomitants" — the *pathē* characterize the *whole human* in his disposedness in the world. The whole human is primarily the object of Aristotle's *psychology* in *De anima* I; the whole of the human is to be understood with respect to his being as *zōē*, as being-in-a-world — so grasped, properly the theme not of psychology but of the discussion of the being of this being. As example Heidegger will take fear. Fear as anxiety is, for the Greeks, co-constitutive of the manner of grasping what is and what is not; Aristotle sees the phenomenon of fear so widely that he became aware there is a fear even when nothing is there that would be an immediate occasion of fear — *fear before the nothing*. From this it becomes intelligible how the Greek sees being properly in the present, takes care of being as presentness.

From the orientation of *aretē* on the fundamental concept of being, Aristotle determines *aretē* as *hexis proairetikē*, standing in a *mesotēs pros hēmas*, delimited *logō* and as the *phronimos* would delimit it. *Aretē* is a composure in being-able-to-resolve; *hexis* in its determination toward *proairesis*, toward the moment, is further interpreted as *mesotēs* — "middle" *pros hēmas*: how the world stands to us, how we are in it, being-able-to-hold-the-middle in resolving, in having-present the decisive moment. In talking-through, this situation delimits itself in various respects. Even in *aretē* as composure the determination of *legein* appears: this *horizesthai logō* is more precisely determined to forestall confusion with a theoretical determining of a matter — it is such a *horizesthai* as a *phronimos* would do, *legein* as *legein* of the *phronimos*, seeing not merely as looking-at that brings states of affairs to relief but seeing the world as circumspection, looking-about in it, primarily as the circumspection in resolving. Being-in-care about *Dasein* has its manner of sight in *phronēsis*; hence the *legein* corresponds to *phronēsis*, is *meta* with reference to it. Considered so, *aretē* is characterized as *ousia*, insofar as its being makes up the human's *Dasein*. With respect to the possibility of acting, of comporting, expressed in *aretē*, *aretē* is not a *mesotēs* but a peak, the highest, *akrotēs*: purely ontologically, in the *ousia*, *aretē* is *mesotēs*; with respect to the possibility it carries, to the *eu*, it is *akrotēs*.

The ethical *aretē* has, according to its being-character, a quite specific *genesis*, characterized against the *dianoetic aretē*, the being-able-to-be-composed in the world as more enlightened about looking-about in it. *Aretē* is related to *praxis*, the ethical *aretē* to *ethos*: its *genesis* is habituation as often-living-through. The dianoetic *aretē* — science as possessing a definite subject-domain — "needs experience and time." That does not say the ethical needs no time, but *chronos* is here taken as *duration*: the duration in which I appropriate factual knowledge is co-constitutive for forming composure as knowing-one's-way. The time-character of the ethical lies in the *often* (*pollakis*). Aristotle brings *aretē ēthikē* etymologically together with *ethos*; *ethos* expresses at once the *genesis*. That duration is precisely *not* constitutive for the ethical *aretē* shows in Aristotle's stress that the human wins proper composure as a *man* — not in youth, not in old age, where the longest time has elapsed. (On the ages of life, Rhet. B 12–15.) The oldest do not, by temporal stretch, have the possibility of being properly in the *hexis*, though with *epistēmē* this is already possible. The *hexis*, related to the *pathē*, is our thread for grasping the being-structure of the *pathē* themselves.

#### b) The Four General Meanings of *pathos*

From *Met.* Δ 21: first, *pathos* is *poiotēs* according to which alteration is possible — white and black, sweet and bitter, heaviness and lightness — not any equipment as such but a so-characterized constitution that in itself offers the possibility of turning over. This characterizes the being as something that can be *affected*: something can *happen* to the so-being. "Happen" hits the proper sense of *paschein* and *pathos*; Aristotle sees in *pathos* the fact of *movement*, less the passive than that something *happens with me*. Here *pathos* in the widest, flattest meaning: possibility of *alloiōsis*, becoming-other; a determination of the being with the character of changeability. Second, of these, the *energeiai* and *alloiōseis* themselves — a being that carries the possibility of something happening with it, now taken insofar as the happening itself is taken in its being as *pathos*: the *energeia*, the being-there of such a turning-over happening-with. Third, still more, the *harmful* alterations and movements, and most of all the *painful* harms — *pathos* narrows: the happening-with has the character of the unpleasant, the *blaberon*; what happens to me is in its happening detrimental, and most so the detriment bearing on *lypē*, so that my *attunement* is affected — an alteration in the sense of being-depressed. Fourth, in a pointed sense *pathos* names the *magnitude*, the extent, of what happens to me, what befalls me to my hurt: "such-and-such is a *blow* for me."

From the four meanings the proper relatedness of *pathos* becomes visible: related to the being of the living thing, characterized by an ever-so-finding-itself. The happening-with-one hits and strikes one into this disposedness, and has in itself the character of the detrimental. But the happening need not, as *paschein*, have the character of detriment, of *phthora*; Aristotle knows a *metabolē*, *kinēsis*, *alloiōsis* where *paschein* has the character of *sōtēria*: something happens with me such that this experiencing or undergoing has the character of *sōzein* — by something's meeting me, I am not destroyed but first come into the proper state, the possibility that was in me becomes properly actual. Hegel took the phenomenon of *sōzein* under the term "Aufhebung" from Aristotle. When one who knows a matter, who is in possession of a knowledge, from this composure actually makes the subject-domain present, sees it bodily, then a certain *kinēsis*, *metabolē* is to be noted, a turning-over we cannot well call becoming-other — or, if one would call it becoming-other, one would have to introduce a new *genos* of *alloiōsis*; as little as one could say the builder becomes, by building, another — he becomes just what he is. Against this *metabolē*, in which the *hexis* is saved, brought to just what it should be, there is a *paschein* of the character of *sterētikon*: something happens with me whereby I *lose* the *hexis*, e.g. growing old. *Pathos* is thus what makes me lose a matter, and an "Aufhebung," saving — Aufhebung in the sense of preserving, lifting-up to the higher proper being of *energeia*.

#### c) *pathos* as the Being-Carried-Off of Human *Dasein* in Its Full Bodily Being-in-the-World

*Pathos* related to *zōē praktikē meta logou* is a being-carried-off (*Mitgenommenwerden*) of *Dasein*. It is carried off by what is there with it in the world — from outside, but from outside as the *world* that is the *wherein* of my being. From *Dasein* itself arise the possibilities and modes of its being-carried-off. This being-carried-off of *Dasein* as being-in-its-world concerns not something we might call the "psychical," to which the conception of *pathos* as affect invites, but a being-carried-off of the being as living as such. Strictly, I cannot say the soul hopes, fears, pities; I can only ever say the human hopes, is courageous. To say the soul grows angry is like saying the soul builds a house; better to say not that the soul pities or learns or thinks, but *the human, by the soul* — soul here grasped as *ousia*, insofar as in the *pathē* is expressed the being-carried-off of the being as living. The theme of what Aristotle names *Peri psychēs*, an ontology of the being, is precisely the human. Hence the *pathē* are not "psychical lived-experiences," not "in consciousness," but a being-carried-off of the human in his full being-in-the-world. So the so-called "bodily states" in anxiety, joy, and the like are no concomitants but belong to the characteristic being of the being, the human.

Aristotle discusses (*De an.* A 1) what really is the object of an inquiry *Peri psychēs* and in what role the *pathē* stand — and thereby the *pathē*, the modes in which a living thing is carried off: it holds an *aporia* whether the *pathē* of the soul are all *common* to what has them (to the *anthrōpos*: *koina* here means the whole human) or whether some are *idion* to the soul itself. This must necessarily be brought to clarity (odd use of *labein*), but it is not easy. Most (appeal to the average experience of the human of *Dasein*) hold that in all courage, and in general in all taking-in, the body is in some way co-involved (*aisthanesthai* here not perception in the narrow sense but taking-in as having-the-world-there, being-open for what is around me). Most of all *noein* seems an *idion* of the soul (the pure contemplating, e.g. the purely mathematical, in which the body as such is not involved). But if *noein* too is a kind of *phantasia* or cannot be without *phantasia*, then thinking too could not be without standing in connection with the whole life of the human. Thinking: not recourse to a brain-process, but to *phantasia*, the making-present of the world in which the made-present is not actually there but, e.g., in memory or bare pale presentification. Even in thinking about something, the matters are there in presentification; *phantasia* is the ground for *noein*. Insofar as *noēsis* is the highest possibility for the human's being, the whole being of the human is so determined that it must be grasped as the *bodily* being-in-the-world of the human.

Heidegger stresses that what Aristotle gives here is *not yet exhausted today* — only phenomenology has begun with it. No separation of "psychical" and "bodily acts"! This is to be seen practically, e.g. how I move my hand. One must see that the primary *Dasein*-function of bodiliness secures the *ground for the full being of the human*. In Aristotle are also already the beginnings of the misorientation into the biological in the whole tradition (Descartes: *res cogitans* — *res extensa*).

Behind this quite general question stands the phenomenon Aristotle names *nous*. The concrete question (posed but not decided, *De an.* Γ 4–5): by what, in the proper sense, is the human's being as being-in-the-world determined; whether the human's being as having-the-world-disclosed-there, uncoveredness, openness of being-in-the-world, is determined by *nous*, and how; whether this determinedness is to be grasped so that *nous* belongs to the human's being, goes up in it, or so that *nous* comes *into* the human from outside, so that the human's being is only a definite possibility of openness that *nous* guarantees; whether there are *pathē* that, beyond the concrete being of the human, still have in themselves a characteristic being. Aristotle compares *nous* with *phōs*, light. As through light a color first comes to its *Da-sein*, is in its *Da* insofar as it stands in brightness — being-there as the characteristic illumination — so every being-there needs a fundamental illumination to be there. The being itself as being-there must have the possibility of being-disclosed; this possibility is nothing but *nous*. The ground-determination of *nous*, of the "meaning" of something, is the *dynaton*, the possibility as such of being-disclosed, of the *Da* of something — in it every concrete grasping moves and holds itself. As such *nous* is *apathēs*, that to which nothing can happen, but which is the condition of the possibility that anything meets the living thing at all, that anything is there for life. So *nous*, with respect to the openness of in-being, is *more* than the human can be; for the way the human seizes this possibility, *nous*, is *dianoeisthai*. Insofar as *nous* makes up the openness of the human, it is a *Da* insofar as life is determined by *lypē* and *hēdonē*. *Nous* is the ground-condition of the possibility of being-in-the-world, which as such towers beyond the respective concrete being of the individual human. Aristotle holds, in this explication (Γ 3–5), wholly within description; this doctrine of *nous* is no theory but grows from concrete experience — he carried it only as far as he in fact saw the matter, and let the inquiry into *nous* stand because factually he came no further. As this *dynaton*, *nous* is further determined as *dektikon tou eidous*, being-able-to-take-up the *eidos*, the look of a being; *nous* is the light in which the look of something is seen — what is said of brightness with respect to color (*aisthēsis*) is said of *nous* fundamentally with respect to the being-determinations of every being as such.

This concrete question Aristotle poses by way of introduction: how far *nous* belongs to the concrete being of the human or not; whether there is an *idion pathos tēs psychēs*; whether *nous* makes up the being of the living thing so as to characterize it as a proper being; whether *nous* is a *meros psychēs chōriston*, a separable part of the soul. He decides from the evidence of the eyes: a living thing, insofar as it is affected by the world, is affected also with respect to its bodiliness; everything aims at the living thing in its full *Dasein*. He shows this with respect to the human's being determined by *nous*: the human's *noein* is no pure one; the meaning of something not actually present grounds in *phantasia*, is possible only from presentification, and presentification is nothing but the *repetition* of what once was present, repetition of a past present. *Phantasia* is not necessarily memory — that is a special presentification, one in which lies the knowing of the former having-experienced of the repeated. So the human's *nous* is related to *phantasia* and thereby to *aisthēsis* and to the *paschein* of the *sōma*.

#### d) The Double Manner of Considering the *pathē* According to *eidos* and *hylē*, and the Question of the Task of the *phusikos*

For us the question is how Aristotle marks the peculiar interweaving of the human's being, in its full *Dasein*, with the *sōma* — a question that determines the manner in which the *pathē* are treated. The analysis of the *pathē* in the *Rhetoric* makes visible the *eidos* of the *pathē* without going into their peculiarity of being, as so-looking, *kinēseis tou sōmatos*, a happening at a living thing in which bodiliness too is claimed. The question whether there is an *idion pathos* of the soul as such, Aristotle first leaves open; he goes over to showing that all *pathē* are *meta sōmatos*, and shows it doubly. In all being-angry-at, being-mild-toward, fearing-before, the body is in a way co-engaged. And there shows the peculiarity that we are approached by *pathēmata*, happenings from the world, that are very strong, yet we do not fall into fear; sometimes, conversely, quite meager occasions bring us into excitement. So it does not lie exclusively in what befalls us when we fall into a *pathos*; the *genesis* of the *pathē* is also given through bodiliness. Still more clearly the *genesis* shows in that sometimes we fall into fear *without anything fearful befalling us*, so that the fearing rises, as it were, in us ourselves — in our being the possibility of fear and anxiety is co-given. This shows that bodiliness in fact speaks along in the *genesis* of the *pathē*. "If that is so, it is clear that the *pathē* are *logoi enyloi*," meanings-in-matter.

The addressing of this phenomenon, if it is to hit the *pathē* in what they are, must go to what they are *from*, wherein they find themselves. Their *hylē* is nothing but the *sōma*, the bodiliness of the human. Because the manner of inquiry into the *pathē* is such, the *horoi* that delimit each phenomenon in itself must fall out correspondingly. The *horos* of *orgē*: being-angry is something like a being-in-movement of the so-and-so-constituted body, a bodiliness finding itself in a quite definite way, or of a body-part — a quite definite movement under the pressure of such-and-such circumstances on account of such-and-such an affair. At once the *hylē* is seen (it lies in *toioude sōmatos*) and the *eidos*, the so-being of the being-approached (*hypo toude heneka toude*). Therewith the *logos* too is addressed. From this Aristotle draws a fundamental science-theoretical determination: it is already a matter for the *phusikos* to take into view what lies in the circle of the theme of the being of a living thing. *Phusikos*: one who investigates nature in the widest sense. In the phenomenon of *pathos* the *sōma* is co-constitutive, as something that carries in itself the possibility of being-in-a-world; *sōma* a quite definite *hylē*, characterized as making life possible. So the *phusikos* considers the *pathē* otherwise than the *dialektikos*: they delimit *pathē* differently, e.g. anger — the one (*dialektikos*, treating rhetoric) considers anger as *orexis antilypēseōs*, being-out-for retaliation (a certain irreconcilability as a mode of being-to-others), the *phusikos* determines anger as a definite surging of the blood at the heart and of warmth. The first *logos* gives the proper *eidos*, what it properly is; but as a determination of the human's being in the world it is necessarily co-determined by its being a *zesis*, a surging, of the blood.

There is thus a double manner of consideration, and the question is what really is the task of the *phusikos* with respect to the *psychē*. Example: the *logos* of a house — how does a "house" look? We can address it as shelter, cover, holding-off the detrimental being-affected, damage by wind, rain, and heat. Another will say: stones, bricks, wood. A third: the look, *eidos*, of this house in wood, stones, bricks, and this on account of making the needed shelter — a being-built led with a view to there being the *skepasma*. Who is the *phusikos*? The one who simply speaks about the material and takes no notice of how the material properly looks? Or the one who speaks only of the *eidos*? Or the one who speaks *ex amphoin*, from both? The right *phusikos* addresses the house so that he addresses it on the look, which look in itself has reference to what the house consists of — the one who primarily aims at what the house *is*, how constituted in itself. This decision gives Aristotle a fundamental superiority over all prior nature-consideration: the being of nature is determined in its look not simply by the *hylē* but primarily by being-moved; only the so-determined being is the proper, secure theme of the *phusikos*. He interrogates the *sōmata* with respect to their *erga* and *pathē* — *pathē* in the widest sense, the first sense of *Met.* Δ 21. He takes the *sōmata* as so-and-so-constituted: considers wood insofar as it comes into question as being-determination of the tree, co-determining the being of the plant. The *technitēs*, by contrast, considers the wood, say an oar, not as co-determining the tree but as far as it has the hardness fitting it to make a rudder. The physician considers the *sōmata* according to what he has in view in his *technē*. A further manner: wood not as so-constituted, not as trunk, not as material, but merely insofar as it is *extended* — then it is a possible object of the *mathēmatikos*. Beyond these there is the consideration that takes every being with respect to its being-determinations, not the extendedness alone, but gathers all possible beings in the fundamental question of *being as such*: this is the object of the *prōtos philosophos*.

For the being-determination of the *pathē* it is important that they are understood in themselves only if taken as *pathē* of the *sōma*; their *eidos* is primarily determined as a determination of the living thing with respect to in-being in the world. *Thymos* and *phobos* are what belongs to a quite definitely constituted body; they are "not separable." There is no such thing as a pure fear as an abstracted comportment to something; it is in itself a comportment of the full human with his bodiliness. But this not-being-able-to-be-abstracted is another than that of mathematical objectivities: the *pathē* cannot be identified with the line and surface of the body in the mathematical sense. The Greek sees a line not primarily in itself: *grammē* is always the limit of a surface, surface the limit of the body; the surface has no being without the body — here too a not-being-able-to-be-separated. So too the *eidos* of fearing has the primary relatedness to a finding-oneself of the body. The difference: the definite constitution of the *sōmata* plays no role in the mathematical non-separability (say the brown- or scratched-being of the body), while for the *pathē* the so-and-so-constituted being is essential. Both are *logoi enyloi*, but in a quite different sense.

This is the ground for the *Rhetoric*'s consideration of the *pathē* with respect to the *eidos*. Important: Aristotle wins the ground-determination of a living thing *not* from physiological considerations. The *eidos* of the *pathē* is a comporting-oneself to other humans, a being-in-the-world; from there the *hylē* of the *pathē* is first properly investigable. In *De an.* A 1 it is a matter of how far *nous*, as ground-determination of the human's being, is a ground-peculiarity of this being, and how far the human makes up only a definite possibility of the being of *nous*: the ground lies in Aristotle's seeing that *nous*, the meaning, is, against all other modes of grasping, a grasping-possibility not limited to a definite being-region like hearing, seeing, but goes to *ta panta*, grasps all possible beings, such that the being need not even be present — a universality of grasping not to be brought into one with the concrete, ever-respective being of the human. Wherein this possibility grounds, growing beyond the human and his concrete being, is the standing question; in its connection Aristotle discusses the *pathē* as the phenomena at which it shows that the concrete being of the human can be understood only if taken as *full* being — above all decisive that we come out of composure in fearing *without anything in the environment meeting us that could be the direct occasion of fear*. In this being-approached by the *pathē*, bodiliness is in some way co-affected. If that is so, the question is into which field of inquiry the being of the character of the living falls: must not the *phusikos* have the *psychē* in his theme? Indeed — insofar as for the *phusikos* every *sōma* is a *toiouton*, so-and-so determined, whence it follows that the *phusikos* is held to determine this *toiouton* in advance, to determine the *hylē* in its positive sense — just the task the earlier *phusiologoi* always neglected. This must be brought nearer from the opposite side (§19), showing how far the *phusikos* must take the *psychē* into account within certain limits, connected with the analysis of movement in *Phys.* Γ 1–3.

### § 19. The *phusikos* and His Manner of Treating the *psychē* (De part. an. A 1)

How *psychē* comes into view at all is evident from *De partibus animalium* A 1 — at once a concrete example of how Aristotle really enacts the theoretical *logos*. The treatise is called "On the Parts of Animals," but *zōon* is here taken in the widest sense as living thing, and *morion*/*meros* have a far wider sense than "part" as a quantitative piece: also "function," "accomplishment," "structural moment." The *merē* are all that makes up the joining, the being-jointed, of a definite being. *Peri zōon moriōn* means: on the jointure-and-accomplishment-nexus of the living thing as a definite being.

#### a) The Two Kinds of *hexis theōrias*: Factual Competence (*epistēmē*) and Sureness of the Manner of Treatment (*paideia*)

Aristotle begins with a fundamental consideration of the conditions of scientific research, the counterpart to what appeared at *aretē* (there *aretē* as *hexis proairetikē meta logou*, as the *phronimos* enacts it). Here he points to the *hexis theōrias*, the being-able-to-dispose over scientific research, and determines it on two sides: *epistēmē* and a certain *paideia*. The first is factual competence: to the right possibility of a researcher belongs competence over his domain. Far more decisive for Aristotle is the *paideia*, the sureness of the manner of treatment. Concerning every *theōria* and *methodos*, lower or higher, two modes of the *hexis* appear, one to be addressed as *epistēmē* of the matter, the other as a certain *paideia*. For it belongs to the educated to be able to judge *eustochōs*, on the mark, what one who lectures (*legōn* here in the sense of the one delivering a lecture) hands over of the matter rightly or not. The one who has the *hexis* of *paideia* can decide and judge — surely — how the speaker treats the matter that is theme; over the *how* of the manner of treatment the *pepaideumenos* disposes, whether it is spoken from the right ground-relation to the matter, whether the access is an original one or the speaker has it only from hearsay or by rote. Accordingly the theoretical dealing that disposes over *paideia* is able to proceed, in all or in definite possibilities of research, *with the right methodical instinct* — not the being-run-into an already-given method as technique, but the *hexis*, the freedom, the peculiarly superior openness for a definite factual content and objective domain. One with the right instinct, the right *paideia*, will at once be able to decide whether it makes sense for someone to treat logic mathematically, or the history of Christianity with categories of art history, setting up "types of piety" — he will rather see that the person has understood nothing yet of Christianity. This *hexis* is today wholly neglected, hard to appropriate, harder to convey; just this determination of the *hexis* of *paideia* shows the absolute sureness with which Aristotle presents his researches and how he proceeds against the tradition.

#### b) The Decisive *paideia* in Investigating the *phusei ginomena*. The *hou heneka* as *logos*, the Primary Respect

What is the decisive *paideia* in investigating *phusis*? The one wholly educated (*holōs pepaideumenos*) has the instinct such that even without the relevant factual competence he notices whether the person recites something or stands to the matter; beside the *holōs* is one restricted to a domain, having the corresponding sureness in his field. Aristotle discusses the *paideia* insofar as it bears on the *historia peri phusin* — *historia* meaning: getting-oriented, the primary looking-about at how *phusis* really looks. Two chief questions. First: whether in investigating a domain one must first, as it were, register — occupy oneself first with the *phainomena*, how the things look, as what they primarily give themselves — and then ask how far they are so and so, behave so and so; or whether the order of questions should be otherwise (as the ancients, who speculated about the *archai* of the world without knowing what they meant by the world — whether one should begin with theory, with what one has thought up offhand about a matter, or first look to the matter itself). Second: since to every inquiry belongs the setting-out of the *dia ti*, and since with respect to the being called "nature" there are two possibilities of the *dia ti* — the *hou heneka* and the *hothen hē archē tēs kinēseōs*, the whence of movement — which of the causes is the primary respect. Both questions are to be decided *from the matter itself*; I cannot systematically think up the question but can only decide it from the matter.

Aristotle gives the decision with respect to the *phusei onta* as *zōa*: the *hou heneka* is the first *dia ti* — on the ground of setting out the look of the being of the living thing, the first question must be directed at the *hou heneka*. The grounding: the *hou heneka* is a *logos*, and the *logos* is *archē* alike in the field of beings of production and in the field of beings that stand together *phusei*. The question from what the being determines itself, in what respect it is first to be taken, is decided by the recourse to *logos*. *Logos* means "speaking" and "the spoken" — the ground-determination of *apophainesthai*: the spoken is what is pointed out from the addressed, i.e. in *logos* the addressed, this being, is uncovered, pointed out. Heidegger takes *logos* in this doubleness deliberately: first, *logos*, *legein* in the sense of going-toward-something and pointing-out, *logos* in the access-sense; second, *logos* as the spoken as such, in which lies the being that is addressed — and in this second sense he renders *logos* as *Anspruch*, "claim/what-a-thing-yields-on-address." In German too "addressing" (*ansprechen*) is used in a definite sense — one says of an apparatus "it responds," "gives back" on being called. *Logos* in the access-sense: to point out a matter so and so, to call on it so and so; in the addressing the so-called matter responds, in the pointing-out it shows itself as it is. It matters how a matter is called upon, so that it responds rightly from itself. *Logos* in this second sense is the claim, the factual content a matter yields on being called; so *logos* very often becomes identical with *eidos*: *logos* is the claim, what the matter yields, and in the right addressing it yields how it looks, what it is.

The *hou heneka* is the *logos*, and because the *logos* is the *archē*, the *hou heneka* is the first respect. When it is said the *hou heneka* is the *logos* of a matter, this is meant from a definite mode of encounter: the *telos*. *Telos* is the proper *logos*. *Telos* is not "purpose" but "being-finished," "end." The *synestēkos*, the standing-there as finished, is the proper sense of the being-there of a being; addressed in its being-finished, it gives the right claim. *Telos* as being-finished is that at which production has its end; *telos* as the finished-of-production is that on account of which the production is so and so. Seen on the way of a being's coming-into-being-there, the *telos* is the *hou heneka*. Here *telos* is a *phainomenon* — no apparatus, but the being-finished-there, as the animal moves; with respect to *telos* it is primarily experienced that it comes to its being *in Dasein itself*. Not "goal-strivingness," no dark "teleology."

Heidegger names the fourfold aim of this chapter: discussion of *phusis*; how far the *psychē* falls into the *phusikos*' field; an orientation about the concrete enactment of the *logos theōrētikos* — *alētheia*; and an insight into what the whole course aims at — what *ousia* and *on* mean. *Ousia* was taken from its current sense: the present-being-there, the available, "goods and chattels" — the meaning of being as *being-present*, *Dasein* in the present. This meaning gets sharper light insofar as we can show what the *Da* means for the Greeks: having-come-into-the-*Da*, and indeed through *production*; *her-stellen*, to bring into the *Da*, into the present — the proper sense of *poiēsis*. Being-there is in the proper sense being-produced, i.e. being-finished-there, having-come-to-an-end; *telos* = *peras*. These are threads for the ground-sense of Greek ontology, later effective in the Greeks' succession such that the original sense of being covers itself over and becomes a mere word-sense. The primary sense of *ousia* is "goods and chattels": what is produced from wood, stone, and set on the ground (which too is *phusei on*) is *technē on* — first the *pragmata* and *chrēmata* (what I have to do with, what stands at my disposal, in use in practical life); second the *phusei onta* as *ginomena*; third the *phusei onta* as *aei*. The being-characters are intelligible only from the sense of *Dasein* as being-produced. The *pragmata* are there insofar as produced in *technē*; the *phusei onta* are what is there in self-producing, needing no production by others — there just as the *pragmata*, but their *genesis* in turn has the character of the *Da* (a plant grows up and brings forth others). And finally there is being-there that is *phusei on* as *aei*, needing no production, so there that it need not be produced — properly there, but intelligible only from production. The ground of the being is production; and it is to be seen how *logos* is the possibility of gaining access to being in this sense of being-finished-there.

#### c) The Determination of the Self-Standing *logos* Related to the *phusei ginomena*

The whole discussion moves in the question *pōs lekteon?* — how must one speak? — taking up the thesis that the *hou heneka* is the *prōton* because it is the *logos*. Here it is a matter of a proper *logos*, the *logos* of *theōria*. We know already that *logos* has a fundamental significance in the human's being: *zōē praktikē meta logou*, enacted in addressing and talking-through the world; in *legein* the being of the world and *Dasein* itself as life come to interpretation, according to the extent to which the being moves in the world. Speaking is the constitutive enactment-mode of concernful dealing. For the human's *Dasein* there is the possibility that this definite *legein*, in concernful dealing, *abstracts from concern* in the sense of *poiēsis*, of directing having-to-do; *praxis* can lose the character of *poiēsis*, need not have the character of acting, can take on the character of the mere treating-of-something in the sense of *treatise* (*Abhandeln*). The *logos* becomes *self-standing*, itself becomes the *praxis*. This mode of dealing is *theōria*, no longer looking-about with a view to..., but looking-at, grasping things in their being and being-there. So the theoretical, science, grows as a possibility *out of Dasein itself* — a ground-fact to be kept in view.

The question is how the *logos*, insofar as it becomes self-standing (*logoi* used in the sense of "treatise"), looks in this pointed application to the being-region of the *phusei onta* as *ginomena*. Aristotle proceeds cautiously and finds not the mode of *legein* but the nearest-known, from which he tries to clarify the peculiar *logos* of *theōria*. A nexus is to be exhibited that has a definite kinship with the *logos* of *theōria*: this familiar mode of dealing kindred to *theōria* is *technē*, a *poiēsis*, a producing led and guided by a knowing-one's-way (the building of a house guided by the building-management). Why just this dealing? That *poiēsis* is the nearest is clear; but one must see that the being of *poiēsis* has a peculiar kinship with the *phusei onta* as *ginomena*. The *phusei onta* are precisely such as we do *not* produce but that are already there for us, already in the world, yet so that they have to do with producing in the manner of a *self-producing* — there in self-producing. This is a quite primary finding. Aristotle asks: how does the *logos* in *technē* look, and how accordingly will the *logos* as mere contemplating look? Three things: how Aristotle characterizes *technē*, the *erga technēs*, and the *logos* of *technē*; as what the *phusei onta* as *ginomena* primarily show themselves as *phainomena*; and how the ancients saw the *phusei onta*, or what they failed to see in them — from Aristotle's exhibition of the failure, what matters to him becomes visible.

**α) The *erga technēs* and the *logos* of *technē*.** Whether the physician or the building-master be at it through deliberation (deliberative presentification of the being he deals with) or through *aisthēsis* (concrete exemplification by having already seen a house), in both cases they yield the *logos* — how the matter responds, how it looks — the *logos* of each thing they are making, and thereby yield that the thing is to be brought about so and so. From the *logos* the "therefore" is set out; the *logos* is the *hou heneka*: because it is so and so, it must be brought about so and so — i.e. the *genesis* is intelligible only from the *telos*. It will matter to understand the *phusei onta* from their *logos*; in this connection *psychē* arises, as it is theme of the *phusikos*. The two determinations — being-present and being-produced — make the Greek being-concept intelligible. Aristotle draws out the *Dasein*-characters, the mode of presence and the mode of being-produced: setting out the characteristic being-moments of being as living in nature; the phenomenal fact the Greeks name *psychē* — *phainomenon psychēs* — must become visible if soul belongs to the being, i.e. is in the world.

The nexus of the *erga technēs* and the *logos*: the *technē* gives, in its primary deliberation, the look the to-be-produced should have, the *poiēteon*, the gait of production. The *logos* is characterized by the *if–so*: if such-and-such is to be finished, then such-and-such must happen. In this *if–so* lies co-enclosed that from the look of the to-be-produced a definite *whence* of producing is demanded, a definite *hylē* (for a shoe I need such-and-such leather). The whence of producing is itself there in this definite availability, prescribed from what is to be there as finished: it is necessary that a so-constituted *hylē* be present in advance if there is to be a house or another being in its being-finished, and the becoming and being-moved (the gait of production) must be first this step, then that, and so on up to the *telos*; from the how of the look of the finished being, in its anticipatedness, are prescribed the gait, sequence, and direction of production. Therewith the *genesis* is in itself founded in the *telos*: because the look of the house is such, the production must be such — for the becoming is *for the sake of* the being-present (the finished being-there), and not the reverse. In short: *technē* (not the producing itself, but a kind of *epistēmē*, knowing-one's-way about the to-be-produced) is a *logos* of the *ergon* which is without the *hylē*. Heidegger notes his own correction here (having in the previous session mistakenly referred the neuter *ho* to *logos*; the text has *ho aneu hylēs*, so *aneu hylēs* refers to the *ergon*). The *ergon* is seen in advance; so far as seen in advance it is not yet produced. The *logos* is *logos ergou* — the work is talked-through in advance as finished, so the whole nexus of producing is talked through, and thereby in this *logos* the *hylē* is precisely, in a definite way, co-talked-through. *Aneu hylēs* must not mislead one into taking the *eidos* as something non-sensible; the *hylē* is in the *ergon*, but not in the genuine relation. The primary comportment to the whence of producing is the grasping producing itself; the *logos* does not contain this producing but springs ahead of it, and only so is it the prescription of the gait and direction, bringing production into its right possibility. Precisely because being-there means being-finished, being-produced, every production must be founded by the *eidos*. This "looking-so," anticipated in *technē*, is what determines the finished being-there in its being-there, characterizes it in its being-there-as-house — i.e. the being-there of a being is in itself co-determined by *what it is*. The Greeks determine being in the sense of being-there fundamentally through *what*-being — not as a being for itself, but as Aristotle takes *what*-being as constitutive for the being-there itself. In seeing the house as it looks, one sees it not isolated, not in this or that moment, at this time of day, under this lighting, inhabited by these people, but this being-there as one sees it *in averageness* as house, as one daily lives in it and sees it, this *what* in the averageness of the present of this being in dealing. What averagely comes to meet in its look makes up the being-there. It is senseless to ask in what sense the Greeks grasped "individual" being as concrete *Dasein*-determination; the Greek does not come at all to see in this *hic et nunc* the proper *Da*.

**β) The Being-Characters of the *phusei ginomena*.** How does Aristotle, on the thread of the *erga technēs*, determine and see the *phusei onta* as living? How does the *phusei on* show itself? Everywhere we address an encountering thing as *tode toude heneka*, this on account of that, wherever there shows something like a *telos* toward which the movement comes to its end, *nothing hindering*, so that it is unhindered. Primary experience is the seeing of a finished-being self-moving; the constitutive moment: something is in movement, so that it comes to an end. Clear, then, that this is what we address as *phusis*. The ground-fact characterizing the sense of *phusis* as a mode of being-there is a finished-being in which being-finished, being-become, is *taken up in its provenance-from... as a self-producing*. Further: the *hou heneka* and the *kalon* are found more in the *erga* of *phusis* than in those of *technē*. The sense of *kalon* is related to the *mēdenos empodizontos* — *kalon*: the beautiful, what has succeeded and in this succeeding is so there that no failure is present. *Technē* as handicraft-making is characterized by having to try, the material can be unsuitable, it needs such-and-such circumstances and chances to succeed; whereas the *phusei onta* go smoothly and are there in this smooth-having-gone, *kalon*. This experience of the *kalon* led the ancients to address this *phusei on*, which always "works," as *anankaion*, such that in principle nothing can come between. But there is a difference in necessity, a double *anankaion*: *anankaion haplōs* and *anankaion ex hypotheseōs*. Simply necessary is what always is, excluding in itself that it ever became — the ever-being excludes having-become, a being-there needing no becoming, incompatible with it; this ever-so-being-there is the simply necessary. Beside it a necessity in the being that just is *through* having-become, playing itself out in the *if–so*, *ex hypotheseōs*: if such-and-such is to become, then under this presupposition such-and-such must necessarily happen. In short: *phusis makes everything it is for the sake of something* — always within the manner of consideration shown above; something is finished on the way over a movement does not mean some dark "teleology." So Aristotle can determine the *phusei on* as an *esomenon*, a being that has its being in becoming-so, by, as it were, running ahead of itself.

**γ) Critique of the Manner of Consideration of the Old Physiologists.** These ground-determinations of the *phusei on* are what the ancients first missed in their consideration of nature; accordingly they could not see rightly the special being of nature as living. What they primarily saw was: the being *moves*. But the fact that I see a moved thing and address it as moved does not yet say I see the *movement*; it does not yet give the possibility of lifting out the movement of this moved as a being-determination. The old and first to philosophize about nature considered the *hylikē archē*, the from-whence, addressed as a *hylikon*, determined by the *hylē*. In the field of *technē*: a table as present is addressed, when its being-there is asked after, as wood — this being-there, the table, is wood-being. They saw the being primarily with respect to *what it consists of*, and shaped the further inquiry accordingly: asking what sets in movement, they answered — this wood-being; believing to answer the question "what is the being-there?" So they will never get an answer to the question of the being-there and the being-characters of the table, since wood-being determines also the chair or the cupboard.

Aristotle talks through the ancients' manner so as to approach more and more the right respect in which the *phusei on* is to be discussed; within the proper respect and what it yields, we meet the *psychē*. He then makes his self-correction on *aneu hylēs* (above), stressing that the *hylē* is co-talked-through in the *logos ergou*, that the *eidos* is not something non-sensible, and that the *hylē* is in the *ergon* though not in the genuine relation. From the nexus of *ergon*, *hylē*, *logos*, and *poiēsis*, the way *hylē* itself is determined is to be seen: *hylē* is by no means mere non-being, *mē on*, indeterminate stuff or a limit for the form. *Hylē* is precisely the *determinate*: this wood has just the suitability on the ground of which it comes into question as *hylē* for such-and-such. *Hylē* is *dynamis*, the positive possibility for such-and-such, first made visible from the *eidos*. Hence Aristotle: one must address primarily the look of each being-there and it insofar as it has a look; the material, what the being consists of, is never to be addressed at itself. With respect to *logos*, then, the *hylē* is un-self-standing, must first be disclosed from the *eidos*. This *lekteon* governs the present consideration too: when nature is investigated, it must be interrogated primarily according to its look; only when the *eidos* is made visible is there the possibility of investigating the whence of consisting, the whence of arising, the *genesis*. So the *eidos*, the *ousia*, is the foundation for the *genesis*.

On this thread Aristotle discusses the ancients more precisely — how far they took the *phusei onta* into view. It is not enough to interrogate the *phusei onta* by what they consist of (*to ek tinōn*: fire, water, earth, air); one must also interrogate the *eidos*, just as in *technē* the grasping of the material does not suffice without prescription of the material from the *eidos*. A thing like a bedstead is a so-looking *en tōde*, in such-and-such; and one must say *poion tēn idean* — the *schēma*, the outline, figure, is to be determined as what sort it is in look. *Idea* — the only use of the word in Aristotle is *idea* and *eidos*; *idea* means nothing but "look" (a front against Plato): the outline of a being, how constituted in its look — not how it exists supersensibly somewhere, but the outline as it shows itself. For *phusis*, the being-there of natural things taken with respect to the *morphē* (= *schēma*), is superior to the natural things taken with respect to the *hylikē phusis*, the whence of their consisting; with the *morphē*, the *schēma*, I win the being of natural things properly.

Aristotle notes that Democritus in fact first pursued this question, going after the *schēma* and *chrōma* (figure and color) as what determines a *phusei on* in its being. Democritus rightly says the human is, with respect to its look, what it is — known to us by color and figure. But Aristotle objects that the *dead* human too has the same look and figure and is precisely *not* human; so the look-determination as *schēma* and *chrōma* does not suffice. Further, a hand cannot be what it is if it arises from anything whatever, e.g. bronze or wood: a wooden hand is no hand, looks just the same and by Democritus' determination would be a hand, but cannot enact its specific accomplishment — as a flute of stone is no flute, one cannot play on it. A wooden hand does not live, is not there as hand. To the *eidos* belong also the *dynamis* and the *ergon*: the constitutive for the *Da*-character of the living is the *ergon*, the accomplishment, from which the whence of consisting is determined. A hand cannot consist of wood; it needs a *sōma toiouton*; the *hylē* must satisfy the characteristic accomplishment of the hand as *morion* of the *zōon*. So Democritus and the ancients spoke "too simply" — as if a carpenter (*tektōn*) were to speak about a wooden hand, treating of something that looks like a hand but is none. The physiologists give the *geneseis* and *aitiai* of the *schēma*, saying "by such-and-such powers it was fashioned" — but if a carpenter is asked after the *genesis* of what he made, from what powers and means, it will not suffice to answer "by a tool (a hammer) falling on it" it became so-looking. So the ancients speak of nature: by so-looking things falling together it became so. The carpenter understands his being much better: he will say *why* he led the blow so and *for the sake of what* (with what in view he led the hammer), will speak of the cause in which the *morphē* became so-and-so-determined; the *technē* determines his *poiēsis*, and the *technē* has its ground in the anticipation of the *eidos*.

So too with the living, one must rightly ask after the *telos*, hence after the *dynamis* and the *ergon*. It is true the ancients did not rightly address the living. "If, then, that which determines the look of a living thing so that hand is as hand — if that at the end is what we call soul, then the *phusikos* must necessarily treat of the soul" (if he would treat the living as a being-there). The question is only whether of the whole soul or only certain parts.

Heidegger carries this to the end and returns to the *pathē*: if the *pathē* are to become the object of an inquiry, then with the *pathē* as disposedness of the living, in which at once bodiliness is co-approached, first the *eidos* must be taken into view. The proper being-there must be set out to be able, possibly, to study also the "physiological," the "bodily states." So the orientation in considering the somatic is given by the *eidos* of human life, characterized as *zōē praktikē meta logou*.

#### d) The Double Proof for the Limited Domain of the *phusikos*

Aristotle worked out, in criticizing prior nature-philosophy, the ground-determinations decisive for the natural being of the character of life: it is not enough to come, beyond the *hylē*, to grasping the *schēma* (in a sense already the *idea*); one who stops there fails. In the *idea* life is characterized as *dynamis*, that it *can* something, and thereby the *hylē*, as co-determining the being, gets its characterization: the hand is hand only as living, insofar as it can grasp and feel, and such a hand demands that its *hylē* be a definite one, organized, characterized by the definite being of the *being-able*; the *hylē* is the "flesh," *sarx*. What the ancients did not heed shows itself as the *being-able* that determines the being of the *hylē* — what we call soul. The question arises: has the *phusikos* to investigate the being of life in all its possibilities and its whole stretch, or is only a definite section of the living theme for him? Aristotle decides this doubly: indirectly, and from the finding of how the natural being shows itself as living. Both times he recurs to the same finding of the self-showing of the being.

**α) Indirect Proof.** If the *phusikos* had to investigate *all* the living with respect to its being-alive, then beside *phusikē*, this science, there would be *no philosophy*. This rests on a definite presupposition: for *noein* and understanding go to the *noēton*, the thinkable, the whole field of possible taking-in, which is *panta* — all that in any sense is, is *noēton*. So the object of *phusikē epistēmē* would be all beings whatever. Under what presupposition is this conclusive? Only if, for Aristotle, to the consideration of a being of the character of the living belongs necessarily the consideration of the being at which the living, on the ground of its being-possibility, stands, on which it is related as its dealing-with-which. Only if life originally means being-in-a-world, being in the manner of being-at-something (*Dabeisein*), and the "at-which" is the world in which the living is — only then is the consideration conclusive. If *nous* fell into the consideration, if *nous* were object, then all *noēta* too would be object of *phusikē epistēmē*. It is the affair of one and the same science to treat of being in the sense of taking-in, thinking, and of being in the sense of the thinkable, if thinking and thinkable are toward-one-another, *pros allēla* (*pros* with the accusative: "toward" something, "over against" it). Thinking is nothing but this *pros*; thinking demands by its being: to be *open toward the other*; its being cannot be understood, primarily seen, if the toward-which is not there, on which it as taking-in, fearing, and so on, is of itself out. The ground-determination of the being as living becomes visible here as *pros allēla*, being before-one-another, open for-one-another; the living in its being can be determined only if the being with which it is is understood in its being. Life is being-at (*Dabeisein*). Only under this presupposition is the reasoning conclusive.

Aristotle himself does not pursue the consequence: the *phusikos* treats only of *phusei on* that is *kinoumenon*, moved; but there is also moved being that is not in the manner of life, insofar as *nous* is the decisive possibility. Since by the indirect proof *nous* and the *noēta* are excluded, these *phusei onta* in the manner of life can only be the living that is *anoēton*, that is not in the manner of thinking about it, so that its being is led by thinking and thinking-over. It follows that only the living of the character of *anoēton* comes into consideration. The *anoēta* Aristotle sets over against the being that has *phronēsis*, *phronēsis* as *dianoeisthai*: *anoēta*–*phronima*, *aloga*–*elloga* (*elloga*: in which *logos* is in the manner of *logon echon* in the primary sense). He uses these expressions promiscuously, saying on occasion that animals too have in a certain way a *phronēsis* — *phronēsis* here as getting-oriented, *aisthēsis*, not determined by *nous*, not in the proper sense.

The living is distinguished, with respect to its being, by being-at (*Dabeisein*). Aristotle has this ground-determination so sharply in view that on the ground of this being-sense he can prescribe the gait and construction of the individual steps of all life-research — of methodical significance for how the investigation of the so-called psychical is to set in. It is the question whether a living thing, insofar as it lives, has various being-possibilities: that it can perceive (*aisthēsis*), is out for something (*orexis*), can move toward it, and the like, or that it has the world so as to think it over. The difficulty is how one lifts out these individual being-possibilities of a living thing, how they are in their being-with-one-another; what is first to be investigated. Whether the being-possibilities are to be investigated in themselves, or themselves in enactment (can I so primarily study the being-able-to-take-in?); and if it comes to studying the *erga* first, whether then first to study the *antikeimena*, what stands over against each definite being-possibility (over against *aisthēsis*, the taking-in, the world in the character of being-colored). In fact one must start from the *antikeimena*; only in one with them is the *ergon* to be grasped. "First one must say what thinking itself is in its enactment" — for prior, with respect to addressing, are the actual, the present, before the possibilities. A possibility I only grasp, bring to sight, by bringing it, as it were, in its *Da*, as *energeia*, to sight. If that is so, still prior are the *antikeimena* to be considered. So too with the most primary possibility of the being of a living thing, *auxēsis*, with which Aristotle sets *gennēsis* — growth and bringing-to-the-world: first to study being-at-nourishment, and in connection the bringing-to-the-world of another, such as the living thing itself. *Gennēsis*: this becoming means nothing but coming-to-the-world — not simply that something lies present, a stillbirth, but that the being is so there that it is in its world. From this it is seen that the ground-determination from which the course started — that life means being-in-the-world, and according to a definite possibility — has its ground.

**β) Proof from the Character of Being-Moved Itself.** The second proof is direct, from investigating becoming, the character of being-moved itself. From the completed indirect proof, only the *anoēta* come into consideration; for the *phusikos* this moved being, with respect to the ground-determination of its being as being-in-the-world, will be the primary theme. Three modes of self-moving in the world: *auxēsis* (growth), *alloiōsis* (alteration), *phora* (locomotion). These three, characterized by movement, are to be investigated with respect to the *archē*, the whence of this being, the being-character of the proper possibility of being-in-the-world. *Auxēsis* is in a special sense cut to the *plant*, whose being is characterized only thereby: plants have no *aisthēsis*, are so in the world that they do not take in the world in which they are, take nourishment from it and in nourishing move in a peculiar way. How primarily — one might almost say phenomenologically — Aristotle sees the plant's proper being-moved shows at *De an.* 413 a 26 ff.: the plants have this peculiar being-possibility of being-in-the-world that they grow "toward opposite places, directions"; they move in their growth to all sides, nourish themselves from all sides, and so live. *Auxēsis* is the primary and only determination for the life of the plant. *Aisthēsis* Aristotle characteristically grasps as *alloiōsis*: by, for a being that is in the world in the manner of taking-in-the-world, something other being each time there from the world, encountering, life being with-one-another, it itself becomes another — which can only have sense if it itself is in its being so determined that this being means being-in-the-world. With most animals is added the further possibility of *phora*, being-able-to-move from one place to another — not abstractly thought up but concretely taken into view: in *De part. an.* Aristotle treats the various possibilities of *phora*: *ptēsis* (flying), *neusis* (swimming), *herpsis* (creeping), *badisis* (walking).

Now the seemingly dogmatic thesis becomes intelligible, that the *logos* of the *hou heneka* is the primary. In the *hou heneka* the proper being-character of the being of the living comes to appearance. Life is always related to the *hou heneka*, *telos*, being-finished-there in the sense of being-in-the-world; all being-determinations of the living are primarily to be oriented on this being as being-at, from which alone each being first gets its definite character in its look. Therewith we can grasp the *hylē* too in its characteristic being: the *sōma*, the *hylē* of a living thing, is not simply stuff that looks various in outline, but the *sōma* of the living is *organon*, has the primary character of the being-to..., being-at, in-being. *Organon* means: a being that has the character of accomplishment, oriented in itself on being-at-an-end. Because every moment of the full-being of the living has, with respect to the *hylē*, this full character, the interpretation of this being must start from the primary character of in-being — with the plant from *auxēsis*, with the animal from *aisthēsis* and *phora*, with the human from *nous*. Only from *nous* are the other being-possibilities to be understood in their being. Aristotle again points to the analogy of *technē*: if there is the necessity to split something, the splitter must be hard; if hard, then of iron — so this necessity, this accomplishment, prescribes the being-character of the *hylē*. All *hylē* in the living is *organon*, determined by the possibility of the living, prescribed by the accomplishment. The being-possibility of the *phusei onta* has its limit at *aisthēsis* and *phora*. These two causes — the *hou heneka* and, founded in it, the second question, whence the *archē* of movement — show in what being-nexus the *pathē* stand: they are co-determined by the *sōma*; the *pathē* are determinations of the being-possibility of the human. On movement Aristotle treats in *De motu animalium* (long held non-Aristotelian; W. Jaeger, from the manuscripts in Rome, showed it in fact Aristotelian and re-edited it); an essential moment is that it shows how movement as such is possible only if something rests.

#### e) The Determinedness of the History of the Investigation of Nature by Truth Itself

The critical consideration of prior nature-research gives a peculiar insight into the history such research can take: a science can long be in operation, material brought together, certain propositions and theories brought to light, and yet the science not be at its object at all; and the advancement of a science does not depend on the measure of acumen or proof-technique that hangs on it. Science is an affair of the right relation to the matters, which cannot be forced but is, at most with respect to preparations, up to us — at bottom a matter of destiny, how far those come and are there who make this ground-relation living. Despite their theories, the researchers were gradually *forced by truth itself* to see the being. Aristotle uses two characteristic expressions important for what he understands by truth. Of Empedocles: he was "led by truth itself," which in a sense let his theory collapse. Of Democritus: he first laid hold, not as necessary for physical *theōria* but "carried by the matter itself," led to the discovery that the being must be grasped not merely with respect to the *hylē* but also its *schēma*. *Alētheia* and *pragma* are here used in the same sense — *alētheia* is not "validity" attaching to the proposition (as an errant logic thinks) but nothing other than the being in its uncoveredness, is *pragma*, insofar as the being I deal with is there in a certain uncoveredness. The access to the matters was hindered because one denied the question of the *to ti ēn einai*, whereas in all inquiry in the narrower sense one is to ask after the what and how. Under Socrates this grew, but the seeking about nature ceased; those philosophizing bent toward the useful *aretē* and the *politikē*. This closes §19 and returns the course to the *pathē* — now to *pathos* as *hēdonē* and *lypē* in §20.

### § 20. *pathos* as *hēdonē* and *lypē* (Eth. Nic. K 1–5)

From considering the being-character of the living we saw: life means being-in-a-world. This determination now becomes double. First, the being of this living nature is determined in its *eidos* as this *dynamis* of being-in-the-world — as *eidos*, a being-determination of the being itself. Second, as *encounter from the world*: the living is in the world in a second sense too, in the sense of world-belonging. My being is being-in-the-world, and at once, in a second sense, in the world as belonging to it, such that I can encounter, for another, in the world, like a chair. For the Greeks both are *eidos*; the Greek does not know the difference between outer and inner consideration. From this, fundamental nexuses of the being of life in the wider sense follow. Being-with-one-another now gets a sharper determination: first, in being-with-one-another such beings are with one another as each for itself is being-in-the-world — the encountering-one-another is being-there-for-one-another, so that each being that is for the other is in the world; the encountering is in the world of the encountered, is there for another being. Second, in being-with-one-another we have, with one another, *the same world*. Being-with-one-another is at once: having, with one another, the same world. When one writes a book on epistemology, that is the presupposition — whether the questions can then still be posed in the usual way one may leave to the epistemologists to decide. Heidegger adds a barb at the contemporary quarrel over whether philosophy should be "philosophy of life": "philosophy of life" is like "botany of plants" — the emphatic claim that botany has to do with plants is as comic and senseless as its opposite.

Gathering the whole consideration of the *pathē*: the *pathē* are something that happens in the soul, is in being-alive, and mean more precisely the being-carried-off, the coming-out-of-composure, *kineisthai*, that aims at the proper being of the living, being-in-a-world. *Pathē* are modes of being-carried-off with respect to being-in-the-world; by the *pathē* the possibilities of getting-oriented in the world are essentially determined. The being-out-of-composure is in itself related to composure, the *hexis*. Averagely and everyday we are carried off, move in swings; with respect to this there is a composure. Because the *pathē* so characterize themselves as a mode of the being of the living, whose ground-structure is being-in-the-world, dealing in it, dealing with others, the prescription for the analysis of the individual *pathē* results — to be considered with respect to: the world in which the one concerned finds himself, the *Umwelt* of the living; the manner of the disposedness, the comporting-to the with-world; and how one must oneself be constituted to be affected by such-and-such *pathē*.

A characteristic determination not yet discussed: to each *pathos*, each being-carried-off, a definite disposedness "follows," *hepetai* — not in a temporal sense: in one with the respective *pathos* there is a *hēdonē* or *lypē*. This co-givenness of the respective disposedness is so fundamental that Aristotle says *pathos* itself is a *hēdonē* or *lypē*. The ground-determination of *hēdonē* (Eth. Nic. K 2): perhaps even in the base being there is a *phusikon*, a being-possibility belonging to its being, better than they (the base) are in themselves, on which these beings are out as *oikeion agathon*, the being at which they properly find their end. This says nothing but that in all living beings lies the determination that they are *out on the proper Dasein-finishedness*; every living thing is, as it were, tendentious, has the tendency to be as being-finished. All are out on *hēdonē*, a disposedness, and mostly not on the one they believe they strive for, not what humans say they are after, but all on the *same*: it matters to them *to live*. The being as living is a being to which, in its being, being-there matters; *theion* in Aristotle is nothing religious but the proper being of ever-being. So it is already visible that *hēdonē* is a determination of the living given with being-alive as such. More precisely, *hēdonē* is nothing but a fundamental determination of being-in-the-world, insofar as being-in-the-world is a being that I at once *have* — "have" a pale expression for "know about." *Hēdonē*, the finding-oneself, is that in which I have disclosure about my being-in-the-world: I *have* my being-in-the-world, have at once a determination of my being, a mode of my being. This phenomenon is nothing but what we mean when we ask someone "how's it going?" *Hēdonē* is no so-called "pleasure" but a determination of being in itself as life.

Aristotle carries out the clarification of *hēdonē* (K 3) by comparison with *aisthēsis*. Seeing, having-in-view, active looking-at, is in itself "finished," *teleion* — there is nothing that could still be added to make the seeing, in what it is, more complete; always, when I see, the seeing is in itself there at a stroke. This hangs together with seeing's being nothing but a now-actualized mode of being-present-in-the-world in the manner of having-it. *Hēdonē* is like this: it is a whole, and at no time could one grasp a *hēdonē* whose *eidos* would be completed by becoming over a longer time; hence it is no *kinēsis*, for every *kinēsis* is in time and of some *telos* (like building, finished when it makes what it is out for). *Hēdonē* is, just like *aisthēsis*, *en tō nyn*, in the moment, what it is, *mē en chronō*, not in time in the sense of a definite stretch; it does not first come to being-finished within time. This character, that it is no *kinēsis*, characterizes it as a determination of the presentness of *Dasein* as such. In Rhet. A 11 Aristotle does say *hēdonē* is a *kinēsis tis* (similarly *phronēsis* above with the animals), insofar as it too has the determination of *pathos*, of the respective being-carried-off — in it the determination of the turning-over from... to...; with respect to that, *hēdonē* is in a certain sense a *kinēsis*, *metabolē*. But *hēdonē* itself is not a being-mode that occasionally sets in, that could co-occur with another comportment; *hēdonē* is in itself already there with the being as living. It does not lie as a possibility in the respective dealing itself, no *hexis* of *aisthēsis*, such that by seeing rightly and seeing the suitable object, through the completion of seeing the *hēdonē* sets in — not a result of these circumstances, but conversely: this possibility of so-and-so-finding-oneself grounds in my being as being-in-the-world. Not a result of definite circumstances — so that Aristotle in his determination of *hēdonē* can directly identify it with *zōē*, life. The sentence "all are out on *hēdonē*" means nothing but "all are out on living," so that in being, it matters about being-there. Whence finally the question arises whether we grasp life on account of the finding-ourselves, or the finding-ourselves, the *hēdonē*, on account of life — a question left aside for the moment (Aristotle decides it in the following chapters).

The proper being of the human, the highest being-possibility, lies in *theōrein* — the possibility of being-there in the most radical sense. *Hēdonē* is, in short, nothing but the determination of the presentness of being-in-the-world, there in the finding-oneself as such. One must discard the determination of traditional psychology that grasps *lypē* and *hēdonē* as an annex of psychical processes. With *hēdonē*, life as being-in-the-world is always aimed at. Only so is it intelligible how Aristotle characterizes the various *pathē*. With what right is *phobos* grasped as *lypē*, a disposedness determined by being-depressed? We have a peculiar ground-structure: *Dasein*, insofar as it is life, is always respective *Dasein*; there is no *Dasein* in general. *Dasein* is always: *I am* — no being that is, but that I am, which at once has the possibility of being such a being as *one* is. Corresponding to the respectiveness of *Dasein*, every disposedness is always a definite one; there is no finding-oneself in general; every finding-oneself is so and so. Every *hēdonē* is a definite one, likewise every *lypē*.

*Hēdonē* in the nexus named at the definition of the *pathē*: it is said to be "there-with," *hepetai*. Closer consideration shows the co-being-there of *hēdonē* means nothing but the co-being-there of *Dasein* itself, which is affected by a definite *pathos*. *Hēdonē* as disposedness is the mode of the having-oneself of a *Dasein*. Already therewith life is characterized as being-in-the-world, life as in-being. The possibility is given that the so-being, as oriented, in a certain way *has itself* along. We must take distance from the orientation of having-oneself on *reflection*. Reflection is only an, as it were, outré form in which *Dasein* is conscious of itself; from it one can never come to understand the primitive mode of disposedness. The affective as such already has the character of having-oneself. *Hēdonē* reaches so originally into the being of *Dasein* that it can be identified with *zēn*; *hēdonē* belongs to *Dasein* itself.

This disposedness, expressed by *hēdonē*, has a double possibility: insofar as this finding-oneself has the character of *hairesis*, and insofar as it is *phygē*. The disposedness is characterized once as going-toward, grasping-toward, going toward *Dasein* itself; or such a character that the disposedness "shrinks back" before *Dasein*, "flees" it. This is given in *hēdonē* or *lypē*. *Hairesis* and *phygē* are the characters that characterize the ground-possibility of life as a being-at-oneself; they are the ground-movednesses of *Dasein*. No accident that *hairesis* and *phygē* appear where it is a matter of the last ontological interpretation of *Dasein*. Because *hēdonē* or *lypē* is originally bound up with the *Dasein* of the living and makes up the ground-disposedness, the mode in which *Dasein* as it were carries itself along, *hēdonē* can be characterized as *pathos* — such a *pathos* of which Aristotle says it is *enkechrōsmenon*, "wholly steeped in colors," a *pathos* that dyes and steeps through the *bios*, the "existence" — *bios*, not *zōē*: *bios* as existence, life in the emphatic sense of the human, that grasps itself in *proairesis*. Another closer formation: Aristotle constantly says that with every concern both *hēdonē* and *lypē* are co-given, with every *pathos*, but just as much with every taking-in, thinking, thinking-over, with *theōria*, insofar as these are ground-modes of life — as inseparable companion, *hēdonē*.

Summary of the determinations of *pathos*: the excursus (over *De part. an.*) showed that *pathos*, characterized by its *eidos*, is determined as being-in-the-world, a determination of the *pros allēla*. Insofar as with every *pathos* *hēdonē* is co-given, in-being itself is *had*, had in the two possibilities of *hairesis* and *phygē*. The being of the living as *pathos* is a being of the character of being-carried-off and being-carried-along; in it lies the moment of *metabolē*, coming-out-of-composure and being-out-of-composure — turning-over from... to... Further, to the determination of *pathos* (Rhet. B 1) the moment of the turning-over of *krinein*: in this coming-out-of-composure *krinein* is co-affected, discriminating, taking-a-stance; the manner of being-oriented over the world, in the world, is co-claimed in this being-affected by a *pathos*. Therewith the inner nexus between *pathos* and *logos* is set out — *logos* as enactment-mode of *krinein*.

### § 21. *phobos* (Rhet. B 5)

We pass to the characteristic of fear. Aristotle treats it (Rhet. B 5) in two directions: *phobos* as *pathos*, how "being-afraid" represents a quite definite concretion of "being-out-of-composure"; and *phobos* as *pistis* — the proper thread for the interpretation of the *Rhetoric*: how far "being-afraid," as a ground-determination of the *Dasein* of the other, the hearer, speaks along in deliberating, in becoming-resolved about an affair to be settled.

#### a) Schematic Outline of the Characterization of Fear

A schematic outline of how fear is characterized in this chapter. First Aristotle gives theme, definition, and ground-determinations in their first execution. Then he characterizes the *phoberon*, more precisely the *phobera*, the "fearful," the "fearsome" in the sense of what, at its coming-to-meet, I fall into fear before; included in the consideration of the *phobera* is the consideration of the *sēmeia*, those encounter-characters of the fearful that *announce* it (*phobera* are objects, circumstances, and the like). Then he treats the *phoberoi*, *phobera* of the character of the living, other humans — how far other humans with whom I live together are for me of the character of the *phoberon*. Then he gives the pervasive determination of the *phoberon*, concerning both *phobera* and *phoberoi* — the moment that makes up the properness of the fearful. Then the disposedness in fearing, how I must find myself to be, or fall, into fear — only at this moment does the proper interpretation come to its end; only here does the phenomenon of fear become properly visible. Finally, from the so-determined phenomenon of fear, the thread for forming the *pistis*: *phobos* and other *pathē* are definite disposednesses in which the hearer finds himself or should; the orator must see to it that he possibly sets the others, through his speaking, into fear — he must be oriented about the phenomenon to grip the others rightly, to be able to set them into fear.

#### b) The Theme, the First Definition, and the First Determinations

The theme and first determinations: what sort of thing is feared, which humans, and the how of the having-oneself — the disposedness in fearing. One sees the ground-orientation on the sense of *pathos* as being-in-the-world, standing-to-others and finding-oneself thereby; these are the respects under which every *pathos* stands. From there Aristotle seeks the first definition. But fear becomes properly intelligible only where he gives the *pōs echontes*. In the first definition only a *formal* structure of fear is given; it is not clearly set out that such a finding-oneself is a *fearing*. Aristotle characterizes *phobos* as a *lypē tis ē tarachē ek phantasias mellontos kakou phthartikou ē lypērou* — not even *hepetai* but directly *phobos lypē tis*: fearing is something like a being-depressed, a disposedness characterized as *phygē*, as it were "fleeing" before my *Dasein*, no *hairesis*, no lifted *Dasein*, but shrinking back before it. The *ē tarachē*, "confusion": *lypē* more precisely characterized as getting-into-disarray, being-in-disarray — more closely determined insofar as I myself stand to myself in the manner of shrinking back before myself, before my *Dasein*. The *ek phantasias*, "from something that shows itself"; *phantasia* (in the wholly original sense): what shows itself, the self-showing; *ek phantasias* = *ek tou phainesthai*. Therewith is expressed: what shows itself is not yet properly there, not there in *aisthēsis*, is there in such a way that it is in a certain way not yet there — a getting-into-disarray from the seeing-before-oneself of a not-yet-being, so not yet there that it has the character of what still will or should come. The *mellontos*, "not yet there" in the sense of the impending, the coming-toward-me. Such a *mellon* as *kakon*, *phthartikon*: what comes toward me in the manner of detriment, in the sense of what could be detrimental to my *Dasein*, could do it harm. In short: a disposedness placed before a possibility that concerns me, comes toward me, and as such announces itself, and precisely through the announcing comes nearer.

It is to be noted in advance that what I fear, what I am characterized with in my being-in-fear, is so determined that it concerns me so and so, a *lypēron* that can bring me out of composure. Not every *kakon* is object of fear; the *kakon* must be such that it has designs on my *Dasein*, so that I get into disarray. In the preceding chapter Aristotle says: the *lypēra*, what brings me into the peculiar disposedness of shrinking back before myself, are *aisthēta*, I feel this being-approached, so that I then get into disarray — whereas at the impending of an injustice that will strike me, or before the possibility that I grow imbecile, I do not properly have fear but at most *hate* it. I do not get into disarray at it; such an impending leaves me cold, cold in the coldness of hate. To fear belongs this peculiar encounter-manner, that it *excites* the one who falls into fear.

Further, the encountering must be determined as *synengys*, not wholly far off, "in nearness." What is in the distance we have no fear before. All know they will die, that death is impending, but because it is not near, they are not at it with their *phronēsis*, do not turn to it, do not look about after it. What I fall into fear before must have the character of nearness: impending that presses, as such, into nearness. Constitutive for this possibility of being-afraid is this definite encounter-manner of the *Umwelt*: what encounters in the *Umwelt* must have the character of *threat*; the so-being-in-the-world is a being-threatened. But being-threatened is not yet being-afraid. In every fearing lies a being-threatened, but not conversely. Fear is precisely a definite finding-oneself, a comporting-to-oneself in being-threatened.

#### c) The Threatening (*phobera*) and the Encounter-Characters That Announce It (*sēmeia*)

Aristotle gathers the "threatening" under three moments. First, it must *show itself* (*phainesthai*) as so and so, yet not as properly there. Characteristic of the fearful is *possibility*, being-possible, but in the sense of the *indeterminate*; the moment of indeterminacy heightens the "it can" with respect to the possible disposedness of being-afraid — the indeterminate heightens the threatening. Second, *dynamin echein megalēn*: something can do me harm, over against which I find myself in a definite powerlessness, that comes toward me in the character of "mightiness." This is constitutive for threat; something I am superior to from the outset, that can do me no harm, cannot threaten me, even if it is indeterminate whether it strikes me. Third, this so-mighty in the possibility of being-able-to-come, "brought into nearness," the *plēsiasmos*, makes the threat into "danger." Something threatening in far distance is not danger; the threat becomes danger when it contracts as such upon me. From here the peculiar moment of fear becomes intelligible that we know from the somatic, from constriction. The threatening is the indeterminate possibility of something that can concern me, is superior to me, concentrates on me — not factually, but in the peculiar character of pressing-into-my-nearness, so that the *phoberon* is announced, so that this *phoberon* is, as it were, represented by the *sēmeia*. The *sēmeia* take over the forming of the peculiar *Da*-character of the *phoberon*: taking over the function of the *Da*, to be a not-being-there-in-drawing-near. Three *sēmeia*: *echthra*, *orgē* (hostile disposition, rage); *adikia* (unjust disposition); *aretē hybrizomenē* (earnestness that is mocked, provoked).

It is instructive for understanding the explication of fear that from what can excite fear one cannot yet fully determine the falling-into-fear — so that in the fearful not all moments are yet enclosed: *no parallelism of the fearful with being-afraid*. In phenomenology it is asserted, but it does not correspond to the facts. The characteristic moment of what the fearing one stands before must be grasped as the *possible*; therein is prescribed the way in which the fearful itself properly becomes what it can be — the heightening of this possible as one that *will come*, come toward me in the character of the detrimental. This possible is heightened in its possibility by being there and not there, so that it announces itself in its being-there; but in the announcing lies that it is not yet itself there. The manner in which the fearful becomes present as such is *phantasia*, not *aisthēsis*. The moment that makes up nearness is the *plēsiasmos*; the finding-oneself before a threatening becomes, through the *plēsiasmos*, the situation of danger. The function of drawing-near, of announcing, the characteristic heightening of the possibility that lies in the fearful, is taken over by the *sēmeia*.

On the three *sēmeia*: *echthra*, *orgē* — hostile disposition and rage are characterized by *proairesis*; rage and hostility encounter as modes of the *Dasein* of one who *can break loose* in the next moment; whether he does is uncertain, but he can — rage brings just this "can" to a peak. *Adikia*: one who has the inclination to harm others and the power to do so; here again the determination of *proairesis* brings the being-able into dangerousness. We have a *double* "can": insofar as he has the power to carry out what he has in mind — he can; and insofar as this having-power is in *proairesis*, it is pushed into a second "can," so that behind this (first) "can" stands the second "can" of *proairesis*, of being-able-to-resolve. Through the sense of *adikia* the *dynamis* is set into the right possibility; the threatening becomes the dangerous; with the telescoping of the "cans," the uncertainty of what impends is heightened. *Aretē hybrizomenē*, the "provoked earnestness": the provoked one is constantly on the jump, can become danger for me unawares, harm me; insofar as he can harm me unawares, he is dangerous. All these moments as *sēmeia* take on themselves the character of what they indicate; they are the announcing of this threatening, and while they announce, they themselves become fearful. The whither of the referring communicates itself, in its being-character as threatening, to the referring itself; the threatening becomes, through the announcing, the dangerous; the *sēmeia* form the dangerousness of the threatening and thereby become themselves dangerous.

#### d) Humans Themselves, Insofar as They Are Fearful (*phoberoi*)

After the *phobera* Aristotle discusses the *phoberoi*, humans themselves insofar as they are fearful, with a general observation. The possibility that humans are fearful to one another lies first in that humans are "of bad disposition," out for the bad; second, out for their own advantage, gain; third, mostly "cowardly," committing themselves for nothing, not to be relied on. Such humans are found as characterized by *zōē praktikē*, by *proairesis*; all these moments are there in *proairesis*, in the possibility that with and from them the human resolves to such-and-such. Aristotle enumerates nine characters, characterizing various situations in which a human can encounter another as fearful. Fearful is: the one to whom I am delivered up (one who has committed a crime — his fellow-knowers are fearful, since, being gain-seeking and out for harm, the danger stands that they betray him; being-delivered-up to definite humans is a definite possibility of having to do with the fearful); the "mighty" for those inferior to them (with the mighty, the double "can" already characterized); those who are harmed or believe themselves harmed, insofar as they are out to repay the harm — one has something to expect from them; those who have themselves harmed another and now fear the repayment, arranging that they not be harmed again by the one they harmed; competitors in one and the same matter, insofar as the other, to reach the advantage, is capable of anything (this being-capable-of-anything carries the possibility of threat); those who are a threat to such as can accomplish more than we — if these, mightier than we, are struck, then all the more we ourselves (here again the possibility of coming-into-danger through the peculiar detour over others, a characteristic heightening through the detour); those who have already brought down our superiors; those who bring down weaker ones than ourselves, for from them it can be expected that they one day fall upon us; and, among enemies and adversaries, not so much those who strike at once, who wear their heart on the tongue and say out at once what they intend, but far more fearful the *praoi*, the reserved, the *eirōnes*, the ironical (who act as if the affair at issue were not so important as it is taken), the *panourgoi*, the smooth (one does not know where one stands with them — a peculiar uncertainty: one does not know whether with them all is really in order or whether they only act so); with them it is not evident whether the threat is near, so that it is never clear that in fact no danger is at hand — heightening of the uncertainty through the indeterminacy of nearness. These examples, according to the whole nexus, concern relations of one *polis* to another and, within the *polis*, among individuals — the *phoberoi* seen in this direction of being-with-one-another.

#### e) The Properness of the Fearful (*phoberon*)

Aristotle then gives a proper characterization of the *phoberon*. What one has failed at and can no longer set right, what could have been avoided and is now unavertible, is fearful in the highest degree — unavertible, not simply, but *for me*; the avertibility still stands with the opponent. Such a situation presents the fearful in the highest sense: the unavertible, but not simply, only for me; the possibility of averting stands with the other, who is hostile to me. Insofar as the unavertibility is not absolute but stands with the other, and the other has *proairesis*, it is characterized as threat. This being-threatened is determined by the *elpis* of the threatened: even the most threatening in the highest degree must still, in a certain way, offer the prospect of a staying-away. The fearful becomes more proper the more the character of the prospect of help dwindles, where there is no help and yet it is still expected by the threatened — the prospect of staying-away must still stand.

#### f) The Disposedness in Being-Afraid

Being-afraid must be characterized as an *oiesthai*: the one who is to fall into fear must "believe" that the definite thing that threatens threatens *him*; further, that the threatening proceeds from this definite human and that he threatens him *now*. The threatening must not merely be there in the manner that I know it could once happen to me — not the being-oriented over the possibility of threat, but the being-for-it, believing, that I have such-and-such to expect, that from this human something happens to me now. Characteristic of the way the *phobera* are there for me is the *oiesthai*; where this peculiar *oiesthai* stays away, one can indeed know of a threatening, but it cannot be feared. This being-for-it stays away with those to whom things go splendidly, who hold themselves secure against every threat — those who dispose over riches, bodily powers, following, influence; these never fall into fear, in them a peculiar contempt forms, insolence, impudence (*hybristai kai oligōroi kai thraseis*). Further, fear stays away with those who have another *oiesthai*, who believe nothing more can happen to them because they have already been through everything — the "hardened" (*apepsygmenoi*) stand outside the possibility of fear. This manner of appropriating one's own *Dasein* in the opining about it must shift; it must come to a definite *oiesthai* for the fearful to be able to come into nearness for me at all.

This believing-oneself-in-danger is at once one that moves in an *elpis*: appropriating the threatening as concerning one, and yet at once hoping that one gets away. The *elpis sōtērias* is for being-afraid as constitutive as the believing in a being-threatened. In this "hope of being-saved" reveals itself the peculiar disposedness that I am *concerned* about what I fear: it must concern me, can be nothing indifferent. Only now is the peculiar *tarachē*, "unrest," intelligible. The unrest is nothing but the against-one-another of *oiesthai* and *elpis*: believing oneself lost, and yet hoping. The possibility of rescue must be held fast, and in the expectant holding-fast of the possibility of the not-being-able-to-be-destroyed moves the peculiar "shrinking-back" before what threatens me — *lypē* as *phygē*. The possibility of being saved, in short, of *being*, is there; nevertheless I shrink back before being. That is the ground-sense of *tarachē*. *Dasein* does not go away from itself but holds fast, in hope, the possibility of rescue. So in *tarachē* the two moments of *diōxis* and *phygē* show themselves — both ground-determinations of the proper movedness of *Dasein*.

#### g) Fear as *pistis*. Courage as the Possibility of Composure Toward It. The *pathē* as Ground for the *logos*

Aristotle says: insofar as the human comes into this unrest, determined by *oiesthai* and *elpis*, he becomes *ready to deliberate*. Humans who fall into fear run to others to take counsel, to fetch advice. If I set people into fear, if I present the political events as dangerous, I thereby make them ready and inclined to take counsel; I make them themselves into those who contribute to the coming-about of an intended resolve — I make them into *pistis* themselves. With respect to speaking-with-one-another in everydayness, fear shows itself as the disposedness that *brings to speech*. What shows itself here in the circle of everydayness is a phenomenon that has a far more original foundation, insofar as in the human's *Dasein* it can be a matter of fear in another sense, what we call *anxiety* or *dread* (*Angst oder Grauen*): where it is *unheimlich* to us, where we do not know before what we are afraid. When it is *unheimlich* to us, we begin to speak. This is a hint for the *Dasein*-like *genesis* of speaking: how speaking hangs together with the ground-determination of *Dasein* itself, characterized by *uncanniness* (*Unheimlichkeit*). Heidegger here plants the seed of the *Being and Time* thesis: *Angst*, distinct from fear before a definite threatening thing, is fear before the indefinite, before being-in-the-world itself, and it is the ground from which *logos* springs.

The fear Aristotle characterizes has itself the possibility of being seized *resolutely* by the human. Fear as a definite *pathos* has the possibility of a *hexis*; such a possibility is *courage*. But it is visible that I can be courageous in the right sense only if I *fear*. Fear is the condition of the possibility of courage. Whoever does not fear, or talks himself into not fearing (which is mostly the case), does not come to resolving rightly and being courageous. It matters to seize courage — to fear in the right way and thereby come into resolteness. Connected with this is Augustine's sentence *initium sapientiae timor Domini*, which makes the fundamental relevance of fear for *Dasein* visible. (On possibilities of composure toward fear: Rhet. B 5, more fully Eth. Nic. Γ 9–10.) Heidegger's point is precise and against sentimentality: courage is not the absence of fear but a *hexis* toward it; the right way of fearing is the ground of the right way of resolving.

The *elpis sōtērias* gives at once the hint that fearing, in connection with *sōtēria*, stands in a peculiar connection with *Dasein* itself. Aristotle says of the *hexis* — more precisely, of the being-able-to-dispose over the moment rightly — that it *sōzei mesotēta*, "saves the middle," brings me into the proper being that corresponds to the circumstances. *Sōzein* is also used in the metaphorical sense: *ho theos sōzei ton ouranon*, "the god saves the heaven" — saving in the sense of the not-letting-perish, the maintaining-in-*Dasein*. *Sōzein*, *sōtēria*: counter-concepts to *phthora*, the "vanishing-from-*Dasein*."

Finally, how fear and the *pathē* stand in connection with *logos*, insofar as *logos* is taken as speaking-with-one-another that has the function of accomplishing the interpretation of *Dasein* in its everydayness. Insofar as the *pathē* are not merely an annex of psychical processes but *the ground on which speaking grows and into which the spoken again grows*, the *pathē* are in their turn the ground-possibilities in which *Dasein* primarily orients itself about itself, finds itself. The primary being-oriented, the illumination of its being-in-the-world, is no *knowing* but a finding-oneself that can be variously determined according to the *Dasein*-mode of a being. Only within the so-characterized finding-oneself and being-in-the-world is the possibility given to *speak about* things, insofar as they are divested of the look they have in the nearest dealing. Now arises the possibility of coming to a definite *objectivity* (*Sachlichkeit*), which in a certain way sets back the manner of seeing the world as it is prescribed by the *pathē*. Only when one sees *Dasein* so can one set the *pathē* back. Only from here can one understand what it was for the Greeks, who were as it were *in love with logos*, to work themselves out of conversation and idle talk to an objectivity — and that it is false to hold Greece generally for a land of milk and honey, where each day a new discovery was made, as if things had fallen into these famous men's laps.

### § 22. Supplements to the Explication of *Dasein*

The subtitle: supplements to the explication of *Dasein* as being-in-the-world.

#### a) The *hexis* of *alētheuein* (Eth. Nic. Δ 12–13)

Recall that the *pathē* are grasped in the *Rhetoric* as *pisteis*, insofar as they speak for an opinion that leads the living-together of humans in the *polis*. These *pisteis* are that *ex hōn hē protasis*, the whence and from-which the respective pre-giving of the known is taken; every argumentation speaks from a self-evident. The *pathē*, determined by *hēdonē*, mark the respective finding of *Dasein* in its world. In the consideration of *phobos* and the *pathē* in general, these were considered insofar as they are determinations of the *hearer*. But everyone, being with other beings in *Dasein*, is hearer just as much as speaker. So the *doxa*, in whose forming the *pathē* co-participate, characterizes the *interpretedness* (*Ausgelegtheit*) of *Dasein* in everydayness. The *koinōnia*, being-with-one-another, is in the having-the-world-there-with-one-another a having-with-one-another of definite *doxai*, whose orientation comes from how *Dasein* itself in each case speaks about itself.

This *koinōnia* has the special possibility of the mutual being-with-one-another of humans: *homilia* or *syzēn*, living-together. This living-together is averagely and everyday characterized by *doxa*; *Dasein* in everydayness holds itself in the "more or less," moves in swings, does not take itself so exactly, is un-objective (*unsachlich*) to a certain degree — un-objective toward itself. Insofar as it is that and at once has the possibility of resolving to something proper, is in the possibility of *proairesis*, it has, with respect to the uncoveredness of its being, a *hexis* too. There is, with respect to *homilia*, *syzēn*, a *hexis*: a disposing over the *genuineness* of the comportment to others and to oneself. One determined by this *hexis* Aristotle names the *alētheutikos*: to dispose over *Dasein* with respect to uncoveredness, to give oneself so that the self-giving and being-with-others is not a hiding-oneself, dissembling — to give oneself as one is and as one thinks.

The *syzēn* is characterized by *zōē praktikē meta logou*. This *hexis* plays itself out *en logois kai praxesin kai tō prospoiēmati*. *Prospoiēma*: the saying-about-oneself in the sense of ascribing-to-oneself what one asserts of oneself — what one claims of oneself in the manner of ascribing it to oneself. This *prospoiēma*, *prospoiēsis*, is mostly in swings. Usually and mostly humans are, hidden or plainly, in *homilia*, of the character either of the *alazōn* or of the *eirōn*. The *alazōn* is the one who makes something of himself, talks big: he speaks of himself what stands in general repute, or asserts of himself such as "he does not dispose over at all," or "greater or more significant than what he himself is" — making something of himself, so that he *hides his proper being*, not one who gives his proper being undissembled. The other possibility, characterized by the *eirōn*: denying what he is, not admitting his being as it immediately shows itself, making his being less — Socrates, who gave himself out as though he knew nothing and yet knew more than the others. The *eirōn* has good and bad possibilities. The middle is the *alētheutikos*: to be "truthful," "undissembled" — each speaks and acts as he is.

One sees the accomplishment of *logos* in being-in-the-world, and thereby the inner bond of *logos* with being-in-the-world, and at once that the un-coveredness, the uncovered being-oriented in being-toward-oneself and being-toward-others, is characterized by *alētheia*, more precisely by *alētheuein* as a *hexis*, this *alētheuein* in the manner of the being-able-to-be-there-unconcealed. Aristotle treats the various possibilities of *alētheuein* thematically in *Eth. Nic.* Z: a manifold of such *hexeis*. Two are the highest: *sophia* and *phronēsis* — "circumspection" in the moment, and *theōrein*, that disclosing of the world, disclosing of being, in which no practical side-intention can come into question, which *alētheuein* as *bios theōrētikos* represents the proper and highest possibility of Greek existence.

#### b) The World as Nature-World

We have so far characterized the human's *Dasein* as being-in-the-world and determined the world first through the encounter-moments of the *agathon*. The being-character of the world we deal with is determined as *endechomenon allōs*, is more or less so, subject to change. In this *Umwelt*, the world we deal with in our concern, the world shows itself at once *as nature*. Nature is no being-region that would stand *beside* this world but is the world itself, as it shows itself in the *Umwelt* in a definite way, characterized in that the world as nature is that being as it shows itself for our being-in-the-world in daily dealing as *always-already-being-there*: seafaring on the sea, fish are caught in the river. The everydayness of producing is always producing *from* something that is drawn — from the mine, from the forest. All that everydayness needs, it has, and it is there in nature. It is important to see that nature is primarily *not* something like an object of scientific consideration. Nature is the *already-always-being-there* of the world; world is primarily seen as an aspect of the *Umwelt* itself. The change of day and night comes ever again, likewise the sun in its course and the stars. In the *Umwelt* I have, there is the ground I stand on, air whose presence, as it were, waits on me. So the world must be understood if one determines being-in-the-world as dealing-with-it; the world is seen in this *Dasein*-experience as what always is and what can also be otherwise.

The proper ever-being, which need not first long be sought for natural orientation in the world, is the *heaven*. The Greek heaven and world must be understood as a *vault* on which the sun rises and sets. The human's practical concern plays itself out in the middle, the *meson*. The earth is the orientation-center for orientation in the world, an orientation that need not yet be theoretical, not natural-scientific. This orientation-system is an *absolute* one. There is nothing from which my *Dasein* would be relative; there is only *one* *Dasein*, the *Dasein* on the earth as absolute orientation-center. For Aristotle there are three ground-movements: away from the middle, *anō*; toward the middle, *katō*; around the middle, *kyklos* — three movements in which *Dasein* as being-in-the-world stands. All that is in the world itself is the *kosmos*. The being as *kosmos* is characterized by the presentness of what is always already there, *parousia*. Every being in its being is determined by being *peras*, the become-finished that has its limits — "limit" not determined by the relation of one being to another, but the limit is itself a being-moment at the being; *peras* is its *place*, its site, its being-produced, its being-at-its-place. So the being that moves in the *kosmos* has each its definite limit of its movement, i.e. its place. *Place* is a *positive determination of being*; place belongs to the being as such. Present-day physics comes back to this in the concept of "field."

So much on the character of the world insofar as it is to be considered as the nature-world. This nature is not, as it were, alongside — not first nature and then all sorts of things added; rather, if one would see the natural things with respect to their being-there, one must see them *through the Umwelt*, or as it is there as *Umwelt*. Only then does one have a right ground to grasp primarily the mode of being-there of the being of nature.

This closes the Erster Teil (GA p. 268). The whole first part has led from the current sense of *ousia* through the Aristotelian determination of the human's being (*zōē praktikē meta logou*, *psychēs energeia kat' aretēn*) and the *Rhetoric*'s hermeneutic of everyday *Dasein* (*doxa*, the *pathē*, *phobos*) to the *hexis* of *alētheuein* and the nature-world — everywhere with the single aim of exhibiting the rootedness (*Bodenständigkeit*) of conceptuality in *Dasein* itself, which the Zweiter Teil will then take up as its explicit theme in a repeating interpretation of Aristotelian ground-concepts.

# **Commentary on Heidegger, *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* (GA 3)**

A paragraph-by-paragraph commentary on the *Kantbuch* of 1929, covering the main text (GA 3, pp. 1–246) — the Introduction and all four Sections, through §45. The six appendices (pp. 249–311), including the *Davoser Disputation* with Cassirer, are not included here; they are a different genre and would need their own treatment.

Citations give the GA 3 page. Kant is cited as Heidegger cites him, by the A and B pagination of the *Critique of Pure Reason*. Translations are working renderings, not a published edition. Two recurring choices could mislead if left unmarked. *Grundlegung* is rendered "laying of the ground" rather than "foundation," because Heidegger's whole polemic turns on refusing the picture of a foundation slid under a finished building. *Enthüllung* and *Offenbarkeit* are kept apart as "unveiling" (of being) and "manifestness" (of beings): the pair carries in Kantian idiom exactly the distinction that *Sein und Zeit* §44 carries as disclosedness and uncoveredness, and collapsing them destroys the book's central claim about truth.

A small glossary of German terms is retained where an English word would blur them. **Dasein** — the being that we ourselves are, and here specifically "the Dasein in the human being" (*das Dasein im Menschen*), which is not a synonym for "human being" and is the pivot of the Fourth Section. **Einbildungskraft** appears throughout as "imagination," but never in the sense of fancy or image-making: it is a power of forming (*Bildung*), and Heidegger's argument depends on hearing *Bild* (image, look) inside it. **Gegenstehenlassen** — the letting-stand-over-against by which finite knowing first lets an object be an object; Michel Henry's *objectivation* translates this word, and readers coming to GA 3 from *L'essence de la manifestation* should note that it names an act of the subject, not a property of the thing. **Umschlag** and **Zurückweichen** — the reversal Kant performs between the two editions, and the shrinking back from the imagination that Heidegger reads in it.

The argument of the book, stated once so the reader can hold the arc. The *Critique of Pure Reason* is not epistemology and not a theory of natural-scientific knowledge; it is a laying of the ground of metaphysics, and what it grounds is ontology — the prior understanding of being that lets beings be encountered at all. That grounding runs back through five stages to a single source, and the source turns out to be the transcendental imagination, which Kant himself names the common root of sensibility and understanding and then abandons. Because the imagination is at bottom time, and time is pure self-affection, the ground of ontological knowledge is the temporality of a finite self. Kant shrank back from this because it dissolves the primacy of reason; the Fourth Section retrieves what he shrank from, and the retrieval is the metaphysics of Dasein as fundamental ontology. The book therefore reads Kant as an ally forced into service, and Heidegger's own later prefaces concede as much — the 1973 preface calls the *Kantbuch* a refuge (*Zuflucht*), a place to look for an advocate for the question of being that *Sein und Zeit* had raised and no one had heard.

Certain misreadings dog this text, and the commentary guards against them throughout. The book is not Kant philology and does not claim to be; Heidegger concedes the charge of violence in the preface to the second edition and grants that historical philology is right every time it presses the charge against a thinking dialogue. Finitude (*Endlichkeit*) is not a defect measured against an infinite intellect but the positive constitution of human knowing, and the whole reading collapses if it is heard as a lament. Ontological knowledge is not knowledge of some higher class of beings, and transcendence is not a passage across to a transcendent object; both name the prior projection of the being of beings within which any object can be met. The transcendental imagination is not a third faculty added to two others, and "root" is not a cause producing effects — Heidegger's claim is that intuition and thinking are *stems* of one root, so that neither is intelligible on its own. A schema is not an image and not a generic picture; the schema-image is introduced precisely to keep the two apart. Time as pure self-affection is not introspection and not the inner sense of empirical psychology, and the self is not first given and then found to be temporal. Finally, the metaphysics of Dasein is not a philosophical anthropology, and the Fourth Section is a sustained argument that the question "what is the human being?" cannot be posed anthropologically at all.

## **Einleitung: Das Thema der Untersuchung und seine Gliederung**

The investigation sets itself the task of interpreting the *Critique of Pure Reason* as a laying of the ground of metaphysics, in order to set the problem of metaphysics before us as the problem of a fundamental ontology. Two theses are packed into one sentence, and their order matters: the Kant interpretation is the means, the exhibition of fundamental ontology the end. Kant's text is not the subject matter but the medium in which a systematic claim is to be made visible.

Fundamental ontology names the ontological analytic of the finite essence of the human being that is to prepare the foundation for the metaphysics "belonging to the nature of the human being." It is the metaphysics of human Dasein required if metaphysics is to be possible at all, and it remains in principle distinct from every anthropology, including philosophical anthropology. That demarcation, stated here in a single clause, is what the entire Fourth Section will have to earn. Laying out the idea of a fundamental ontology means exhibiting this analytic as a necessary demand and thereby making clear in what intention, in what manner, within what limits and under what presuppositions it poses the concrete question: what is the human being? An idea first announces itself through its power to illuminate, so the idea of fundamental ontology is to prove and present itself in an interpretation of the *Critique*. The Kant reading is thus a test of illuminating power, not an appeal to authority — which is why the eventual verdict that Kant shrank back does not damage the demonstration.

What "laying of the ground" means must be clarified in advance. The expression takes its sense from building. Metaphysics is no building already standing, yet it is actual in all human beings "as a natural disposition" (Kant, B 21). Laying a ground could therefore mean sliding a foundation under this natural metaphysics, or replacing one already laid by pushing a new one under it.

Exactly this picture must be kept out of the idea. Laying the ground is rather the projecting of the building-plan itself, in such a way that the plan at the same time prescribes on what and how the building is to be grounded. Yet as the projecting of a plan it is again no empty production of a system with its compartments, but the architectonic delimitation and articulation of the inner possibility of metaphysics — the concrete determination of its essence. The metaphor is not decorative. A plan is drawn before the building exists and determines what would count as its ground; that is precisely the structure of the a priori, and Heidegger is smuggling the whole doctrine of projection into an image from the building trade.

All determination of essence is completed only in the laying bare of the ground of essence. This single sentence is the hinge on which the Third Section turns: an essence-determination that stops short of its own ground has not finished, which is why the five stages of the Second Section cannot be the end of the book.

Laying the ground, as the projection of the inner possibility of metaphysics, is necessarily a letting-become-effective of the bearing power of the ground that has been laid. Whether and how this happens is the criterion of the originality and breadth of a laying of the ground. A ground is not laid by being pointed at; it is laid by being made to bear. Heidegger thereby gives himself the standard by which he will convict Kant: the imagination is reached and named, and then not allowed to bear.

Should the interpretation succeed in bringing to light the originality of the origin of metaphysics, that originality is genuinely understood only when it is also brought into the concrete happening of letting the origin spring forth — that is, when the laying of the ground is repeated. *Wiederholung*, retrieval, is announced here as the title of the Fourth Section and as something other than restatement: to retrieve is to perform again, from the ground, what the first performance left unperformed.

Since metaphysics belongs to the "nature of the human being" and factically exists with him, it has always already formed itself in some shape. An explicit laying of its ground therefore never happens out of nothing, but in the strength and the weakness of a tradition that prescribes to it the possibilities of its starting point. With respect to the tradition enclosed in it, every laying of the ground is, in relation to earlier ones, a transformation of the same task. Tradition is not an obstacle to be cleared away but the very medium of the possibilities available — a claim that licenses Heidegger's method of destruction and simultaneously licenses his violence, since a transformation of a task is not a report on it.

The interpretation must accordingly bring four things to light: the laying of the ground of metaphysics in its starting point, in its execution, in its originality, and in a retrieval. The four Sections are named, and the sequence is one of increasing depth rather than of subject matter: the same single act is examined four times, each time from further back.

The body of the book carries a title of its own, standing over all four Sections: the laying out of the idea of a fundamental ontology by way of the interpretation of the *Critique of Pure Reason* as a laying of the ground of metaphysics. The Sections are subordinate to that one heading, and the reader who forgets it will take the four Sections for four topics rather than four passes at one.

## **Erster Abschnitt: Die Grundlegung der Metaphysik im Ansatz**

Exhibiting Kant's starting point amounts to answering one question: why does the laying of the ground of metaphysics become, for Kant, a critique of pure reason? The answer comes through three subsidiary questions — what concept of metaphysics did Kant find before him, what is the starting point of the laying of its ground, and why is this laying of the ground a critique of pure reason. The three §§ answer them in order.

### **§ 1. Der überlieferte Begriff der Metaphysik**

The horizon within which Kant saw metaphysics, and within which his own grounding had to begin, is roughly marked by Baumgarten's definition: metaphysics is the science containing the first principles of what human knowing grasps. In the concept of "first principles of human knowledge" lies a peculiar and initially necessary ambiguity — first principles may mean the highest beings from which everything else derives, or the fundamental determinations of anything that is. Baumgarten's division follows: ontology, cosmology, psychology and natural theology belong to metaphysics. Heidegger's marginal gloss sharpens the definition — metaphysics is the first science insofar as it contains the governing grounds of what human knowing *represents* — shifting the accent from what knowing grasps to how it represents, which is the direction the whole book will take.

The motives and history of the formation and consolidation of this school concept are not to be presented here; only a brief pointer at what is essential, to loosen up the problematic content of the concept and prepare an understanding of the fundamental significance of Kant's starting point. A footnote names the Kant research of Pichler, Heimsoeth, Wundt, Kroner and Nicolai Hartmann, and registers the objection that governs Heidegger's whole relation to it: they all hold fast from the outset to the conception of the *Critique* as epistemology, and now set out the metaphysics and the "metaphysical motives" alongside it. Setting metaphysics *alongside* epistemology is precisely what Heidegger refuses; the *Critique* is either a laying of the ground of metaphysics or it is misread.

It is known that the initially book-technical meaning of *meta ta physika* — a collective name for those treatises of Aristotle placed after the ones belonging to the "Physics" — later flipped over into a philosophically interpretive characterization of what those treatises contain. This flip does not have the harmlessness usually attributed to it. It pressed the interpretation of these treatises in a wholly determinate direction and thereby decided the conception of what Aristotle treats there as "metaphysics." Whether what is collected in the Aristotelian "Metaphysics" is metaphysics at all must be doubted. A cataloguing accident is thus made responsible for a doctrine; and the doubt Heidegger enters here is the seed of his later readings of Aristotle against the metaphysical tradition that claims him.

Kant himself still wants to assign the expression a directly substantive meaning, and Heidegger quotes the metaphysics lectures: since *physis* means nature, and we reach concepts of nature only through experience, the science that follows upon it is called metaphysics, from *meta*, trans, and *physica* — a science lying as it were outside the domain of physics, beyond it. Kant reads the accident as an insight, which is exactly the naturalization Heidegger has just called into doubt.

The book-technical expression that occasioned this substantive interpretation itself sprang from an embarrassment in the substantive understanding of the writings thus ordered in the Aristotelian corpus. For precisely what Aristotle aims at there as *prōtē philosophia*, philosophizing in the first place, later school philosophy — logic, physics, ethics — had no discipline and no frame into which it could be fitted. *Meta ta physika* is the title for a fundamental philosophical embarrassment. The point is not that the editors blundered but that they had nowhere to put first philosophy, because first philosophy is not a region alongside others.

The embarrassment in turn had its ground in the unclarity of the essence of the problems and cognitions discussed in those treatises. So far as Aristotle expresses himself about it, a remarkable doubling shows up precisely in the determination of the essence of "first philosophy." It is both knowledge of beings as beings and knowledge of the most eminent region of beings, from out of which beings as a whole are determined.

This double characterization contains neither two fundamentally different, mutually independent trains of thought, nor may one be weakened or eliminated in favour of the other, nor may the apparent duality be prematurely reconciled into a unity. What is needed is to illuminate the grounds of the apparent duality and the manner of the belonging-together of the two determinations, out of the guiding problem of a "first philosophy" of beings. The task becomes the more pressing in that the doubling does not first appear with Aristotle but has governed the problem of being since the beginnings of ancient philosophy. This is the *ontologische Differenz* being read out of a philological crux; and Heidegger's refusal to reconcile the two determinations too quickly is his standing methodological signature — the apparent contradiction is the phenomenon, not an obstacle to it.

To hold fast to this problem of the essential determination of "metaphysics," one may say by way of anticipation: metaphysics is the fundamental knowledge of beings as such and in the whole. This "definition" may count only as an indication of the problem — the questions where the essence of the knowledge of the being of beings lies, how far this necessarily unfolds into a knowledge of beings as a whole, and why this in turn sharpens into a knowledge of the knowledge of being. So "metaphysics" remains the title for the embarrassment of philosophy as such. The third question is the one that makes the book possible: a metaphysics of metaphysics is demanded by the structure of the problem itself, not imposed on it.

Post-Aristotelian Western metaphysics owes its formation not to the taking over and continuation of a supposedly existing Aristotelian system, but to the failure to understand the questionworthiness and openness in which Plato and Aristotle left the central problems. Two motives principally determined the formation of the school concept of metaphysics and increasingly prevented the original problematic from being taken up again. What made the tradition possible was a failure of hearing; the openness of the Greek beginning was closed by being systematized.

The first motive concerns the content-division of metaphysics and stems from the Christian faith-interpretation of the world. On that interpretation all non-divine beings are created — the universe; among creatures the human being has a distinguished position insofar as everything depends on the salvation of his soul and his eternal existence. Accordingly the whole of beings is divided into God, nature and the human being, to which regions theology, whose object is the *summum ens*, cosmology, and psychology are then promptly assigned. These make up the discipline of *metaphysica specialis*; in distinction from it, *metaphysica generalis* (ontology) has beings "in general" (*ens commune*) as its object. The tripartite architecture of school metaphysics is thus a theological inheritance, not a discovery about beings — which is why the human being's privileged place in it is a doctrinal residue rather than an argued result, a point the Fourth Section will exploit.

The second motive concerns metaphysics' manner of knowing and its method. Since it has beings in general and the highest being as its object, in which "everyone takes an interest" (Kant), it is the science of the highest dignity, the "queen of the sciences." Its manner of knowing must accordingly be the strictest and unconditionally binding, which requires that it conform itself to a corresponding ideal of knowledge. Mathematical knowledge counts as that ideal: rational and a priori in the highest sense, independent of contingent experience, hence pure science of reason. Knowledge of beings in general and according to their principal regions thus becomes a "science from mere reason." Metaphysics borrows its ideal of rigour from a domain foreign to it; the borrowing, not any particular doctrine, is what will fail.

Kant holds fast to the intention of this metaphysics, and even shifts it more strongly into *metaphysica specialis*, which he calls "metaphysics proper," "metaphysics in its final purpose." But in the face of the constant miscarriage of all attempts in this science, its inconsistency and ineffectiveness, all attempts to extend pure rational knowledge must first be interdicted until the question of the inner possibility of this science is clarified. So arises the task of a laying of the ground in the sense of an essential determination of metaphysics. Kant's critique is thus conservative in aim and revolutionary in effect: he wants what the tradition wanted, and the demand that it first show its possibility is what destroys it.

### **§ 2. Der Ansatz der Grundlegung der überlieferten Metaphysik**

In metaphysics as the pure rational knowledge of the "universal" in beings and of the respective totality of their principal regions, a "crossing-over" is performed beyond what experience can offer as particular and partial. Crossing over the sensible, this knowledge seeks to grasp the supersensible being. But its procedure has hitherto been "a mere groping, and, what is worst, among mere concepts" (B xv). Metaphysics lacks any binding demonstration of the insights it claims. What gives this metaphysics the inner possibility of being what it wants to be? The crossing-over is registered as a fact about metaphysics before it is analysed; that word, *Überschritt*, will shortly become *Transzendenz*, and the whole book is the analysis of it.

A laying of the ground in the sense of a delimitation of inner possibility must above all aim at the final purpose of metaphysics, an essential determination of *metaphysica specialis*, since this is knowledge of the supersensible being in an exceptional sense. But the question of the inner possibility of such knowledge finds itself thrown back upon the more general question of the inner possibility of a making-manifest of beings as such at all. Laying the ground is now the illumination of the essence of a comportment toward beings in which they show themselves at themselves, such that all assertion about them becomes demonstrable from there. The question about a special region of beings is deflected into the question about access to beings as such — and the criterion of demonstrability is set by the beings' showing themselves, not by the coherence of concepts.

What belongs to the possibility of such comportment? Is there an "indication" of what makes it possible? There is: the procedure of the natural scientists. A light dawned on them; they grasped that reason sees only what it itself produces according to its own projection, that it must go ahead with principles of its judgments according to constant laws and compel nature to answer its questions, rather than letting itself be led by nature as if on a leash (B xiii f.). The plan of a nature in general, projected beforehand, first gives in advance the constitution of being of the beings to which all investigating questioning is to be able to comport itself. This prior plan of the being of beings is inscribed in the fundamental concepts and principles of the science of nature in question. What makes comportment toward beings possible — ontic knowledge — is therefore the prior understanding of the constitution of being: ontological knowledge. The distinction that carries the entire book is introduced here, and it is introduced from the practice of physics rather than from a theory of consciousness.

Mathematical natural science gives an indication of this fundamental conditioning-relation between ontic experience and ontological knowledge. But its function for the laying of the ground of metaphysics is exhausted in that. Pointing to the relation is not yet the solution of the problem, only a statement of the direction in which the problem, understood in fundamental generality, must first be sought. Whether it can be found there alone, or at all — that is, whether the idea of a *metaphysica specialis* may be projected at all on the measure of the concept of positive scientific knowledge — is precisely what remains to be decided. The natural scientist's insight is a clue, not a model. Heidegger is guarding the reading against exactly the neo-Kantian conclusion he will name in §3.

The projection of the inner possibility of *metaphysica specialis* is led back, by way of the question of the possibility of ontic knowledge, to the question of the possibility of what makes ontic knowledge possible. But that is the problem of the essence of the prior understanding of being, of ontological knowledge in the widest sense. And the problem of the inner possibility of ontology contains the question of the possibility of *metaphysica generalis*. The attempt to lay the ground of *metaphysica specialis* is thus pressed back within itself onto the question of the essence of *metaphysica generalis*. The specialized disciplines of school metaphysics are not refuted but bypassed: asking after their possibility forces the enquiry into general ontology, and Heidegger has thereby made the *Critique* into a book about ontology without having to argue that God, world and soul are illegitimate topics.

With a laying of the ground that begins in this way, Kant brings himself immediately into dialogue with Aristotle and Plato. Ontology now becomes a problem at all for the first time. With that the first and innermost tremor enters the edifice of traditional metaphysics. The indeterminacy and obviousness vanish in which *metaphysica generalis* had hitherto treated of the "generality" of the *ens commune*. The question of the laying of the ground demands for the first time clarity about the kind of universalization, and thereby about the character of the crossing-over, that lies in the knowledge of the constitution of being. Whether Kant himself reaches full clarification of the problem is a subordinate question; enough that he recognized its necessity and above all presented it. It becomes clear too that ontology is not primarily related to the grounding of the positive sciences at all: its necessity and its role are grounded in a "higher interest" that human reason finds in itself. And because *metaphysica generalis* provides the necessary equipment for *metaphysica specialis*, the essential determination of the latter must also transform itself in the laying of the ground of the former. The concession about Kant's own clarity is not modesty but licence: Heidegger has secured the right to press the problem further than Kant took it, which is what the Third Section does.

Laying the ground of metaphysics as a whole means unveiling the inner possibility of ontology. That is the genuine — because metaphysical, that is, related to metaphysics as the sole theme — sense of what is constantly misinterpreted under the title of Kant's "Copernican turn." Kant's text is quoted: hitherto it was assumed that all our knowledge must conform to objects, but all attempts to make out something about them a priori through concepts came to nothing on that presupposition; let it therefore be tried whether we do not make better progress in the tasks of metaphysics by assuming that objects must conform to our knowledge, which already agrees better with the demanded possibility of an a priori knowledge of them that is to establish something about objects before they are given to us (B xvi).

Kant means: not "all knowledge" is ontic, and where ontic knowledge is present it becomes possible only through an ontological one. The Copernican turn so little shakes the "old" concept of truth as the conforming (*adaequatio*) of knowledge to beings that it presupposes it, indeed first grounds it. Ontic knowledge can conform to beings ("objects") only if these beings are already manifest beforehand as beings, that is, known in their constitution of being. It is to this last knowledge that objects must conform — that is, their ontic determinability. The manifestness of beings (ontic truth) revolves around the unveiledness of the constitution of being of beings (ontological truth); ontic knowledge can never conform "to" objects on its own, because without the ontological it does not even have a possible "to what." The Copernican turn is thus read as a thesis about the founding of truth rather than about the mind's contribution to the object. The pairing of manifestness and unveiledness restates *Sein und Zeit* §44 — uncoveredness of beings founded in disclosedness of being — in a vocabulary from which the word Dasein has been withdrawn, and this is the passage to cite for the continuity of the two books on truth.

So the laying of the ground of traditional metaphysics begins with the question of the inner possibility of ontology as such. Why does this laying of the ground become a "critique of pure reason"?

### **§ 3. Die Grundlegung der Metaphysik als „Kritik der reinen Vernunft"**

Kant brings the problem of the possibility of ontology to the question: how are synthetic judgments a priori possible? Interpreting this problem-formula explains why the laying of the ground of metaphysics is carried out as a critique of pure reason. The question of the possibility of ontological knowledge requires a preliminary characterization of that knowledge, and Kant, in accordance with the tradition, grasps knowing as judging. That inherited equation of knowing with judging is registered without protest here; the book will end by dismantling it, since the whole doctrine of the imagination shows that judgment is not the seat of ontological knowledge.

What kind of knowledge is present in ontological understanding? Beings are known in it. But what is known there belongs to beings, however they may be experienced and determined. This known what-being of beings is supplied in ontological knowledge before all ontic experience, and yet precisely for it, a priori. A knowledge that supplies the what-content of beings, or unveils beings themselves, Kant calls "synthetic." So the question of the possibility of ontological knowledge becomes the problem of the essence of synthetic judgments a priori. The point to hold is that the a priori is not prior in the sense of being independent of experience but prior *for* experience — a temporal-sounding "before" that the Fourth Stage will show to be temporal in the strict sense.

The instance grounding the legitimacy of these substantive judgments about the being of beings cannot lie in experience, since experience of beings is itself always already guided by the ontological understanding of those beings which experience is to make accessible in a determinate respect. Ontological knowledge is accordingly a judging according to grounds (principles) not suppliable in the manner of experience. The circularity is deliberate: experience cannot warrant what makes experience possible.

Kant calls our faculty of knowing from a priori principles "pure reason" — that which contains the principles of knowing something absolutely a priori (A 11, B 24). Insofar as the principles contained in reason make up the possibility of an a priori knowledge, the unveiling of the possibility of ontological knowledge must become an illumination of the essence of pure reason. But delimiting the essence of pure reason is at the same time the differentiating determination of its non-essence (*Unwesen*), and thereby the bounding and restriction — the critique — of pure reason to its essential possibilities. Laying the ground of metaphysics as the unveiling of the essence of ontology is "critique of pure reason." Critique is heard as limitation in the constructive sense: a boundary determines an essence rather than curtailing it, and *Unwesen* is not corruption but the range of what falls outside the essence.

Ontological knowledge, the a priori "synthesis," is what "the whole critique is really there for" (A 14, B 28). All the more urgently, then, does this synthesis require closer determination at the very moment the guiding problem is fixed. Kant uses the term in manifold meanings, and precisely in the problem-formula of the laying of the ground these meanings intertwine. Naming an ambiguity in Kant's central term at the point where the problem is being set is not pedantry: the three senses about to be distinguished are what let Heidegger locate a synthesis that is neither predication nor empirical combination, and that synthesis is the transcendental imagination.

The question asks after the possibility of judgments synthetic a priori. Every judgment as such is already an "I combine" — namely subject and predicate. As judgments, even "analytic" judgments are already synthetic, though the ground of the agreement of the subject-predicate connection lies solely in the representation of the subject. Synthetic judgments are then "synthetic" in a twofold sense: first as judgments at all, second insofar as the legitimacy of the connection of representations is supplied from the beings themselves about which the judgment is made.

But in the synthetic judgments a priori that have been made a problem, a still different manner of synthesis is at issue. This synthesis is to supply something about beings that is not drawn from them in the manner of experience. This supplying of the determination of being of beings is a prior relating-to beings, and this pure "relation to..." first forms the "to what" and the horizon within which beings become experienceable at themselves in the empirical synthesis. It is this a priori synthesis whose possibility must be illuminated. An investigation concerning the essence of this synthesis Kant calls a transcendental one: "I call all knowledge transcendental that is occupied not so much with objects as with our manner of knowing objects insofar as this is to be possible a priori" (B 25, A 11). Horizon is the decisive word. The third synthesis is not a joining of two given items but the opening of a field in which items can be given at all, and every later determination of the imagination will be a determination of this horizon-forming.

Transcendental knowledge therefore investigates not beings themselves but the possibility of the prior understanding of being — which at the same time means: the constitution of being of beings. It concerns the crossing-over (transcendence) of pure reason to beings, such that experience can now first conform to them as possible objects. Transcendence is here not a relation to something beyond the world but the passage from the understanding of being to the beings that can consequently be met; the "beyond" crossed is the boundary of what is merely given.

To make the possibility of ontology a problem means to ask about the possibility, that is, the essence, of this transcendence of the understanding of being — to philosophize transcendentally. That is why Kant, to make the problematic of traditional ontology recognizable, uses for *metaphysica generalis* the designation "transcendental philosophy," and why in mentioning that traditional ontology he speaks of the "transcendental philosophy of the ancients" (B 113). Kant's own name for his enterprise is thus read as a name for ontology, and the phrase about the ancients is Heidegger's warrant for taking transcendental philosophy to be the continuation of the Greek question of being rather than a modern departure from it.

The *Critique* nevertheless gives no "system" of transcendental philosophy but is "a treatise on method" (B xxii). That does not mean a doctrine of the technique of procedure, but the working out of a complete determination of the "whole outline" and the "whole inner articulation" of ontology. In this laying of the ground of metaphysics as the projection of the inner possibility of ontology, "the whole preliminary sketch of a system of metaphysics is delineated" (B xxiii).

The intention of the *Critique of Pure Reason* is accordingly misconstrued in principle if the work is interpreted as a "theory of experience" or even as a theory of the positive sciences. The *Critique* has nothing to do with "epistemology." If one could let the epistemological interpretation stand at all, one would have to say: the *Critique* is not a theory of ontic knowledge (experience) but a theory of ontological knowledge. But even with this conception — already remote from the prevailing interpretation of the Transcendental Aesthetic and Analytic — the essential is not hit, namely that ontology as *metaphysica generalis*, as the foundational part of metaphysics as a whole, is here grounded and for the first time brought to itself. With the problem of transcendence, "epistemology" is not put in the place of metaphysics; rather ontology is questioned as to its inner possibility. The concession is precisely calibrated: even the improved epistemological reading is refused, because any epistemology takes knowledge as its theme, whereas the *Critique* takes being as its theme and knowledge only as the place where being is understood.

If truth belongs to the essence of a knowledge, then the transcendental problem of the inner possibility of synthetic a priori knowledge is the question of the essence of the truth of ontological transcendence. What must be determined is the essence of "transcendental truth," which "precedes all empirical truth and makes it possible" (A 146, B 185) — for "no knowledge can contradict it without at the same time losing all content, that is, all relation to any object, hence all truth" (A 62 f., B 87). Ontic truth necessarily conforms to ontological truth. That is once again the legitimate interpretation of the sense of the "Copernican turn." With this turn Kant presses the problem of ontology into the centre. For the problematic of the possibility of original ontological truth nothing may be presupposed, least of all the "fact" of the truth of the positive sciences. Heidegger has now located within Kant's own text a truth that precedes empirical truth and grounds it, which is the Kantian name for what *Sein und Zeit* called the disclosedness in which alone assertions can be true. The refusal to presuppose the fact of scientific truth is aimed directly at the Marburg school, for whom that fact was the starting point.

The laying of the ground must rather trace the a priori synthesis back within itself alone to the germs underlying it, which let that synthesis grow into what it is — which make it possible in its essence. Kant himself says of the *Critique*, out of clear insight into the peculiarity of a laying of the ground of metaphysics, that the work is difficult and requires a resolute reader willing to think himself little by little into a system that lays nothing as given at the ground except reason itself, and thus, without supporting itself on any fact, seeks to develop knowledge out of its original germs (*Prolegomena* §4). The word "germs" (*Keime*) is Kant's own, and Heidegger takes it as authorization for the regress of the Third Section: a germ is what a thing grows from, so following the synthesis back to its germs is not construction imposed on Kant but the programme Kant announced.

So arises the task of showing how this development of the possibility of ontology out of its germs is carried out.

## **Zweiter Abschnitt: Die Grundlegung der Metaphysik in der Durchführung**

Before the inner possibility of ontological knowledge can be projected, a survey must already have opened onto the dimension of the regress to the bearing ground. It is the necessary fate of every genuine break-in to a hitherto concealed field that the field determines itself only gradually; the direction of access consolidates, and the way becomes passable, only in the course of the advance. So even where the first break-in remains guided by the security and unerring directive force of creative disclosing, an explicit systematic clearing and marking-out of the field is at first lacking. "Critiques require knowledge of the sources, and reason must know itself" — yet Kant works out this more original self-knowledge of reason only through the *Critique*. What Kant lacked is not insight but a map; and the excuse Heidegger offers him here is the same one he will withdraw in the Third Section, where the failure to follow the root is no longer a matter of missing cartography.

The interpretation that comes after, by contrast, is neither yet nor any longer in possession of the original directive force of the projecting, and must therefore secure the guiding survey explicitly in advance and anticipate the main stages of the inner movement of the whole. Before the execution can be re-enacted, the survey of the dimension of regress has to be supplied. Coming late has one advantage — the whole is visible — and one deficit, the loss of the discoverer's instinct; §§4–8 exist to compensate for the second by exploiting the first.

The Section accordingly falls into two parts: the characterization of the dimension of regress, and the stages of the execution of the projection of the inner possibility of ontology.

### **A. Die Kennzeichnung der Rückgangsdimension für die Durchführung der Grundlegung der Metaphysik**

The task is the essential determination of ontological knowledge by illuminating its origin out of the germs that make it possible. That requires clarity above all about the essence of knowing as such, and about the place and kind of the field of origin. Precisely this prior and sufficient characterization of the dimension of origin has been unduly neglected or misinterpreted in the interpretation of the *Critique* to date, so that, given the further wavering about the work's intention, no productive appropriation of its basic tendency could succeed. Along with the characterization of the field, the manner of the unveiling of the origin must also be marked out in its peculiarity.

#### **I. Die wesentlichen Charaktere des Ursprungsfeldes**

##### **§ 4. Das Wesen der Erkenntnis überhaupt**

Kant does not discuss the essential characters of the field of origin explicitly and thematically but takes them up more in the manner of "self-evident presuppositions." All the less may the interpretation overlook the function these positings exercise in advance. A marginal note asks for the point to be developed more sharply by starting from the difference between knowledge as representing and knowledge as knowing, with knowledge as knowing the guiding concept — a sharpening Heidegger carried out only in the 1935/36 lectures on the thing.

The presuppositions gather into a thesis. The source-ground for the laying of the ground of metaphysics is human pure reason, and in such a way that precisely the *humanity* of reason — its finitude — becomes essential for the core of the problem. The characterization of the field of origin must therefore concentrate on clarifying the essence of the finitude of human knowledge. This finitude by no means consists first or chiefly in the fact that human knowing exhibits all sorts of defects of inconstancy, inexactness and error; it lies in the essential structure of knowledge itself. The factical limitedness of knowing is only a consequence of that essence. Finitude is thus removed at the outset from the register of complaint. It is not that we know less than a god; it is that the structure by which we know anything at all is the structure of being dependent on what we did not make.

Exhibiting the essence of finitude requires a general characterization of the essence of knowing, and in this respect what Kant says in the first sentence of the thematic discussion of the *Critique* is usually valued far too low: whatever the way and the means by which a knowledge may relate to objects, intuition is that by which it relates to them immediately, and to which all thinking as a means is directed (A 19, B 33, italicized by Kant himself). For any understanding of the *Critique* one must hammer in: knowing is primarily intuiting. The marginalia press the point in both directions. Intuiting means here having the being itself manifest by letting it be given receptively; and "primarily" means at the bottom of knowing's essence *as finite*, so that thinking belongs necessarily to that same essence as the secondary — secondary in the order of the structure's build-up, not in the popular sense of "basically dispensable." Precisely because knowing is primarily intuition, an intuition on its own is never for us a knowledge.

Reinterpreting knowledge as judging (thinking) therefore violates the decisive sense of Kant's problem, since all thinking has merely a serving position with respect to intuition. Thinking is not "also" present alongside intuition; by its inner structure it serves what intuition primarily and constantly aims at. But if thinking is to be essentially related to intuition, both must have a certain inner kinship permitting their unification, and that kinship — descent from the same genus — is expressed in Kant's remark that for both "representation in general (repraesentatio)" is the genus (A 320, B 376 f.). A further marginal note refuses to let "serving" be heard as subordination: the understanding does indeed tower over intuition — in finitude and in neediness — and the greater that primacy, the more unconditional the dependence on intuition, and the less it can be dispensed with.

"Representation" here first has the wide formal sense in which something indicates, announces, presents another. Such representing can be performed "with consciousness"; there belongs to it a knowing of the announcing and of the being-announced (*perceptio*). And when in representing something through something not only the representing but what is represented in it is represented as such, that is, made conscious, then such representing is a relating-oneself to what presents itself in the representing. Knowledge is a representing in this sense of "objective perception."

Knowing representation is either intuition or concept (*intuitus vel conceptus*): the former relates immediately to the object and is singular, the latter mediately, by means of a mark that can be common to several things. On the first sentence of the *Critique*, then, knowing is a *thinking intuiting*. Thinking — representing "in general" — serves only to make the individual object, the concrete being itself in its immediacy, accessible, and accessible for everyone. "Each of these two is indeed representation, but not yet knowledge." A marginal note adds Kant's polemical placement against Locke and Leibniz: sensibility and understanding are "two entirely different sources of representations," which blocks in advance the reading of the difference as one of degrees of clarity.

One could infer from this a reciprocal and entirely equally weighted relation between intuiting and thinking, so that one might say with equal right: knowing is an intuiting thinking, hence at bottom judging.

Against that it must be held fast that intuition makes up the proper essence of knowledge and, for all the reciprocity of the relation, carries the proper weight. This emerges not only from Kant's declaration and from his own italicization of the word, but from the fact that only on this interpretation can the essential thing in the definition be grasped — the finitude of knowledge. The first sentence of the *Critique* is already no longer a definition of knowing as such but the essential determination of *human* knowledge: "as regards the human being" — in distinction from "God or another higher spirit" — "every knowledge of his consists of concept and intuition." Reading the sentence as a definition of knowledge as such is what produces the epistemological Kant; reading it as a determination of finite knowledge is what produces the metaphysical one.

The essence of finite human knowledge is elucidated by setting it off against the idea of infinite divine knowledge, the *intuitus originarius* (B 72). But divine knowledge is intuition not because it is divine; it is intuition because it is knowledge at all. The difference between infinite and finite intuition consists in this: infinite intuition, in immediately representing the individual — the one unique being as a whole — first brings this being into its being and helps it to its arising (*origo*). Absolute intuiting would not be absolute if it were dependent on an already present being, in conformity with which the intuitable first became accessible. Divine knowing is that representing which in intuiting first creates the intuitable being as such. And because it intuits beings immediately as a whole, seeing through them absolutely in advance, it needs no thinking. Thinking as such is therefore already the seal of finitude: divine knowledge is "intuition (for all his knowledge must be such and not thinking, which always proves limits)" (B 71). A marginal note supplies the correlate — such knowing is free of all sensibility and at the same time free of the need to know through concepts. The infinite intellect is not a superior version of ours but a structurally different one, and it is introduced solely as a contrast-foil: Heidegger is not asserting that it exists.

The decisive difference would be missed, however, if one said: divine knowing is only intuiting, human knowing is a thinking intuiting. The essential difference lies primarily in the intuiting itself, since knowing is properly intuition. The finitude of human knowledge must accordingly be sought first in the finitude of its own intuition. That a finite knowing being must "also" think is only an essential consequence of the finitude of its intuiting. Only so does the essential serving position of "all thinking" come into the right light. The whole architecture of the book depends on locating finitude in receptivity rather than in discursivity: if finitude lay in thinking, its overcoming would lie in a better intuition, and the transcendental imagination would have no work to do.

##### **§ 5. Das Wesen der Endlichkeit der Erkenntnis**

Negatively put: finite knowledge is non-creative intuition. What it has to present immediately in its singularity must already be present beforehand. Finite intuition finds itself dependent on the intuitable as something already a being of itself. What is intuited is derived from such a being, which is why this intuition is also called *intuitus derivativus*, derived intuition (B 72). Finite intuition of beings cannot give itself the object; it must let it be given to it. Not every intuition as such, but only finite intuition, is receptive. A marginal note blocks the obvious mistake: "derived" does not mean deduced from absolute intuition, and finite knowing is not a derivative of the absolute — the question of ontic origin is not at issue at all. "Derived" names a structural feature, that finite knowing comes to what it knows from elsewhere instead of creating it.

The character of the finitude of intuition therefore lies in receptivity. But finite intuition cannot take in unless what is to be taken in announces itself; by its essence it must be approached, affected, by what is intuitable in it. Affection is thus deduced from receptivity rather than presupposed as a physical fact — which is what allows the next move.

Because the essence of knowledge lies primarily in intuition, and because the finite essence of the human being stands in the theme, Kant continues immediately after the first sentence: intuition "takes place only insofar as the object is given to us; but this in turn is possible, at least for us humans, only in that it affects the mind in a certain way" (A 19, B 33). The qualification "at least for us humans" is inserted only in the second edition, and it only makes clearer that in the first edition finite knowledge was the theme from the outset. Heidegger needs this, since the second edition is elsewhere his evidence of Kant's retreat; here the addition is a clarification, not a change of mind, and he says so.

Because human intuition as finite takes in, and the possibility of a receptive "getting" demands affection, instruments of affection — the "senses" — are in fact necessary. Human intuition is not "sensible" because its affection happens by means of sense-organs; the reverse: because our Dasein is finite, existing in the midst of beings that already are and delivered over to them, it must take in what already is, that is, offer beings the possibility of announcing themselves. For the possible transmission of that announcement, instruments are necessary. The essence of sensibility consists in the finitude of intuition; the organs serving affection are *sense*-organs because they belong to finite intuition. With this Kant won for the first time an ontological, non-sensualist concept of sensibility. And since empirically affective intuition of beings therefore does not necessarily coincide with "sensibility," the possibility of a *non-empirical sensibility* remains essentially open — pure intuition, space and time, as Kant's own note at B 146 f. confirms. This inference is the linchpin of the whole reading. If sensibility means dependence rather than sensation, a pure sensibility is not a contradiction, and the way is clear for time to be the ground of ontological knowledge.

Knowledge is primarily intuition, a representing that immediately represents the being itself. But if finite intuition is to be knowledge, it must be able to make the being itself accessible as manifest, for everyone and at all times, in what and how it is. Finite intuiting beings must be able to share the intuition with one another. Yet finite intuition, as intuition, at first remains attached to the individual thing intuited on each occasion. What is intuited is a known being only if anyone can make it intelligible to himself and to others and thereby communicate it. This intuited individual, this piece of chalk here, must be determinable as chalk or as body, so that we can together know this being itself as the same for us. To be knowledge, finite intuition needs at all times such a determination of the intuited as this or that. Universality is thus derived from the demand that beings be the same for several knowers, not from a doctrine of concepts — the need for the understanding is a consequence of finitude's isolation in the singular.

In such determining, what is represented in intuition is further represented with a view to what it is "in general." The determining does not thematically represent the universal as such: it does not make the bodiliness of the thing into an object, but takes a look toward the universal only in order, with that in view, to direct itself to the individual and determine it from that look. This "universal" representing, which as such serves intuiting, makes what is represented in intuition more representable, in that it comprehends several under one and on the ground of this embracing "holds for many." Kant therefore calls this representing in general (*repraesentatio per notas communes*) "representing in concepts," and determining representing shows up as the "representation [concept] of a representation" (intuition). Heidegger's marginal note supplies the reason the text leaves implicit: the receptive representing becomes, through the concept, more *taking*, and only so can it have beings at all.

Determining representing is in itself an asserting of something about something, a predication. "The judgment is thus the mediate knowledge of an object, hence the representation of a representation of it" (A 68, B 93). The "faculty of judging" is the understanding, and the representing proper to it makes intuition intelligible.

Insofar as judging determination is essentially dependent on intuition, thinking always unites with intuition in serving it. Through such uniting (synthesis) thinking relates mediately to the object, and the object becomes manifest — true — in the unity of a thinking intuition. The synthesis of thinking and intuiting accordingly performs the making-manifest of the encountered being as object. Heidegger names it the truth-making, *veritative* synthesis, and identifies it with the "supplying" of the substantive determinateness of the being itself discussed in §3. Truth is thereby located in a synthesis that is not the joining of subject and predicate — a displacement that will be decisive when the veritative synthesis reappears in its pure form as the ontological synthesis.

The thinking that unites with intuition in the veritative synthesis is for its part, as judging, a uniting in another sense. Kant: a judgment is the representation of the unity of consciousness of various representations, or the representation of their relation insofar as they make up a concept (*Logik* §17). Judgments are "functions of unity," a representing of the unifying unity of the concept in its predicate-character. Heidegger names this the *predicative* synthesis.

It does not coincide with the uniting in which judging presents itself as the connecting of subject and predicate. That last synthesis Heidegger names the *apophantic*.

In the veritative synthesis, which makes up the essence of finite knowledge as such, the predicative and the apophantic syntheses are necessarily joined into one structural unity of syntheses. Three syntheses are thus nested rather than juxtaposed: the making-manifest of the being contains within itself the concept's unification of many under one, which in turn is articulated in the subject-predicate connection.

Anyone who asserts that for Kant the essence of knowledge is "synthesis" says nothing at all so long as the word is left in its ambiguous indeterminacy. The distinction just drawn is what makes the assertion sayable — and what will let Heidegger identify a fourth, pure synthesis that no analysis of judgment can reach.

Finite intuition, needing determination, is dependent on the understanding. The understanding not only belongs to the finitude of intuition but is itself still more finite, since it lacks even the immediacy of finite intuiting. Its representing needs a detour, a look toward a universal through which and from which the several individuals become conceptually representable. This detour-character, discursivity, belonging to the essence of the understanding, is the sharpest index of its finitude. The understanding is thus doubly demoted: it is dependent, and it is mediate. The tradition's ranking of intellect above sense is exactly inverted, and inverted on the tradition's own criterion of immediacy.

Yet just as the metaphysical essence of finite intuition as receptivity retains in itself the general essential character of intuition — that it is "giving" — so the finitude of the understanding still shows something of the essence of an absolute knowledge, an original, letting-spring-forth intuition. The understanding, related to finite intuition, is as little creative as intuition is; it never produces beings. But in distinction from the taking-in of intuiting it is a kind of bringing-forth. Judging about beings does not simply produce the universal in which the intuited is conceptually represented — the universal is drawn according to its content from what is intuitable. Only the manner in which this content holds as an embracing unity for many is the accomplishment of the understanding.

In producing the form of the concept the understanding helps to supply the content of the object. In this kind of "placing," the peculiar re-presenting of thinking announces itself — the word *Vor-stellen*, placing-before, is broken open to show a placing at work in it. The metaphysical essence of the understanding as thus "producing" is co-determined by this character of "from itself" (spontaneity), but not hit at its core. Spontaneity is conceded and then set aside; it is a true description of the understanding that fails to reach what the understanding is, and the Third Section will supply what does reach it.

The finitude of knowledge has so far been characterized as receptive and therefore thinking intuition, an illumination carried out with a view to the structure of knowing. Given the fundamental significance of finitude for the problem, the essence of finite knowledge is now to be illuminated from another side: with a view to what is knowable in such knowledge.

If finite knowledge is receptive intuition, then the knowable must show itself from itself. What finite knowledge can make manifest is essentially the self-showing being — the appearing, appearance. The title "appearance" means the being itself as object of finite knowledge. More precisely: only for finite knowledge is there anything like an object at all, since only finite knowledge is delivered over to what already is. Nothing already existing can stand over against infinite knowledge as something it would have to direct itself by; such directing-oneself-according-to would already be dependence, hence finitude. Infinite knowing is an intuiting that as such lets the being itself arise. Absolute knowing reveals the being to itself in letting it arise and has it manifest at all times "only" as arising — as *Ent-stand*. The coinage is built on *Gegen-stand*, what stands over against: where the finite knower has beings as standing over against it, the absolute knower has them as standing forth in their very arising. Insofar as the being is manifest for absolute intuition, it "is" precisely in its coming-to-be; it is the being as being in itself, not as object. So even the formula that this intuiting first produces the "object" in intuiting fails to hit the essence of infinite knowledge. A marginal note states the consequence baldly: objectivity is being in the empirical sense — that is, "object" is not a neutral term for what is, but the way beings are for a finite knower.

The being "in appearance" is the same being as the being in itself; indeed only this one being can become an object, though only for a finite knowledge. It reveals itself according to the manner and range of the ability to take in and determine that a finite knowledge has at its disposal. A marginal note fixes the identity precisely: what is the same is not the *what* but the *that* of the X. The thing in itself and the appearance are the same being, not the same content.

Kant uses "appearance" in a narrower and a wider meaning. Appearances in the wider sense (*phaenomena*) are a kind of "object," namely the being itself which finite knowing, as thinking receptive intuition, makes manifest. Appearance in the narrower sense means that in appearances in the wider sense which is exclusively the correlate of the affection belonging to finite intuition, stripped of all thinking: the contents of empirical intuition. "The undetermined object of an empirical intuition is called appearance" (A 20, B 34); appearing means "to be an object of empirical intuition" (A 89, B 121).

Appearances are not mere semblance but the being itself, and this being is not something other than the things in themselves but just this one being. The being itself can be manifest without the being being known "in itself," that is, as *Ent-stand*. The double characterization of the being as "thing in itself" and as "appearance" corresponds to the twofold way in which it can stand in relation to infinite and to finite knowing: the being in its arising, and the same being as object. The thing in itself is thus not a second object hidden behind the first but the same object correlated to a knowing that we do not have.

If the finitude of the human being is the problem-basis for the grounding of ontology in the *Critique*, then the work must lay particular weight on this difference between finite and infinite knowing. Hence Kant says of it that "it teaches us to take the object in two meanings, namely as appearance or as thing in itself" (B xxvii). Strictly one should not speak of "object" at all, since for absolute knowledge there can be no ob-jects — and a marginal note sharpens even that: for God there is no *being* at all, if being belongs to finitude. The *Opus postumum* confirms the reading: the thing in itself is not another being than the appearance, "the distinction of the concepts of a thing in itself and of the thing in appearance is not objective but merely subjective. The thing in itself is not another object but another relation (*respectus*) of the representation to the same object." A further marginal note glosses the noumenon accordingly, as a representation of reason that is not a particular object standing behind the phenomenon. Another note restrains the claim about finitude being the "problem-basis": finitude is not Kant's explicit theme — it is what his theme presupposes, and the difference between those two matters for how much Heidegger may claim to be reporting rather than interpreting.

From this reading, the expressions "behind the appearance" and "mere appearance" can now be clarified. The "behind" cannot mean that the thing in itself does after all stand over against finite knowledge, is merely not grasped "completely," presses its essence forward and occasionally becomes indirectly visible. It expresses rather that finite knowledge as finite necessarily at the same time conceals, and conceals in advance, so that the "thing in itself" is not merely imperfectly but essentially not at all accessible to it as such. What is "behind the appearance" is the same being as the appearance; but because finite knowledge gives the being only in the ob-ject, it does not let that same being be seen as *Ent-stand*. "According to the Critique everything in an appearance is itself again appearance." Concealing is thereby made a positive structural feature of finite knowing rather than a shortfall in it — the Kantian form of the thesis that untruth belongs to the essence of truth, and the passage to hold against §35's problem of transcendental untruth.

It is accordingly a misunderstanding of what "thing in itself" means to believe one must prove by positivistic critique the impossibility of knowing things in themselves. Such attempts still presuppose that the thing in itself is something intended as an object within finite knowledge, whose factical inaccessibility could and would have to be demonstrated. Correspondingly, in the phrase "mere appearance" the "mere" is not a restriction or diminution of the thing's reality but only the denial that the being is known infinitely in human knowledge: "even to the deepest investigation of its objects, in the world of sense, we have to do with nothing but appearances" (A 45, B 62 f.).

The essence of the difference shows itself especially clearly in the double meaning of "outside us" (A 373). In both meanings the being itself is always meant. As thing in itself it is outside us insofar as we, as finite beings, are excluded from the kind of infinite intuition that belongs to it. As appearance it is outside us insofar as we ourselves are not this being and yet do have access to it. Conversely, the discussion shows that these fundamental concepts of the *Critique* can be understood and made into a further problem only when the problematic of the finitude of the human essence is explicitly laid at the ground. They are not two layers of objects stacked behind one another within an indifferently posited "knowledge." Every reading that takes the distinction as one between two sorts of thing has failed at the first step, and Heidegger has now made finitude the sole key to it.

With this characterization of the finitude of human knowledge, the essential features of the dimension within which the laying of the ground moves have been indicated, and with them a clearer prefiguring of the direction the regress to the sources of the inner possibility of ontology must take.

##### **§ 6. Der Quellgrund der Grundlegung der Metaphysik**

The interpretation of the essence of knowledge and of its finitude yielded: finite intuition (sensibility) needs as such to be determined by the understanding, and conversely the already finite understanding is dependent on intuition, "for we can understand nothing except what carries with it in intuition something corresponding to our words" (A 277, B 333). When Kant says that "neither of these properties is to be preferred to the other" (A 51, B 75), this seems to contradict his placing the basic character of knowing in intuition. But the necessary belonging of sensibility and understanding to the essential unity of finite knowledge does not exclude but includes a rank-order in the structural grounding of thinking upon intuition as the leading representing. Precisely this rank-order must not be overlooked in view of their reciprocal belonging-together, nor levelled to an indifferent correlation of content and form, by anyone who wants to come nearer the innermost drift of Kant's problem. Kant's parity claim concerns indispensability, Heidegger's rank-claim concerns founding; they are compatible, and the form/content picture is what conflates them.

For the question of the regress to the source-ground of the possibility of finite knowledge it might now seem enough to stop at the simple reciprocal duality of its elements — the more so as Kant himself orients the "arising" of our knowledge on "two basic sources of the mind," the first being the receiving of representations (the receptivity of impressions), the second the faculty of knowing an object through these representations (the spontaneity of concepts) (A 50, B 74); and says still more sharply that besides these two sources of knowledge we have "no others" (A 294, B 350).

But this duality of sources is no mere juxtaposition. Only in the unification prefigured by their structure can a finite knowledge be what its essence demands: "only from their uniting can knowledge arise" (A 51, B 75 f.). And the unity of this uniting is no subsequent result of the elements' having come together; what unites them, this "synthesis," must let the elements themselves arise in their belonging-together and unity. If finite knowledge has its essence precisely in the original synthesis of the basic sources, and if the laying of the ground must press through to the essential ground of finite knowledge, then already at the prefiguring naming of the "two basic sources" a pointer to their source-ground — an original unity — suggests itself. The argument turns on a single claim, and it is the claim that carries the rest of the book: a unity that first lets its members be what they are cannot be assembled out of them. Hence the root cannot be a compound of the stems.

Kant does in fact give, both in the Introduction to the *Critique* and at its close, a remarkable characterization beyond the mere enumeration. In the Introduction: "only so much seems necessary by way of introduction or preliminary reminder, that there are two stems of human knowledge, which perhaps spring from a common but to us unknown root, namely sensibility and understanding, through the first of which objects are given to us, but through the second thought" (A 15, B 29). And in the Architectonic: "we content ourselves here with the completion of our business, namely merely to sketch the architectonic of all knowledge from pure reason, and begin only from the point where the general root of our cognitive power divides and throws out two stems, one of which is reason. Here, however, I understand by reason the whole higher faculty of knowledge, and thus oppose the rational to the empirical" (A 835, B 863). The "empirical" here means the experiencing receptive, receptivity, sensibility as such.

The "sources" are here grasped as "stems" springing from a common root. But at the first passage the "common root" is named with a "perhaps," while at the second "the general root" counts as existing. At both places, nevertheless, the root is only pointed at. Kant not only does not pursue it but even designates it as "unknown to us." From this follows the one essential thing about the general character of Kant's laying of the ground: it does not lead to the sun-clear absolute evidence of a first proposition and principle but knowingly goes, and points, into the unknown. It is a *philosophizing* laying of the ground of philosophy. The discrepancy between "perhaps" and "the general root" is the textual crack Heidegger works in the Third Section. And the closing formula is a thrust at Husserl as much as at the neo-Kantians: a grounding that terminates in absolute evidence would have made philosophy into a science, and Kant's refusal of that is here counted to his credit.

#### **II. Die Weise der Ursprungsenthüllung**

##### **§ 7. Die Vorzeichnung der Stadien der Grundlegung der Ontologie**

The grounding of metaphysics is the projection of the inner possibility of the a priori synthesis: its essence must be determined and its origin presented out of its source-ground. The elucidation of finite knowledge and the characterization of the basic sources have circumscribed the dimension in which the origin is to be unveiled. With that, the question of the inner possibility of synthetic a priori knowledge receives both a sharpening and a complication.

The preparatory laying-out of the problem yielded that knowledge of beings is possible only on the ground of a prior, experience-free knowledge of the constitution of being of beings. But finite knowledge, whose finitude is in question, is by its essence a receptive determining intuition of beings. If finite knowledge of beings is to be possible, it must ground itself on a knowing of the being of beings that lies before all receiving. Finite knowledge of beings therefore requires for its own possibility a non-receptive — apparently non-finite — knowing, something like a "creative" intuiting.

So the question of the possibility of the a priori synthesis sharpens: how can a finite being, which as such is delivered over to beings and dependent on taking them in, know — that is, intuit — beings before all taking-in, without being their "creator"? Put otherwise: how must this finite being be according to its own constitution of being for such an experience-free supplying of the constitution of being of beings, an ontological synthesis, to be possible? The paradox is stated as sharply as possible rather than softened, and the whole remainder of the book is its resolution: the answer will be that the finite knower produces not the being but the horizon within which beings can be met, and that it does so by being time.

If the question is posed this way, and every knowledge as finite is doubled into the two named elements and hence is itself synthesis, a peculiar complication enters the question of the possibility of the a priori synthesis. For this synthesis is not identical with the veritative synthesis, which concerns only ontic knowledge.

The ontological synthesis is, as knowledge at all, already synthetic, so the laying of the ground must begin by exhibiting the pure elements — pure intuition and pure thinking — of a pure knowledge. Then it must be illuminated what character the original essential unity of these pure elements, the pure veritative synthesis, has. This unity is to be such that it likewise determines pure intuition a priori: the concepts belonging to it must arise before all experience not only in their conceptual form but also in their content. Which means that the pure predicative synthesis necessarily belonging to the pure veritative synthesis is one of a distinguished kind, and that the question of the essence of the "ontological predicates" must move into the centre of the problem. Concepts whose *content* is a priori are not concepts in the ordinary sense at all, and naming this in advance is what makes the categories a problem rather than a list.

The question of the inner possibility of the essential unity of a pure veritative synthesis presses still further back, to the illumination of the original ground of that inner possibility. Only through unveiling the essence of pure synthesis from its ground does the insight arise how far ontological knowledge can be the condition of the possibility of ontic knowledge; and with that the full essence of ontological truth is delimited.

The laying of the ground of ontology accordingly runs through five stages: the essential elements of pure knowledge; the essential unity of pure knowledge; the inner possibility of the essential unity of the ontological synthesis; the ground of the inner possibility of the ontological synthesis; and the full essential determination of ontological knowledge. The five stages are not Kant's own division and do not answer to the Aesthetic, Analytic and Dialectic; they are the "inner drift" Heidegger claims to read behind the architectonic, and the claim will be defended in the introduction to part B.

##### **§ 8. Die Methode der Ursprungsenthüllung**

Already the preliminary characterization of the essential structure of finite knowledge showed a wealth of interconnected structures of synthesis. Insofar as the pure veritative synthesis contains the idea of a knowledge that in a certain sense apparently is not finite, the question of the possibility of ontology for a finite being became complicated. And the indication of the source-ground of the basic sources led into the unknown.

Given this character of the guiding problem and of the dimension in which it can be worked on, it is not surprising that the kind of unveiling of the origin and the way of the regress into the source-ground remain at first indeterminate. Their security and determinacy grow only in the course of the advance into the hitherto concealed region and in the confrontation with what shows itself there. Method cannot be settled in advance of a domain that is itself not yet secured; this is Heidegger's standing objection to method-first philosophy, and here it is Kant's own procedure that exemplifies it.

The region of the unveiling of the origin is, to be sure, nothing other than the human "mind" (*mens sive animus*), and its disclosure would be assigned to "psychology." Insofar as an interpretation of "knowledge" is at issue, whose essence commonly counts as judging (*logos*), "logic" must be involved in the disclosure of the mind as well. Taken externally, "psychology" and "logic" will divide the task, or quarrel over primacy, expanding and reshaping themselves as convenient.

But considering on the one hand the originality and incomparability of what Kant is seeking, and on the other the questionableness of the traditional "logic" and "psychology," which are not cut for such a problematic at all, it proves hopeless to grasp what is essential in Kant's laying of the ground by the guiding thread of a "logical" or "psychological" formulation of the question, or in an external linking of the two. That "transcendental psychology" is only the expression of an embarrassment becomes clear as soon as one has grasped the fundamental and methodological difficulties that face the determination of the finite essence of the human being. The label is dismissed not because it is unkind to Kant but because it names a discipline that does not exist: there is no psychology of what makes psychology's object possible.

So only one course remains: to leave the method of the unveiling of the origin open and not press it prematurely into a traditional or invented discipline. In leaving the character of the method open, one must keep in memory what Kant himself said about the work immediately after finishing the *Critique*: "this kind of investigation will always remain difficult" (letter to Marcus Herz, 1781).

A general pointer at the fundamental character of the procedure is nevertheless needed. The kind of investigation can be grasped as "analytic" in the widest sense: it concerns finite pure reason with a view to how, from the ground of its essence, it makes something like ontological synthesis possible. Kant therefore designates the *Critique* as a "study of our inner nature" (A 703, B 731), and this unveiling of the essence of human Dasein is for the philosopher "even a duty." A marginal note refers the procedure to transcendental reflection, which locates it within Kant's own vocabulary rather than Heidegger's.

"Analytic" here does not mean a dissolving and smashing of finite pure reason into elements, but on the contrary a "dissolving" in the sense of a loosening laying-free of the germs of ontology — a marginal note makes the sense unmistakable by glossing it as making fluid, bringing into flow. It unveils those conditions that let an ontology as a whole germinate according to its inner possibility. Such analytic is, in Kant's own words, a bringing "to light by reason itself" of "what reason wholly brings forth out of itself" (A xx). Analytic thus becomes a letting-be-seen of the genesis of the essence of finite pure reason out of its own ground. Analysis is inverted into genesis, which is why the Kantbuch can end in a doctrine of temporality rather than in a table of conditions.

Such analytic therefore contains a projecting anticipation of the whole inner essence of finite pure reason. Only by constructing through this essence does the essential structure of ontology become visible; and as so unveiled, that structure at the same time determines the construction of the foundations it requires. This projecting laying-free of everything that essentially makes an ontology possible brings metaphysics onto the ground and soil in which it is rooted as a "visitation" (*Heimsuchung*) of human nature. Kant's word at B xv for metaphysics as an affliction that keeps returning is taken over without irony: metaphysics is not a discipline one takes up but something that happens to a finite being, and the Fourth Section will make that the whole content of the question about the human being.

### **B. Die Stadien der Durchführung des Entwurfs der inneren Möglichkeit der Ontologie**

At this point the interpretation must assure itself anew, and with increasing sharpness, of the guiding problem. What is asked is the essential possibility of the ontological synthesis. Laid out, the question runs: how can finite human Dasein in advance cross over — transcend — beings that it has not only not created itself, but on which it is even dependent in order to be able to exist as Dasein at all? The problem of the possibility of ontology is thus the question of the essence and the essential ground of the transcendence of the prior understanding of being. The problem of the transcendental — that is, transcendence-forming — synthesis can therefore also be posed thus: how must the finite being we call the human being be according to its innermost essence, in order to be able to be open at all to beings that it is not itself and that must therefore be able to show themselves from themselves? Transcendence has now been defined entirely in terms of openness to what one did not make, which is why the answer cannot be a faculty of the subject in the ordinary sense; and the word Dasein enters the argument at exactly the point where the question turns from knowledge to the constitution of being of the knower.

The stages of an answer were prefigured above; now they are to be run through individually, though without an interpretation that exhausts everything equally. The interpretation follows the inner movement of Kant's laying of the ground without holding to his own disposition and its formulation. It must go back behind that disposition, in order to be able to estimate, from a more original understanding of the inner drift, the appropriateness, the right and the limits of the outer architectonic of the *Critique of Pure Reason*. The methodological licence for everything that follows is stated openly here: Heidegger is not claiming to expound Kant's order but to recover the movement that order imperfectly registers, and he undertakes in advance to justify the departure by the light it sheds on the architectonic itself.

#### **Das erste Stadium der Grundlegung: Die Wesenselemente der reinen Erkenntnis**

If the essence of a synthetic a priori knowledge is to be laid before our eyes, the stock of its necessary elements must first be illuminated. As knowing, the transcendental synthesis must be an intuition, and as a priori knowing a pure intuition; and as pure knowing belonging to the finitude of the human being, pure intuition must necessarily determine itself through a pure thinking. The first stage is thus dictated by the results of §§4–5 and not by the *Critique*'s division into Aesthetic and Analytic, though it will run parallel to it.

##### **a) Die reine Anschauung im endlichen Erkennen**

###### **§ 9. Die Aufhellung von Raum und Zeit als reinen Anschauungen**

Can anything like a pure intuiting be found in the finite knowing of beings? What is sought is an immediate, though experience-free, letting-something-singular-be-encountered. Pure intuiting is, as finite, a receptive representing; but since what is now to be taken in is the being of beings and not beings, it cannot be a present being that gives itself. Pure receptive representing must give itself something representable. Pure intuition must therefore in a certain way be "creative." The paradox announced in §7 recurs in its first concrete form, and the quotation marks around "creative" carry the whole burden: pure intuition produces nothing that is, yet does not merely find what it intuits.

What is represented in pure intuition is no being — no object, no appearing being — and yet not simply nothing. All the more insistently must it be exhibited what is represented in pure intuition and in its way alone, and how, in accordance with the represented, the manner of representing is to be delimited.

Kant exhibits space and time as pure intuitions. First it must be shown, with respect to space, how it announces itself within finite knowledge of beings, and where accordingly its essence alone can be presented.

Kant lays out the unveiling of the essence of space and of time so that each negative characterization of the phenomenon is followed by the positive one prefigured in it.

That the essential characterization begins negatively is no accident. It sets in with the defensive statement that space and time are not this and not that, because what is to be positively grasped is in advance and by its essence already familiar, though not yet cognized — indeed in a certain way *mis*-cognized. The negative route is thus not a shortage of positive content but the appropriate form for something too near to be noticed, and the same move governs Heidegger's treatments of world and of time elsewhere.

Space — the relations of beside-, above- and behind-one-another — is not come upon somewhere "here" or "there." Space is not a thing present among other beings, no "empirical representation," that is, nothing represented in such a representing. For anything present to be able to show itself as extended in determinate spatial relations, space must already be manifest before all receptive grasping of what is present. It must be represented as that "wherein" what is present can first be encountered: space is something necessarily and in advance, that is, purely, represented in finite human knowing. A marginal note guards the point from the obvious empiricist reply — space is not the abstractable, not an abstraction from many different things — and another registers dissatisfaction with Kant's own second argument while affirming the conclusion: space is not a determination dependent on appearing, but the reverse.

But since this represented "holds for every" individual spatial relation, it looks like a representation that "holds for many," that is, a concept. Once again the essential analysis of what is represented as space gives information about the corresponding representing. Space, Kant says again negatively, is no "discursive" representation. The unity of the one space is not gathered together with respect to several individual spatial relations, nor built up out of a comparative consideration of them. The unity of space is not that of a concept but the unity of something that is at itself a single one. The many spaces are only limitations of the one single space. And this space is not merely what can be limited on each occasion — the limiting boundaries themselves are of its essence, that is, spatial. The one single space is, in each of its parts, wholly itself. A concept is not contained in its instances; space is contained in every one of its. That is the whole of the argument, and it decides that space is intuited, not thought.

Representing space is therefore the immediate representing of a single individual, that is, intuition, if the essence of intuition must be determined as *repraesentatio singularis*. And space is what is intuited in a pure intuition.

But pure intuition as intuition must give the intuited not only immediately but immediately *whole*. This pure intuiting is no mere taking in of a piece; even in the limitations it intuits the whole at once. "Space is represented as given as an infinite magnitude" (A 25, B 39). That space is a magnitude does not mean it is a such-and-such large thing, and "infinite magnitude" accordingly does not mean an endlessly large thing: "magnitude" here means magnitude-hood, which first makes a such-and-such large — a quantity — possible. As Kant's *Nachlaß* puts it, the quantum in which alone all quantity can be determined is, in respect of the multitude of its parts, indeterminate and a continuum: space and time.

That this magnitude-hood is "infinite" then means: space differs from the determinate individual parts not in degree or in richness of composition, but infinitely, that is, essentially. It lies before all parts as the limitable single whole. Unlike the universality of a concept, it does not have the manifold individuals "under" itself but, as always already co-intuited, "in" itself — in such a way that this pure intuition of the whole can at any time yield the "parts." Representing such an "infinite" magnitude-hood as given is therefore a *giving* intuiting; and insofar as the single whole is given at once, this representing lets its representable spring forth and is in this sense an "original" representing. A marginal note names what is at stake: this is the *transcendental* concept of the infinite. Infinity here means priority of the whole to its parts, not endless extent — and this transcendental infinity within finite knowing is what makes the paradox of a "creative" finite intuition tractable.

Pure intuition thus does have its intuited, and has it in such a way that it gives it only in and through the intuiting itself. The intuited is admittedly neither a present being nor thematically grasped in the pure intuiting itself. In handling things and perceiving them their spatial relations are indeed intuited, but mostly not meant as such. What is intuited in pure intuition stands non-objectively and moreover unthematically in a fore-look; and what is looked toward is the single whole that makes possible the ordering in beside-, under- and behind-one-another. What is intuited in this "manner of intuiting" is not simply nothing. The mode of givenness is the point: the a priori is not before us as an object but ahead of us as what we look through, which is why it is missed by any inspection that expects to find it.

Even from this it can be gathered that a further illumination of what is "originally represented" in pure intuition becomes possible only if it can be brought to light more penetratingly in what sense pure intuition is "original," that is, how it lets its intuited spring forth. The question is deferred to §28, where the answer will be that pure intuition is an accomplishment of the transcendental imagination; the first stage therefore leaves its own central term unexplained on purpose.

###### **§ 10. Die Zeit als die universale reine Anschauung**

Pure intuition is sought as the one essential element of ontological knowledge in which the experience of beings is grounded. But space, as pure intuition, gives in advance only the whole of those relations in which the encounters of outer sense are ordered. At the same time we find givens of "inner sense" that show no spatial shape and no spatial relations but announce themselves as a succession of states of our mind — representations, strivings, moods. What we look toward in advance in experiencing these appearances, non-objectively and unthematically, is the pure one-after-another. Hence time is "the form of inner sense, that is, of the intuiting of ourselves and of our inner state" (A 33, B 49); time determines "the relation of representations in our inner state"; and "time can be no determination of outer appearances; it belongs neither to a shape nor a position" (A 33, B 49 f.).

So the two pure intuitions are distributed over two regions of experience, and it seems at first impossible to find a pure intuition that constitutes every knowledge of the being of experienceable beings and so permits the problem of ontological knowledge to be posed universally. Yet immediately beside this assignment of the two pure intuitions to two regions of appearance stands Kant's thesis: "time is the formal condition a priori of all appearances in general" (A 34, B 50). Time therefore has a primacy over space, and as the universal pure intuition it must become the leading and bearing essential element of the pure, transcendence-forming knowledge.

The interpretation that follows shows how time moves more and more into the centre through the individual stages of the laying of the ground, and only thereby unveils its own essence more originally than the preliminary characterization in the Transcendental Aesthetic can manage. Everything in the book is thus staked on a promise made here: what time is cannot be read off the Aesthetic, because the Aesthetic treats time in isolation and time's essence shows only in its function.

How does Kant ground this primacy of time? It may at first strike one that Kant denies time-determination to outer appearances, when everyday experience finds time precisely in them — in the course of the stars and in natural happening, growth and dying — and so immediately that time is equated with the "heavens."

But Kant does not deny time-determinacy to outer appearances outright, if time is to be the formal condition a priori of all appearances. One thesis takes within-time-ness away from what is physically present, the other gives it. How are the opposed statements to be reconciled? When Kant restricts time as pure intuition to the givens of inner sense — representations in the widest sense — this restriction contains precisely an extension of the possible domain within which time can function as a prior manner of intuiting. For among representations are found those that as representations also let beings be encountered which the representing being itself is not.

Kant's consideration therefore takes this route. Because all representations, as states of representing, fall immediately into time, what is represented in representing also belongs as such in time. By the detour over the immediate within-time-ness of representing, a mediated within-time-ness of the represented results — of those "representations" determined by outer sense. Because outer appearances are accordingly only mediately within time, time-determination belongs to them in a certain way and in a certain way not. The inference from the within-time-ness of outer intuiting as a psychical happening to the within-time-ness of what is intuited in it is made considerably easier for Kant by the ambiguity of "intuition" and "representation," which mean at once states of the mind and what those states have as their object. The argument is thus reported and its weakness named in the same breath: Kant's proof of time's universality runs through psychology and an equivocation.

Whether this grounding of the universality of time, and thus of its central ontological function, holds good and can be the decisive one, and whether space as pure intuition is thereby displaced from a possible central ontological function, must here remain open. The question returns in §35, and it is not answered in Kant's favour there either; the primacy of time is something Heidegger will have to ground otherwise than Kant grounded it.

If it is possible at all, the grounding of time's universality is possible only in this way: although space and time as pure intuitions both belong "to the subject," time dwells in the subject more originally than space. But time, immediately restricted to the givens of inner sense, is at the same time ontologically more universal only if the subjectivity of the subject consists in openness for beings. The more subjective time, the more original and the wider the unbounding of the subject. The sentence looks like a paradox and is the thesis of the book. Subjectivity is not an enclosure, so what is most inward is not thereby most confined; if the subject's being consists in being open to beings, then what belongs most intimately to it is precisely what opens it widest. Every reading that hears Kantian "subjectivity" as interiority will find this unintelligible, which is why Heidegger states it before he can prove it.

The universal ontological function Kant assigns to time at the beginning of the laying of the ground can therefore justify itself sufficiently only if time itself, precisely in its ontological function as an essential component of pure ontological knowledge, forces us to determine the essence of subjectivity more originally. That is the work of §34.

The "transcendental aesthetic" has the task of exhibiting the ontological *aisthesis* [perceiving, sensing] that makes it possible to "discover a priori" the being of beings. Insofar as intuition retains the lead in all knowledge, one of the "requisite pieces for the solution of the general task of transcendental philosophy" — that is, of ontology — has now been won.

Yet as little as one may evaporate pure intuition as an essential element of ontological knowledge, so little can the isolating interpretation of one element make that element visible in its elementary function. Not the elimination of the transcendental aesthetic as a preliminary stock of problems, but the preservation and sharpening of its problematic must become the ownmost goal of the laying of the ground Kant carries out, if that grounding is sure of its own task. The demand is aimed at the Marburg reading, which treats the Aesthetic as a stage to be absorbed into the Logic; Heidegger's counter-move is not to defend the Aesthetic as it stands but to claim that its problem outgrows it.

First, though, the second essential element of a pure finite knowledge must be exhibited in the same isolating consideration: pure thinking.

##### **b) Das reine Denken im endlichen Erkennen**

###### **§ 11. Der reine Verstandesbegriff (Notion)**

The other element in the finitude of human knowledge is thinking, which as determining representing aims at what is intuited in intuition and so serves intuition alone. The object of an intuition, always an individual, determines itself as "this and that" in a "general representation," the concept. The finitude of thinking intuition is therefore a knowing through concepts, and pure knowing is pure intuition through pure concepts. These must be exhibited if the full essential stock of a pure knowledge is to be secured; and to find them, what is sought under the title must first be clarified.

In representing a linden, a beech, a fir as a tree, what is intuited on each occasion is determined as this and that from a look toward something that "holds for many." This holding-for-many characterizes a representation as a concept but does not yet hit its original essence, for it is grounded in turn, as a derived character, in the fact that in the concept the One is represented in which several objects agree. Conceptual representing is a letting-several-agree in this One. The unity of this One must therefore be seen out in advance in conceptual representing and held before all determining assertions about the several. This prior seeing-out of the One in which several can agree is the fundamental act of concept-formation, and Kant calls it "reflection": "the consideration of how different representations can be comprehended in one consciousness." Such considering brings a unity embracing several before itself, so that in relation to this unity the several are compared (comparison), while what does not accord with the One held forth is looked away from (abstraction, in Kant's sense). What is represented in conceptual representing is "a representation insofar as it can be contained in different ones." In the concept it is not simply something that in fact belongs to several that is represented, but this belonging insofar as it belongs, that is, in its unity. As this embracing One, what is so represented *is* the concept; hence Kant rightly says that "it is a mere tautology to speak of general or common concepts." Generality is thereby demoted from the definition of the concept to a consequence of it, and the demotion is what makes room for concepts that are a priori in their content.

Because a representation forms itself into a concept in the fundamental act of the prior seeing-out of the many-valid One — in reflection — concepts are also called reflected representations, springing from reflection. The concept-character of a representation, that what it represents has the form of the many-valid One, always springs from reflection. But what, according to its what-content, the determining One is, mostly grows out of empirically comparing and abstracting intuiting. The origin of the what-content of such empirical concepts is therefore no problem.

Under the title "pure concept," by contrast, a "reflected" representation is sought whose what-content essentially cannot be read off appearances; its content too must be obtainable a priori. Concepts that are given a priori according to their content as well Kant calls *notions*, *conceptus dati a priori*.

Are there such concepts? Do such lie prepared in the human understanding? How is the understanding to be able to give a what-content, when it is only an empty function of combining, dependent precisely on a giving intuition? And can such a what, represented as given, be found in the understanding at all when, as is now to happen, it is isolated from all intuition? If the understanding for itself is to be the origin not only of the form of every concept as such but also of the content of determinate concepts, this origin can lie only in the fundamental act of concept-formation, in reflection. The objection is stated at full strength before being answered, and the answer will be that the understanding gives content by giving the unities it works with — not by producing anything intuitable.

Every determining of something as something — every judging — contains the "unity of the act of ordering different representations under a common one" (A 68, B 93). But this act of reflecting uniting is possible only in being itself already led by a prior look toward a unity in whose light a uniting is possible at all. Reflecting is therefore already, quite apart from whatever concepts grow out of its act, the prior representing of a unity that guides the unification, and represents it as such a unity. If the representing of unity lies in reflecting itself, then representing of unity belongs to the essential structure of the fundamental act of the understanding.

The essence of the understanding is original comprehending. In the structure of the understanding's act as representing unification, the representations of the unity that guides on each occasion lie prepared. These represented unities are the content of the pure concepts: the what-content of these concepts is the unity that makes a particular uniting possible. The representing of these unities is in itself, on the ground of its specific content, already a priori conceptual. The pure concept does not still need to have a concept-form supplied to it; it *is* that form, in an original sense. A pure concept is thus not a concept whose content happens to be a priori but the represented unity of an act — which is why the categories will turn out not to be a list of predicates at all.

Pure concepts accordingly do not first arise through an act of reflection; they are not reflected concepts but the representations that belong in advance to the essential structure of reflection and act in, with and for reflection — *reflecting* concepts. Kant's own reflection confirms it: all concepts are reflected representations, but there are concepts whose whole sense is nothing other than one or another reflection to which occurring representations can be subjected; these may be called concepts of reflection (*conceptus reflectentes*), and since every kind of reflection occurs in judgment, they contain absolutely in themselves the mere act of the understanding applied in judgment to relation, as grounds of the possibility of judging.

There are therefore pure concepts in the understanding as such, and the "dissection of the faculty of understanding itself" must bring to light these representations that co-constitute the essential structure of reflection.

###### **§ 12. Die Notionen als ontologische Prädikate (Kategorien)**

The pure understanding yields in itself a manifold, the pure unities of possible uniting. And if the possible modes of uniting — judgments — make up a closed nexus, the closed nature of the understanding itself, then a systematic whole of the manifold of pure concepts lies concealed in the pure understanding. This whole is then the system of those predicates that function in pure knowledge, that is, assert about the being of beings. The pure concepts have the character of ontological predicates, called from of old "categories." The table of judgments is therefore the origin of the categories and of their table. Heidegger's own marginal note against this last inference — "why?" — marks the sentence as a report of Kant's reasoning rather than an endorsement, and the rest of the § withdraws it.

This origin of the categories has been doubted many times over. The main misgiving takes offence at the questionableness of the source itself, the table of judgments and its sufficient grounding. Kant in fact does not develop the manifold of functions in judgment out of the essence of the understanding; he presents a finished table articulated by the four "principal moments" of quantity, quality, relation and modality. Whether and how far precisely these four moments are grounded in the essence of the understanding is likewise not shown, and whether they can be grounded purely formal-logically at all may be doubted.

Then it becomes uncertain what character this table of judgments has at all. Kant himself wavers, calling it now a "transcendental table" (A 73, B 98), now a "logical table of judgments" (*Prolegomena* §21). Does not the reproach Kant makes to Aristotle's table of categories — that it was gathered up rhapsodically — fall back on his own table of judgments?

But it is not to be decided here whether and how far the many objections to Kant's table are justified, or whether they even touch the fundamental defect. What must be seen is that such a critique of the table of judgments, taken as a critique of the source of the categories, has already missed the decisive problem from the ground up. For the categories are not only in fact not derived from the table of judgments; they cannot be derived from it at all. And this because at the present stage of the discussion, where the elements of pure knowledge are treated in isolation, the essence and the idea of the category cannot yet be determined, indeed cannot even be made a problem. The standard objection to the metaphysical deduction is thus disarmed by making it beside the point: Kant's table is faulty, but the fault is not where the critics locate it, and repairing the table would not supply what a category is.

If the question of the origin of the categories cannot in principle yet arise, then the table of judgments must have, for preparing the question of the possibility of ontological knowledge, a function other than the one just stated.

It seems an easy thing to satisfy the task the first stage sets — what is more tangible than the elements of pure knowledge, pure intuition and pure concept, set side by side? But precisely in this isolation it must not be lost from view from the outset that finite pure knowledge is what has been made a problem. Which means, on the earlier findings: the second element, pure thinking, has an essentially serving position with respect to intuition. Dependence on pure intuition therefore belongs to pure thinking essentially, not incidentally and after the fact. When the pure concept is first grasped as a notion, the second element of pure knowledge has not been won in its elementary character at all but on the contrary trimmed of the decisive essential moment, its inner relation to intuition. The pure concept as notion is therefore only a fragment of the second element.

So long as the pure understanding does not stand in view with respect to its essence — its pure relatedness to intuition — no origin of the notions as ontological predicates can be unveiled. The table of judgments is therefore not the "origin of the categories" but merely the "guiding thread of the discovery of all concepts of the understanding." In it the directive to the closed whole of pure concepts is supposed to lie; but it cannot give the unveiling of the full essence of the pure concepts as categories. Whether the table, in the way Kant introduces and presents it, can take on even this restricted function of prefiguring a systematic unity of the pure concepts of the understanding, is left open here.

What now becomes clear is this: the more radically one tries to isolate the pure elements of a finite knowledge, the more insistently the impossibility of such isolation shows itself, and the more obtrusively the dependence of pure thinking on intuition. With that the artificiality of the first approach comes to light. Pure concepts can be determined as ontological predicates only when they are understood out of the essential unity of finite pure knowledge. The first stage thus succeeds by failing, and the failure is the argument: an isolation carried through consistently demonstrates that what was isolated cannot be.

#### **Das zweite Stadium der Grundlegung: Die Wesenseinheit der reinen Erkenntnis**

The isolated pure elements of pure knowledge are time as universal pure intuition and the notions as what is thought in pure thinking. But if the isolating consideration does not even manage to grasp the elements as such completely, then all the less will their unity be won by a linking of the isolated pieces added afterwards. With that the problem of the essential unity of pure knowledge sharpens, if matters are not to rest with the negative characterization that this unity cannot be a band stretched between the elements after the fact.

The finitude of knowledge announces precisely a peculiar inner dependence of thinking on intuition, and conversely a need of intuition to be determined by thinking. The pull of the elements toward one another indicates that their unity cannot be "later" than they themselves but must be laid down "earlier," in them and for them. This unity, as original, unites the elements in such a way that only in the uniting do the elements as such spring forth, and are held by it in their unity. How far does Kant succeed, starting out from the isolated elements, in nevertheless making this original unity visible?

Kant gives the first characterization of the original essential unity of the pure elements, the one that prepares all further clarification, in the third section of the first main division of the "Analytic of Concepts," in the piece headed "On the pure concepts of the understanding or categories" (A 76–80, B 102–105; designated §10 in the second edition). Understanding this paragraph is the key to understanding the *Critique of Pure Reason* as a laying of the ground of metaphysics. Four pages of the *Critique* are thereby declared its centre — not the Aesthetic, not the deduction, not the schematism chapter, but the short section that introduces the pure synthesis of the imagination between intuition and concept.

Because the notions, belonging as they do to the finitude of knowledge, are essentially related to pure intuition, and because this relatedness co-constitutes the essential unity of pure knowledge, the essential delimitation of the category is at the same time the illumination of the inner possibility of the essential unity of ontological knowledge. Kant's answer must now be presented through the interpretation of that piece — but the question itself first needs clarifying.

##### **§ 13. Die Frage nach der Wesenseinheit der reinen Erkenntnis**

If the elements of finite pure knowledge are essentially dependent on one another, that already prevents their unity from being stuck onto them as a subsequent togetherness. That and how the unity underlies the elements is precisely what the preceding isolation covered over and made unrecognizable. And even if an analysis holds to the tendency toward unveiling the original unity, this does not yet guarantee a full grasp of it. On the contrary: given the sharpness with which the isolation was carried out, and the still more clearly emerging peculiarity of the second element, it is to be expected that this isolation can no longer be entirely undone, so that in the end the unity is after all not explicitly developed out of its ownmost origin. Kant's failure is thus predicted before the deduction is read, and predicted from a methodological cause rather than a personal one: an isolation that has been performed too well cannot be reversed, and the analytic order in which one begins decides what one can still reach.

That the unity is not to be the result of the elements' having come together but is itself the originally unifying announces itself in its being named "synthesis."

But in the full structure of finite knowledge manifold syntheses are necessarily played into one another: to the veritative belongs the predicative, into which in turn the apophantic is built. Which of these is meant when the essential unity of pure knowledge is asked after? Obviously the veritative, since it concerns the unity of intuition and thinking. But the others are necessarily included in it.

The essential unity of pure knowledge is nevertheless to form the unity of the togetherness of all the structural syntheses as a whole. The veritative synthesis therefore receives a primacy in this question only insofar as the synthesis-problem concentrates itself in it; which does not exclude that the question is just as necessarily oriented by the remaining forms of synthesis. Moreover, in the question of the essential unity of ontological knowledge it is the *pure* veritative synthesis that is at issue: the original unification of pure universal intuition (time) and pure thinking (the notions). But pure intuition is in itself already, as the representing of a single whole, something like an intuiting uniting, and Kant therefore rightly speaks of a "synopsis" in intuition (A 94). At the same time the analysis of the notion as a "reflecting concept" showed that pure thinking, as the representing of the pure unities, is in itself originally unity-giving and in this sense "synthetic."

The problem of the pure veritative or ontological synthesis must accordingly be brought to the question: what does the original (veritative) "synthesis" of the pure synopsis and the pure reflecting (predicative) synthesis look like? Already from the form of this question it can be estimated that the sought synthesis must have a quite distinguished character, if it is to unify things that already show synthesis-structure in themselves. It must be equal in advance to the forms of "synthesis" and "synopsis" that it is to unite; it must itself originally form them in uniting them. Three of the four occurrences of "syn-" are already in the members to be joined, so the joining term cannot be a fourth of the same kind — it must be what lets the others be syntheses at all. This is the formal shape of the argument for the transcendental imagination, given before the imagination is named.

##### **§ 14. Die ontologische Synthesis**

The question of the essential unity of pure intuition and pure thinking comes from the prior isolation of these elements. The character of the unity belonging to them can therefore first be prefigured by showing how each of these elements structurally demands the other. They show joints that point ahead to something jointed together. The veritative synthesis is then what not only fits into these joints, joining the elements together, but first *joints* the joints themselves. The image is exact and load-bearing: a joint is not a third thing between two pieces but the shaping of each piece so that it meets the other. If the synthesis makes the joints, then intuition and thinking have the shape they have because of it.

Kant introduces the general characterization with a pointer: transcendental logic has a manifold of sensibility a priori lying before it, which the transcendental aesthetic offers it in order to give the pure concepts of the understanding a matter, without which they would be wholly empty; space and time contain a manifold of pure intuition a priori but belong nonetheless to the conditions of the receptivity of our mind under which alone it can receive representations of objects, and which must therefore always affect the concept of them as well; but the spontaneity of our thinking requires that this manifold first be gone through, taken up and combined in a certain way in order to make a knowledge out of it, and "this act I call synthesis" (A 76 f., B 102).

The mutual dependence of pure intuition and pure thinking is here introduced in a remarkably external form. Strictly, it is not "transcendental logic" that has the pure manifold of time "lying before it" — this lying-before belongs to the essential structure of the pure thinking that transcendental logic analyses. Correspondingly it is not the transcendental aesthetic that "offers" the pure manifold; pure intuition is from the outset offering, and offering in the direction of pure thinking. Kant's sentence speaks of disciplines where it should speak of structures, and the substitution is the same one that will let the architectonic disguise the matter throughout.

This pure offering is introduced still more sharply as an "affecting," where affection through the senses is not to be thought. Insofar as this affection belongs "at all times" to pure knowledge, it says that our pure thinking is at all times placed before the time that concerns it. How that is possible remains at first dark. Everything decisive in the book is contained in this dark sentence: a pure affection by time, exercised on the knower by nothing external, is what §34 will call pure self-affection.

Given this essential dependence of our pure thinking on the pure manifold, the finitude of our thinking "requires" that the manifold fit itself to thinking as conceptually determining. But for pure intuition to be determinable through pure concepts, its manifold must be taken out of dispersion — gone through and gathered. This mutual preparing-of-each-for-the-other happens in the act Kant generally calls synthesis. In it the two pure elements meet, each from its own side; it closes the joints that point toward one another and so makes up the essential unity of a pure knowledge.

This synthesis is a matter neither of intuition nor of thinking. Mediating as it were "between" the two, it has kinship with both, and must therefore share with the elements their basic character, that is, be a representing. Kant: "synthesis in general is, as we shall see hereafter, the mere effect of the imagination, a blind though indispensable function of the soul, without which we would have no knowledge at all, but of which we are seldom even once conscious" (A 78, B 103). The word around which the rest of the book turns enters the *Critique* in a subordinate clause, in a promissory "as we shall see hereafter," and with the epithet "blind." Heidegger italicizes the sentence and lets its placement make his case: what Kant needs most is what he introduces most casually.

This first indicates that everything showing itself as synthesis-structure in the essential build of knowledge is evidently effected by the imagination. But what is at issue here in particular, and first, is the essential unity of pure knowledge, the "pure synthesis," so called "when the manifold is given a priori." The pure synthesis fits itself into what unites as synopsis in pure intuition.

At the same time it needs a look toward a guiding unity. It therefore belongs to pure synthesis that as representing uniting it represents in advance the unity belonging to it as such, that is, generally. But to represent this unity of its own generally means that pure synthesis brings itself, with respect to the unity represented in it, to the concept that gives it unity. Pure synthesis thus acts purely synoptically in pure intuition and at the same time purely reflectingly in pure thinking. From this it follows that three pieces belong to the unity of the full essence of pure knowledge.

Kant: "the first thing that must be given to us a priori for the knowledge of all objects is the manifold of pure intuition; the synthesis of this manifold through the imagination is the second, but it still gives no knowledge; the concepts that give unity to this pure synthesis, and consist solely in the representation of this necessary synthetic unity, do the third for the knowledge of an object that comes before us, and rest on the understanding" (A 78 f., B 104).

In this triad the pure synthesis of the imagination holds the middle. This does not have the external sense that the imagination is merely named between the first and the third in an enumeration; the middle is *structural*. In it the pure synopsis and the pure reflecting synthesis meet and join together. This joining-into-one expresses itself for Kant in his establishing the sameness of the pure synthesis in the syn-character of intuition and of the understanding.

"The same function which gives unity to the various representations in a judgment also gives unity to the mere synthesis of various representations in an intuition, which, expressed generally, is called the pure concept of the understanding" (A 79, B 104 f.). By this sameness of the synthetic function Kant means not the empty identity of a formal combining at work everywhere, but the originally rich wholeness of a many-membered uniting and unity-giving that works as intuiting and thinking at once. Which says at the same time: the earlier named modes of synthesis — the formal apophantic of the judgment-function and the predicative of conceptual reflection — belong together into the unity of the essential structure of finite knowledge as the veritative synthesis of intuition and thinking. Sameness here means essential, structural belonging-together. Reading "the same function" as one bare operation performed twice is what produces the intellectualist Kant; reading it as one articulated whole working in both directions is what makes the imagination the middle rather than an auxiliary.

"The same understanding, and indeed through the very same acts by which it brought about the logical form of a judgment in concepts by means of analytical unity, also brings a transcendental content into its representations by means of the synthetic unity of the manifold in intuition in general" (A 79, B 105). What now becomes visible as the essential unity of pure knowledge is far from the empty simplicity of an ultimate principle. It announces itself as a many-shaped act that remains dark both in its act-character and in the many-memberedness of its uniting. This characterization cannot be the conclusion but must be the right beginning of the laying of the ground of ontological knowledge, whose task is now set: to bring the pure synthesis as such to light. But because pure synthesis is an *act*, its essence can become manifest only by being pursued in its springing forth. Only now does it show, from what forces itself forward as the theme, why a laying of the ground of ontological knowledge must become an unveiling of the origin of the pure synthesis — of that synthesis in its letting-spring-forth as such.

When the laying of the ground now enters the stage where "the matter itself is deeply veiled" (A 88, B 121) and complaints about the darkness are therefore out of order, a short halt for methodological reflection on the present position and the further way is all the more necessary.

##### **§ 15. Das Kategorienproblem und die Rolle der transzendentalen Logik**

The problem of the essential unity of ontological knowledge first gives the ground for determining the essence of the category. If the category is to be not only, and not primarily, a mode of "asserting" — *schēma tou logou*, a figure of speech — as the name suggests, but is to satisfy its ownmost essence as *schēma tou ontos*, a figure of being, then it may not function as an "element" (notion) of pure knowledge; rather the known being of beings must lie in it. But knowing being is the unity of pure intuition and pure thinking, so what becomes decisive for the essence of the category is precisely the pure intuitiveness of the notions. A category is thus not a concept that happens to apply to everything but the being of beings as known; and its intuitive side is not a supplement to a logical core but constitutive of it.

The "metaphysical exposition" of pure intuition was the task of the transcendental aesthetic; the illumination of the other element, pure thinking, fell to transcendental "logic," specifically the Analytic of Concepts. The problem of the essential unity has led the investigation beyond the isolation of the elements, and the pure synthesis falls neither to pure intuition nor to pure thinking. The illumination of the origin of pure synthesis that now begins can therefore be neither a transcendental-aesthetic nor a transcendental-logical one; and correspondingly the category is a problem neither of transcendental aesthetic nor of transcendental logic.

Into which transcendental discipline, then, does the discussion of the central problem of the possibility of ontology fall? The question remains foreign to Kant. He assigns to the "Analytic of Concepts" not only the clarification of the pure concept as an element of pure knowledge but also the determination and grounding of the essential unity of pure knowledge. Logic thereby receives an incomparable primacy over aesthetic, where on the other side intuition presents the primary in the whole of knowledge. The homelessness of the central problem is the structural fault Heidegger is naming: the *Critique* has no place for what it most needs to say, and the imagination will accordingly appear in it as an intruder.

This oddity needs clarifying if the problematic of the following stages is to remain transparent — and the more urgently in that interpretations of the *Critique* keep succumbing to the tendency to take it as a "logic of pure knowledge," even where a relative right is granted to intuition and so to the transcendental aesthetic.

In the end the primacy of transcendental logic within the whole laying of the ground of *metaphysica generalis* holds good in a certain way. But precisely for that reason the interpretation must make itself free of Kant's architectonic and make the idea of transcendental logic problematic. A marginal note supplies the reason the primacy is legitimate: since antiquity the whole approach to the question of being has run by way of the *logos* — hence "categories" — so that the question of being becomes onto-*logy*, where "-logy" names not merely the character of a discipline but a doubling, an onto-logo-logy. The primacy of logic is thus not Kant's idiosyncrasy but the inherited form of the question of being, which is why exposing it is a step in the destruction of the history of ontology rather than a complaint about a book's layout.

First it must be understood how far Kant rightly treats not only the second element of pure knowledge but also the problem of the unity of both elements in the "Analytic of Concepts." If the essence of thinking consists in its serving relatedness to intuition, then a rightly understood analytic of pure thinking must draw just this relatedness into the range of its problematic. That this happens in Kant becomes evidence that the finitude of thinking stands in the theme. Given this sense, the predominance of transcendental logic entails anything but a diminution of the function of the transcendental aesthetic, let alone its elimination. But with insight into the ground of the primacy of transcendental logic, that primacy annuls itself — not in favour of the transcendental aesthetic, but in favour of a formulation of the question that takes the central problem of the essential unity of ontological knowledge and its grounding back to a more original basis. The primacy is justified and thereby dissolved in one move: logic rules because thinking is finite, and once finitude is the theme, neither logic nor aesthetic is the discipline in which the ground can be laid.

Because Kant assigns to the Analytic of Concepts the discussion of the conditions and principles of the pure concepts' "use" as well, the intuition-relatedness of pure thinking necessarily enters the theme under the title of use. Yet the question of the essential unity of pure knowledge is always posed starting from the thinking element. The tendency to do so receives constant support from the fact that the category, which at bottom contains the problem of essential unity, always presents itself at the same time as a notion, under the title of the pure concept of the understanding. The very word "concept of the understanding" keeps pulling the category back to the side of thinking, so the terminology enforces the imbalance the analysis is trying to correct.

Above all, with this primary orientation to the thinking element Kant must draw on the general knowledge about thinking supplied by traditional formal logic. Thereby what, turned into the transcendental, leads to the problem of the pure concept as category takes on the character of a logical — though transcendental-logical — discussion.

Finally, the orientation to *logos* and *ratio* has, in accordance with its significance in Western metaphysics, a primacy from the outset in the laying of that metaphysics' ground, and this comes to expression in the determination of the grounding as a *critique of pure reason*. The title of the book already contains the prejudice the book's own results overturn.

Added to all this: for the architectonic mastery and presentation of the "very mixed web of human knowledge" (A 85, B 117) that his analytic first made manifest, Kant needed a certain scholastic framework, which a logic of pure knowledge still to be created could most readily take over from formal logic.

However self-evident this manifold rule of "logic" in the *Critique of Pure Reason* may be, the following interpretation of the further and decisive stages must penetrate through the architectonic of the outer sequence and stamping of the problems and lay before our eyes the inner drift of the problematic that first brought Kant to such a presentation.

#### **Das dritte Stadium der Grundlegung: Die innere Möglichkeit der Wesenseinheit der ontologischen Synthesis**

The apparently firm answer to the question of the essential unity of ontological knowledge dissolves, as that unity is determined more closely, into the problem of the possibility of such a unification. In the pure synthesis, pure intuition and pure thinking are supposed to be able to meet a priori.

What and how must the pure synthesis itself be, to satisfy the task of such a unification? The pure synthesis must now be brought forward in the respect that it shows how it can unite time and notion. Presenting the original self-forming of the essential unity of ontological knowledge is the sense and task of what Kant calls the "transcendental deduction of the categories."

If the basic intention of the "deduction" lies in the analytical disclosure of the basic structure of the pure synthesis, then its genuine content cannot come to expression in a presentation of it as a *quaestio juris*. The legal question may therefore not be taken from the outset as the guiding thread of the interpretation of this central Kantian doctrine; motive and range of the juristic formula must on the contrary be explained out of the deduction's own problem-tendency. That inversion is defended in §18, and the risk it runs is worth naming: the deduction's stated purpose — to establish a right — is set aside in favour of a purpose Heidegger reads off its execution.

For reasons given later, in §31, the present interpretation holds exclusively to the version of the transcendental deduction in the first edition. Kant repeatedly stresses the "difficulty" of the deduction and seeks to remedy its "darkness." The manifoldness and entanglement of the relations, revealing itself ever more strongly in the content of the problem, prevents him from the start from contenting himself with a single approach or resting in a single way of carrying it out. Even the multiple executions still show Kant at wrestling work: often it becomes clearly seen and said only en route what goal the deduction is striving toward, and what should first present itself through the analytical unveiling is sent ahead in a merely "preliminary reminder."

The inner manifoldness of the problem also causes those relations whose clarification makes particular difficulty to receive an overemphatic treatment, tempting the reader to overstate their substantive weight accordingly. That holds especially of the discussion of pure thinking within the whole of the essential unity of pure knowledge. The methodological point matters for the verdict on Kant: where he labours longest is not where the matter is heaviest but where his own resources were thinnest, and the imagination's brevity of treatment is therefore not evidence against its centrality.

The interpretation that follows should not walk the entangled paths of the deduction in detail but lay free the original drift of the problematic. The first requirement for that is to make the deduction's proper goal sufficiently clear with a view to the guiding problem of the laying of the ground of metaphysics.

##### **§ 16. Die Aufhellung der Transzendenz der endlichen Vernunft als Grundabsicht der transzendentalen Deduktion**

A finitely knowing being can comport itself toward beings that it is not itself and has not created only if these already present beings can encounter it from themselves. But to be able to encounter as the being it is, a being must in advance already be "known" as a being at all, that is, with respect to its constitution of being. Which means: ontological — here always *pre*ontological — knowledge is the condition of the possibility that anything like beings themselves can stand over against a finite being at all. A finite being needs this basic capacity of a turning-toward that lets something stand over against it. In this original turning-toward, the finite being first holds open for itself a play-space within which something can "correspond" to it. To hold oneself in advance within such a play-space, to form it originally, is nothing other than the transcendence that marks all finite comportment toward beings. And if the possibility of ontological knowledge is grounded in the pure synthesis, while ontological knowledge is precisely this letting-stand-over-against, then the pure synthesis must reveal itself as what joints and bears the single whole of the inner essential structure of transcendence. Through illumination of this jointure the innermost essence of the finitude of reason unveils itself. *Gegenstehenlassen* [letting-stand-over-against] is now the operative term for the whole enterprise; it names an act of the finite knower that produces no being but produces the standing-over-against in which beings can be met. Michel Henry's *objectivation* renders exactly this word, and his critique of Heidegger's "ontological monism" is aimed at what is said here.

Finite knowledge is receptive intuition, and as such it needs determining thinking. Pure thinking therefore claims a central significance in the problem of the unity of ontological knowledge — not despite but because of the primacy intuition has in all knowledge.

To what essential service is pure thinking called in its serving position? What does it serve within the enabling of the essential structure of transcendence? Precisely this apparently once again isolated question about the essence of pure thinking must lead into the innermost core of the problem of essential unity.

It is not by chance that Kant, in the "transition to the transcendental deduction of the categories" (A 92 f., B 124 f.), gives a pointer to the clearly seen finitude of our representing, and specifically of the purely knowing representing — "for of its causality by means of the will there is no question here." The question is rather what representing can by itself effect with respect to the beings toward which it comports itself. Kant says that the "representation in itself does not produce its object as regards existence." Our knowing is not ontically creative; it cannot set beings before itself out of itself. And in the middle of the deduction Kant stresses that "besides our knowledge we have nothing that we could set over against this knowledge as corresponding to it" (A 104). The exclusion of the will is not incidental: intellectual intuition and creation by will are the two ways a knower could produce its object, and both are shut off, leaving only the production of the horizon.

If our knowing as finite must be a receptive intuiting, it does not suffice to concede this. The problem first awakens here: what belongs necessarily to the possibility of this by no means self-evident taking-in of beings?

Evidently this: that beings can encounter from themselves, can show themselves as standing over against. But if we are not master of the presence of beings, then dependence on taking them in demands precisely that beings be given, in advance and at all times, the possibility of standing over against.

A receptive intuiting can be carried out only within a capacity of letting-stand-over-against, in a turning-toward that first forms a pure correspondence. And what is it that we there let stand over against, from ourselves? It cannot be beings. But if not beings, then a nothing. Only if the letting-stand-over-against is a holding-oneself-into the nothing can representing let something that is not nothing — something like beings — be encountered instead of the nothing and within it, provided such shows itself empirically. This nothing is not the *nihil absolutum*. Nothing here is what stands over against as long as no being does — the sheer standing-open of the horizon, which is not a being and hence, for a philosophy that recognizes only beings, nothing at all. The doctrine is that of *Was ist Metaphysik?*, delivered the same year, and the two texts must be read together on this point: transcendence is being held out into the nothing, and the *Kantbuch* derives it from the structure of finite knowing.

Since finitude is placed as clearly as this in the approach to transcendence, there is no need — in order to escape a supposed "subjective idealism" — of a "turn to the object," a slogan today again praised too loudly and with too little understanding of the problem. What the essence of finitude does press toward, unavoidably, is the question of the conditions of the possibility of a *prior* being-turned-toward the object, that is, of the essence of the ontological turning-toward the object as such. And Kant poses this decisive question in the transcendental deduction — as the first to do so. The realist reaction to idealism is dismissed as a change of allegiance that leaves the question untouched: turning to the object presupposes that one can turn to objects, and it is that presupposition which the deduction investigates. The target is the realism of the day, Nicolai Hartmann's above all.

"And here it is necessary to make oneself understood about what one means by the expression of an object of representations" (A 104). What must be investigated is the character of what stands over against in the pure letting-stand-over-against. "We find that our thought of the relation of all knowledge to its object carries something of necessity with it, since the object is regarded as that which is *against* our cognitions being determined at random or arbitrarily, rather than a priori in a certain way." In letting-stand-over-against as such, something announces itself "that is against."

Kant appeals in exhibiting this resistantness to an immediate finding, and does not omit to characterize its own structure more closely. It must be well noted: what is at issue is not a resistance-character in beings, not even the pressing of sensations, but the *prior resistantness of being*. The objectivity of objects "carries with it" a necessitation. By it everything that encounters is in advance forced together toward an accordance, in relation to which it can then also first turn out to be discordant. In this prior and constant drawing-together toward unity lies a holding-of-unity-before-oneself. But the representing of a representingly unifying unity is the essence of the kind of representation Kant calls a concept, which he names "a consciousness" in the sense of representing unity (A 105 f.). The letting-stand-over-against is accordingly the "original concept" and, since conceptual representing is ascribed to the understanding, the original act of the understanding. But the understanding contains in itself, as a closed whole, a manifold of modes of uniting. So the pure understanding reveals itself as the capacity of letting-stand-over-against. As a whole it gives in advance what is against randomness; originally representing unity, and representing it as unifying, it represents to itself a bindingness that regulates all possible togetherness in advance. "Now the representation of a universal condition according to which a certain manifold can be posited (hence in a uniform way) is called a rule" (A 113); and the concept, "however imperfect or obscure it may be," "is always, as regards its form, something universal, and something that serves as a rule" (A 106). Resistance is thereby relocated: it is not that things push back, but that being is binding. That is why the "turn to the object" changes nothing — the resistance realism appeals to is already an ontological achievement.

But the pure concepts, the *conceptus reflectentes*, are those that have such regulating unities as their sole content. They do not merely serve as a rule; as pure representing they first and in advance give something rule-like. Only in connection with the illumination of letting-stand-over-against does Kant therefore win the more original concept of the understanding: "now we can characterize it as the faculty of rules. This mark is more fruitful and comes nearer to its essence" (A 126).

If the understanding now is supposed to make letting-stand-over-against possible, if it can regulate in advance everything intuition will ever supply, is it not thereby declared the highest faculty? Does the servant not turn into the master? What then becomes of the serving position that has constantly been given out as its essence and as the proper index of its finitude? Has Kant, in the middle of the central problematic of the deduction, forgotten the finitude of the understanding, if his explanation of it as the faculty of rules is to come nearer its essence?

But if that imputation is impossible, since the finitude of reason releases, determines and bears the whole problem of the possibility of metaphysics, how is the position of dominance now becoming manifest to be brought into accord with the serving position? Is the understanding's ruling and holding sway, as the letting-stand-over-against of rules of unity, at bottom a serving? Does it exercise a service by which it most deeply betrays its finitude, because in letting-stand-over-against it announces precisely the most original neediness of the finite being?

Indeed the understanding is the highest faculty — *in* finitude, that is, the most finite of all. And if so, then in letting-stand-over-against as the original act of the pure understanding its dependence on intuition must come to light most sharply of all. Not on an empirical intuition, of course, but on the pure one.

Only insofar as the pure understanding, as understanding, is servant of pure intuition can it remain master of empirical intuition. The formula resolves the whole apparent conflict: the understanding rules what is given empirically because it is itself in service to what gives purely, and its sovereignty over appearances is the exact measure of its dependence on time.

But pure intuition itself, and precisely it, is again of finite essence. Only their essential structural unity sinks pure intuition and pure thinking into their full finitude, which announces itself as transcendence. And if the pure synthesis originally unites the elements of pure knowledge, then the unveiling of the full synthesis-structure of the pure synthesis must suggest itself as the task that alone leads to the goal of the transcendental deduction: the illumination of transcendence.

##### **§ 17. Die zwei Wege der transzendentalen Deduktion**

From the determination of the problematic of ontological knowledge, the sense of the transcendental deduction has emerged: it is the dissecting unveiling of the structural whole of the pure synthesis. This reading corresponds at first little to the word-concept of a deduction, and seems even to contradict Kant's own explicit explanation of what deduction means. But before that can be decided, the deduction must first be re-enacted in its execution and so concretely laid before our eyes. The interpretation here holds to the "third section" of the "Deduction of the pure concepts of the understanding" (A 115–128), in which Kant presents the deduction "in connection."

The heading of that section brings clearly to expression that the problem of the inner possibility of ontological knowledge is nothing other than the unveiling of transcendence: the deduction treats "of the relation of the understanding to objects in general and the possibility of knowing these a priori." To understand the double course Kant lets the deduction take, its task must be recalled.

Beings become accessible for a finite being only on the ground of a prior turning-toward that lets stand over against. This takes the being that may encounter in advance into the unity-horizon of a possible belonging-together. This a priori unifying unity must anticipate toward what encounters. But what encounters is already in advance embraced by the horizon of time held forth in pure intuition. The anticipating unifying unity of the pure understanding must therefore already beforehand have united itself with pure intuition as well.

This a priori single whole of pure intuition and pure understanding "forms" the play-space of letting-stand-over-against, into which all beings can encounter. With a view to this whole of transcendence it must be shown how — and thereby at the same time that — pure understanding and pure intuition are a priori dependent on each other.

This demonstration of the inner possibility of transcendence can evidently be led on two ways.

The first: the presentation sets in with the pure understanding and, through illumination of its essence, shows its innermost dependence on time. This way begins as it were "above," with the understanding, and leads down to intuition (A 116–120).

The second way goes "from below up" (A 119), beginning with intuition and leading to the pure understanding (A 120–128).

On both ways the unveiling of the "two extreme ends, namely sensibility and understanding," which must "necessarily connect" (A 124), is carried out. What is essential is not a linearly conceived linking of two faculties but the structural illumination of their essential unity, so that what becomes decisive is that in which they can connect at all. On both ways this unifying middle must be traversed and thereby brought to light as such. In this going back and forth between the two ends the unveiling of pure synthesis happens. The two ways are thus not two proofs of one conclusion but two passages through one middle, and their doubling is what makes the middle visible — a middle reached from one direction only might be an artefact of that direction.

###### **a) Der erste Weg**

The necessary dependence of the pure understanding on pure intuition must be unveiled so that the mediating unity of the two, the pure synthesis, reveals itself as mediatrix. This requires that the pure understanding, as the starting point of the first way, be clarified far enough for its dependence on a pure synthesis, and thereby on a pure intuition, to become visible from its structure.

The "deduction" is accordingly anything but a deductively logical inferring of the understanding's relations to pure synthesis and pure intuition. It takes the whole of pure finite knowledge into view already at the outset, and in holding fast to what is so glimpsed it proceeds from one element to the other in explicitly setting off the structural relations that joint the whole. Without the sustained fore-look to the finitude of transcendence, every sentence of the transcendental deduction remains unintelligible. The deduction is thus circular in the way any structural analysis is: it can set off the parts only because it already has the whole in view, and a reader who demands that the conclusion be inferred from premises will find only obscurity.

The character of the "against" that makes standing-over-against possible announces itself in a holding-forth of unity. In this representing of unity, the representing is manifest to itself as what binds itself to the unity — as the same that maintains itself throughout the act of purely representing unity. Only in the manifestness that it, the representer of unity as such, is that against which the unifying unity is held as regulating, can anything come toward this representing. Only in such self-turning-toward can what encounters "concern us" (A 116).

The representing of unity has, as pure thinking, necessarily the character of the "I think." The pure concept as consciousness of unity in general is necessarily pure self-consciousness. This pure consciousness of unity is not merely carried out on occasion and in fact but must be possible at all times; it is essentially an "I am able." "This pure, original, unchangeable consciousness I will now call transcendental apperception" (A 107). The letting-stand-over-against representing of unity is grounded in this apperception "as a faculty" (A 117 note). For only as the constantly free "I can" is the "I think" able to let the "against" of unity stand over against it, if binding remains possible only in relation to an essentially free comportment. The pure understanding acts, in its original holding-forth of unity, as transcendental apperception. The reading of the "I think" as an "I can" is the decisive move: apperception is not a standing representation accompanying others but a capacity, and only a capacity can be free enough to bind itself.

What, then, is represented in the unity held forth by apperception? Perhaps the all of beings at once, in the sense of the *totum simul* that the *intuitus originarius* intuits? But this pure thinking is finite, and as such it cannot from itself set beings over against itself by its representing at all, let alone everything at once in its unity. The represented unity precisely *awaits* the encountering being, and as such awaiting it makes possible the encountering of objects that show themselves with one another. As non-ontic, this unity bears in itself the essential tendency toward a uniting of what is not yet united. Hence Kant says of the unity represented in transcendental apperception, right after clarifying it, that it "presupposes a synthesis, or includes it" (A 118).

Kant wavers here, characteristically, in the univocal determination of the structural relation of the unity to the unifying synthesis. In every case the synthesis belongs essentially and necessarily to the unity: the unity is from the outset unifying. Which means that representing unity is carried out *as* a uniting, to whose structural wholeness the having-in-advance of unity is required. Kant does not shrink from saying that transcendental apperception "presupposes" the synthesis. That is the sentence on which the whole verdict of the Third Section rests: the supreme principle of the *Critique* is here said by Kant himself to presuppose the imagination.

Now it emerged already in the second stage that all synthesis is effected by the imagination. Transcendental apperception is accordingly related essentially to the pure imagination. And this, as pure, cannot re-present something empirically pregiven with respect to which it would be merely reproductive; as pure imagination it is necessarily a priori forming, that is, purely productive. Kant also calls the pure productive imagination the "transcendental" one. "Thus the principle of the necessary unity of the pure (productive) synthesis of the imagination *before* apperception is the ground of the possibility of all knowledge, especially of experience" (A 118).

What does the "before apperception" mean here? Does Kant mean that pure synthesis is prior to transcendental apperception in the order of grounding the possibility of a pure knowledge? That reading would coincide with the earlier statement that apperception "presupposes" pure synthesis.

Or does the "before" mean something else besides? Kant does in fact use *vor* in a meaning that first gives the whole sentence its decisive structural sense, and one that includes the first reading within it. He speaks once "of an object *before* a quite different intuition" (A 287, B 343 f.). Changing the *vor* to a *für* at that place is superfluous and weakening at once, especially if one recalls the Latin expression *coram intuitu intellectuali* [in the presence of an intellectual intuition] that Kant likewise uses (A 249). Taking the *vor* in the quoted sentence as *coram*, in the presence of, first brings to light the character of the structural unity of transcendental apperception and pure imagination: the representing of unity has essentially before it, in view, a unifying unity — the representing is in itself a unifying. A single preposition thus carries the argument, and the philological point is doing systematic work: read as "for," the sentence makes the synthesis serve apperception; read as "in the presence of," it makes apperception stand before a synthesis already at work.

But pure synthesis is to unite a priori, and what it unites must be given a priori for it. The universal intuition that gives purely and receptively in advance is time. The pure imagination must therefore relate itself essentially to time, and only so does it unveil itself as the mediatrix between transcendental apperception and time.

Kant therefore sends ahead of all the discussions of the transcendental deduction a "general remark" that "must be laid at the ground of what follows" (A 99): all "modifications of the mind are subject to time, in which they must all be ordered, connected and brought into relations." It may strike one that Kant discusses the a priori essential relatedness of the pure imagination to time more thoroughly and explicitly on neither of the two ways. The whole analysis concentrates instead on making visible the essential relatedness of the pure understanding to the pure synthesis of the imagination — for it is through this relatedness that the understanding's ownmost nature, its finitude, comes most clearly to expression. It is understanding only insofar as it "presupposes or includes" pure imagination: "the unity of apperception in relation to the synthesis of the imagination is the understanding, and the very same unity, in relation to the transcendental synthesis of the imagination, the pure understanding" (A 119). The gap Heidegger notes here is the one his own Third Section will fill: Kant establishes that the imagination mediates without ever setting out how it is related to time, and that unexamined relation is where the Kantbuch does its own work.

###### **b) Der zweite Weg**

The necessary dependence of pure intuition on the pure understanding — the mediating unity of the two, the pure synthesis — is to become manifest as mediatrix. Hence the second way sets in: "now we will lay before our eyes the necessary connection of the understanding with appearances by means of the categories by beginning from below up, namely with the empirical" (A 119).

Even here, where it would be natural to exhibit explicitly the pure condition of the receptivity of finite knowledge, Kant does not dwell on a discussion of pure intuition, that is, time, but passes at once to the demonstration that "sense" does indeed take in, but that anything like a connectedness of what encounters is "not to be had" in it. Such connectedness must nevertheless be experienceable in finite knowing, because the finite being never has beings as *totum simul* but, as Kant here says explicitly, meets what encounters "dispersed and singly" (A 120). But for what arrives to be able to encounter as standing in connectedness, something like "connection" must be understood in advance. To represent connection in advance means, however, first to form representingly anything like relation at all. And this power that first "forms" relations is the pure imagination. The etymology is being made to work: *Einbildungskraft* is a power of forming (*bilden*), and what it forms here is not an image but a relation.

That in which anything can at once be linked and connection be formed is, according to the "general remark," time as pure universal intuition. Letting beings be encountered which are to be able to show themselves in their standing-over-against connectedness must therefore be grounded in the essentially time-related pure imagination. In purely forming determinate relations it gives in advance a regulating uniting, and with that something that is in advance against what encounters being taken in at random. This horizon of regulating binding contains the pure "affinity" of appearances. "It is therefore strange, yet from the foregoing evident, that only by means of this transcendental function of the imagination even the affinity of appearances becomes possible" (A 123).

But all connecting, and all the more the pure forming of uniting as such, structurally includes a prior representing of unity. If the pure synthesis is to act a priori, this representing must itself be a priori, in such a way that it belongs constantly to all forming of unities as an unchangeable one and the same. This "standing and abiding" self is the I of transcendental apperception. As time belongs to all empirical intuition, so to that same intuition, as a letting-beings-be-encountered in their own order, belongs the prior forming of affinity in the transcendental imagination. And pure apperception must be added to this, if the taking-in is to be borne by a pure turning-toward, a letting-stand-over-against (A 124).

But the first way showed that transcendental apperception, which must be added to pure intuition through the essential mediation of the pure imagination, is not itself independently and in isolation present, and therefore does not merely join itself to the pure imagination because the latter incidentally needs it. This transcendental apperception must for its part, as a representing of unity, have lying before it a unity that forms itself in uniting. So on the second way too everything presses toward letting the transcendental imagination come forward as mediatrix. "We therefore have a pure imagination as a basic faculty of the human soul, which grounds all knowledge a priori. By means of it we bring the manifold of intuition on the one side into connection, and this into connection with the condition of the necessary unity of pure apperception on the other" (A 124). Heidegger defends the received text against Erdmann's and Riehl's proposed deletion of the "and," since removing it removes the decisive sense — that the transcendental imagination unites pure intuition within itself *and* unites this with pure apperception. The imagination performs two unifications, not one; without the first, time is not a unified whole to be brought before the I at all.

The triad of pure intuition, pure imagination and pure apperception is now no longer a juxtaposition of faculties. Through unveiling the mediating forming of the pure synthesis, the transcendental deduction has demonstrated the inner possibility of the essential unity of pure knowledge. This unity forms the pure letting-stand-over-against, and as this forming it first makes manifest anything like a horizon of objectivity as such. And because pure knowledge in this way first breaks open the play-space necessary for a finite being, in which "all relation of being or non-being takes place" (A 110), it must be called ontological.

Since finitude is distinguished by the understanding, the understanding plays a special role in the deduction. But precisely on the going back and forth of the two ways it gives up its primacy, and by this giving-up announces itself in its essence, which lies in having to be grounded in the time-related pure synthesis of the transcendental imagination. The understanding's self-abdication is the result of the deduction, and it is what the Third Section will convert into the thesis that Kant found the root and shrank from it.

##### **§ 18. Die äußere Form der transzendentalen Deduktion**

What occasions the transcendental deduction, as a laying of transcendence before our eyes, to take on the form of a *quaestio juris*? Where does the right of this "juristic" formulation lie, and where its limits — a formulation that presses forward only in the first introduction of the deduction, not in its execution?

Kant uses "deduction" not in the philosophical meaning of *deductio* as distinguished from *intuitus*, as in Descartes' *Regulae*, but as the "teachers of law" understand the word. In a legal action "entitlements" are asserted, or "presumptions" rejected. Two things are necessary for that: establishing the facts and the object of dispute (*quid facti*), and then exhibiting what holds by right as a grounded entitlement (*quid juris*). The exhibition of the legal possibility of an entitlement is what the jurists call a deduction.

Why then does Kant bring the problem of the possibility of metaphysics into the form of such a juristic deduction? Does a legal action lie at the ground of the problem of the inner possibility of ontology?

It was shown in §2 how the question of the possibility of *metaphysica generalis* springs for Kant from the question of the possibility of the traditional *metaphysica specialis*. That discipline wants to know the supersensible being rationally, from mere concepts. In the pure concepts, the categories, there lies the presumption of an a priori *ontic* knowledge. Do they have a right to this entitlement or not?

The confrontation with traditional metaphysics "in its final purpose," concerning its own possibility, thus becomes a legal action. Pure reason must be "put on trial," the "witnesses" heard; Kant speaks of a "court" (A 669, B 697; A 703, B 731). The legal action lying in the problem of ontological knowledge requires the deduction, that is, the exhibition of the possibility of the a priori relatability of the pure concepts to objects. Because the entitlement to use these concepts, which do not stem from experience, can never be demonstrated by appeal to their factual use, the pure concepts "always need deduction" (A 85, B 117). The juristic form is thus not a metaphor Kant chose for effect: the legal action is real, and the defendant is school metaphysics.

The entitlement of the categories must be decided by illuminating their essence. As pure representations of unities within a finite representing, they are essentially dependent on pure synthesis and thereby on pure intuition. Otherwise put: the solution of the task formulated merely as a *quaestio juris* consists in unveiling the essence of the categories — they are not notions but pure concepts related essentially to time through the pure imagination. And insofar as they are of this essence, they make up transcendence itself; they co-form the letting-stand-over-against. Hence they are in advance determinations of objects, that is, of beings insofar as these encounter a finite being. The legal question is answered by an ontological one, and the answer is stronger than the question required: the categories are entitled to apply to objects because there would be no objects without them.

Through the analytic illumination of the essence of the categories as essentially necessary building-stones, or joints, of transcendence, their "objective reality" is demonstrated. But to understand the problem of the objective reality of the categories as a problem of transcendence, it is unavoidable not to take Kant's title "reality" in the meaning of present-day "epistemology," where "reality" means as much as "actuality" — what Kant designates by "Dasein" or "existence." *Realitas* means rather, as Kant himself aptly translates it, *Sachheit*, thing-content, and means the what-content of a being circumscribed by the *essentia*. Under the title of the objective reality of the categories the question stands: how far can the content represented in the pure concepts be a determination of what stands over against finite knowledge, of beings as objects? The categories are objectively real insofar as they belong to the ontological knowledge that "forms" the transcendence of the finite being, the letting-stand-over-against.

It is easy to see what follows. If one interprets "objective reality" not from the essence of the pure synthesis of the transcendental imagination, as which it forms the essential unity of ontological knowledge, but holds primarily and exclusively to the expression Kant uses with a view to the outer, introductory formulation of the deduction as a juristic question — the title "objective validity" — and then takes validity, contrary to the sense of Kant's problem, as the logical validity of the judgment, the decisive problem is pushed entirely out of view. The line of misreading is traced exactly, from a Latin term mistranslated as actuality, through a juristic formula taken for the matter, to a logic of validity — and it is the line the Southwest German school followed.

The problem of the "origin and truth" of the categories (A 128) is the question of the possible manifestness of the being of beings in the essential unity of ontological knowledge. If this question is to be concretely grasped and taken up as a problem, the *quaestio juris* may not be construed as a question of validity; it is only the formula for the task of an analytic of transcendence, that is, of a pure phenomenology of the subjectivity of the subject — and of the subject as finite. Naming the deduction a pure phenomenology places Husserl's title over Kant's chapter and simultaneously alters it: a phenomenology of subjectivity that takes finitude as its theme is no longer a phenomenology of transcendental consciousness, and the difference is the whole distance between GA 3 and *Ideen I*.

But if the fundamental problem to which the inherited *metaphysica specialis* gave occasion is thereby solved precisely by the transcendental deduction, does the laying of the ground not already reach its goal with the stage now discussed? And does what has been said not at the same time confirm the right of the usual practice of regarding the transcendental deduction as the central discussion within the positive part of the Doctrine of Elements? Why still a further stage of the laying of the ground of ontological knowledge? What demands a still more original regress into the ground of the essential unity of ontological knowledge?

#### **Das vierte Stadium der Grundlegung: Der Grund der inneren Möglichkeit der ontologischen Erkenntnis**

The inner possibility of ontological knowledge shows itself from the specific wholeness of the constitution of transcendence, whose cohesive middle is the pure imagination. Kant finds this result of the laying of the ground not merely "strange"; he repeatedly stresses the darkness into which all the discussions of the transcendental deduction must press. At the same time the laying of the ground of ontological knowledge strives, beyond a mere characterization of transcendence, to illuminate it so that it can be developed into a systematic whole of a presentation of transcendence — transcendental philosophy, that is, ontology.

The transcendental deduction did make the whole of ontological knowledge in its unity a problem. But there, given the central significance of finitude and the dominance of the logical, rational formulation of the question in metaphysics, the understanding stood in the foreground — its relation to the unity-forming middle, the pure imagination.

Yet if all knowledge is primarily intuition and finite intuition has the character of taking-in, then a fully valid clarification of transcendence must discuss just as explicitly the relation of the transcendental imagination to pure intuition, and thereby the relation of the pure understanding to pure intuition. Such a task leads to presenting the transcendental imagination in its unifying function, and with it the self-forming of transcendence and its horizon in its innermost happening. Kant undertakes this laying free of the essential ground of ontological knowledge as finite pure intuition in the section that follows the transcendental deduction, headed "On the schematism of the pure concepts of the understanding" (A 137–147, B 176–187). The fourth stage is therefore not a supplement to the deduction but its missing half: the deduction ran from the side of the understanding, and the schematism runs it from the side of intuition.

Even this pointer to the systematic position of the schematism chapter within the order of the stages betrays that these eleven pages of the *Critique of Pure Reason* must make up the core piece of the whole extensive work. That central significance can of course strike the eye properly only on the basis of the substantive interpretation, which has to hold to the basic question of the transcendence of the finite being. The claim is the most famous and most contested in the book, and it is made on structural grounds before any exegesis is offered.

Kant again introduces the problem in a more external form, on the guiding thread of the question of the possible subsumption of appearances under the categories. Justifying that formulation is to follow, as with the treatment of the *quaestio juris*, only after the inner dynamic of the schematism problem has been worked out.

##### **§ 19. Transzendenz und Versinnlichung**

A finite being must be able to take in beings, precisely if these are to be manifest as already present. But taking-in demands, for its own possibility, something like a turning-toward — not an arbitrary one, but one that makes the encountering of beings possible in advance. And for beings to be able to offer themselves as such, the horizon of their possible encountering must itself have the character of an offer. The turning-toward must in itself be a pre-forming holding-before-oneself of the offerable as such.

But for the horizon of letting-stand-over-against to function as such a horizon, this offer-character needs to be perceivable. Perceivable means: immediately receivable in intuition. The horizon must therefore offer itself in advance and constantly as a pure look. From which it follows that the letting-stand-over-against of the finite understanding must offer objectivity as such intuitively — that the pure understanding must be grounded in a pure intuition that leads and bears it. The argument moves in one step from the offer-character of the horizon to the necessity that the horizon be *seen*, and everything the fourth stage does follows from it: a horizon that could not be looked at could not let anything be met within it.

What then belongs to this making-perceivable of the horizon of the prior turning-toward? The finite being that turns toward must be able to make the horizon intuitive to itself, that is, to "form" the look of the offer from itself. And since, as the transcendental deduction showed, pure intuition (time) stands in essential relation to pure synthesis, it is the pure imagination that performs the forming of the horizon-look. But then it "forms" not only the intuitive perceivability of the horizon, by "creating" it as free turning-toward; as forming in this sense it is forming in a second sense too — it procures anything like an *image*.

*Bild* is to be taken here in its most original sense, the one in which we say that the landscape offers a beautiful "picture," or that the assembly offered a sad "picture." Image means the look a thing presents, not a representation of it. And already on the second way of the deduction, which starts from the inner connection of time with the pure imagination, Kant says of the imagination that it "is to bring into an image" (A 120).

Only in the happening of this doubled forming — the procuring of a look — does the ground of the possibility of transcendence become visible, and the necessary look-character of transcendence's prior standing-over-against and offering essence become intelligible.

But transcendence is finitude itself. If it is to make intuitive, in letting-stand-over-against, the horizon that forms itself there, and if finite intuition is what "sensibility" means, then the offering of a look can only be a rendering-sensible of the horizon. The horizon of transcendence can form itself only in a rendering-sensible (*Versinnlichung*).

Seen from the pure understanding, letting-stand-over-against is a representing of unities as such that regulate all uniting — the pure concepts. Transcendence therefore forms itself in the rendering-sensible of the pure concepts; and since it is a prior turning-toward, this rendering-sensible must likewise be a pure one.

Pure rendering-sensible happens as a "schematism": the pure imagination gives in advance, by forming schemata, the look — the "image" — of the horizon of transcendence. That the mere pointer to such a rendering-sensible does not suffice, quite apart from the fact that it cannot even be exhibited factually unless its essence is known beforehand, can be gathered from the idea of a pure rendering-sensible itself.

Sensibility means for Kant finite intuition. A pure sensibility would be an intuiting that takes in the intuitable in advance, before all empirical receiving. But finite intuition precisely cannot produce an intuitable being in intuiting. Pure rendering-sensible must therefore be the taking-in of something that first forms itself in the very taking-in — the taking-in of a look which nevertheless does not offer beings.

What character, then, does the intuitable of pure sensibility have? Can it have that of an "image"? What does image mean? How is the look that "forms" itself in the pure imagination, the pure schema, distinguished from the image? And in what sense can the schema in the end also be called an "image"? Without a preceding interpretation of these phenomena of rendering-sensible, the schematism as the ground of transcendence remains veiled in complete darkness.

##### **§ 20. Bild und Schema**

Rendering-sensible names generally the way in which a finite being can make something intuitive to itself, that is, procure a look, an image, of something. Depending on what comes into view and how, look or image mean different things.

Image can first mean the look of a determinate being insofar as it is manifest as present: the being offers the look. Derived from this meaning, image can further mean the copying look of something present — a copy — or the after-forming look of something no longer present, or the pre-forming look of a being still to be produced.

And then "image" can have the quite wide meaning of look in general, without its being said whether a being or a non-being becomes intuitable in this look.

Kant does in fact use "image" in three meanings: the immediate look of a being, the present copying look of a being, and the look of something in general. These meanings are not particularly set off against one another; indeed it is questionable whether the enumerated meanings and modes of being-an-image suffice to illuminate what Kant discusses under the title of "schematism." The ambiguity is Kant's, and the analysis that follows exists to supply a fourth sense he needed and did not distinguish.

The most familiar way of procuring a look, of giving an image, is the empirical intuiting of what shows itself. What shows itself here always has the character of the immediately seen individual, a "this-here" — which does not exclude that a manifold of such is intuited as one richer "this-here," for instance this individual whole of this landscape. It is called a look, *species*, as though it were looking at us. An image is therefore always an intuitable this-here, and every copy — a photograph, say — remains only a transcription of what shows itself immediately as "image." The Latin *species* is being read back to its verbal root: what we call the look of a thing is a seeing that goes in both directions, and the point will matter when the *eidos* is invoked below.

"Image" is used just as often in this second sense of a copy. This thing here, this present photograph, immediately offers a look as this thing, and so is an image in the first, wide sense; but in showing itself it wants to show precisely what is copied in it. To procure an "image" in this second sense no longer means merely to intuit a being immediately, but for instance to buy or produce a photograph.

From such a copy in turn — a death mask, say — an after-image, a photograph, can be produced. The after-image can directly after-form the copy and so show the "image," the immediate look, of the dead person himself. The photograph of the death mask is, as after-image of a copy, itself an image, but only because it gives the "image" of the dead person, shows him as he looks or looked. On the meanings so far delimited, rendering-sensible means the way of immediate empirical intuiting, and the way of immediately contemplating a copy in which the look of a being offers itself.

But the photograph can also show how anything like a death mask in general looks. The death mask in turn can show how the face of a dead human being in general looks — though an individual dead person can show that too. And so the mask itself can show how a death mask in general looks, and the photograph can show not only what was photographed but how a photograph in general looks. The example is worked with unusual patience because the whole point turns on it: one and the same visible thing can show an individual, and can show how something in general looks, and the difference is not in the thing but in how it is looked at.

What do the looks of this dead person, this mask, this photograph now show? What "look" — *eidos*, *idea* — do they give now? What do they render sensible now? They show how something looks "in general," in the One that holds for many. But this unity for several is what a representation in the manner of a concept represents. These looks are now to serve the rendering-sensible of concepts.

This rendering-sensible can no longer mean procuring an immediate look, an intuition, of a concept; for the concept, as the represented universal, cannot be represented in a *repraesentatio singularis*, which is what intuition is. And precisely for that reason the concept is essentially not copyable.

What, then, does the rendering-sensible of a concept mean at all, and what belongs to it? How is the look of the empirically accessible or made-present thing, or of its possible copies, involved in this making-intuitive? We say that this perceived house shows how a house in general looks, hence what we represent in the concept house. In what way does this house-look show the how of the look of a house in general? The house itself does offer this determinate look — but we are not sunk in that look in order to learn how just this house looks. This house shows itself rather as one that, in order to be a house, does not necessarily have to look as it looks. It shows us "only" the *how-as* a house can look.

This how of possible empirical looking is what we represent in the face of this determinate house. A house could look like this. This present house has decided, with its look, for one determinate possibility within the circle of possibilities of looking; but the result of that decision interests us as little as the results of the decisions taken by the factual looks of other houses. What we are after is the circle of possible looking as such — more precisely, what draws this circle: what regulates and prefigures how something must look at all in order to be able to offer the corresponding look as a house. This prefiguring of the rule is no register in the sense of a mere enumeration of the "marks" to be found on a house, but a marking-out of the whole of what is meant by anything like "house." The distinction between an inventory of features and the marking-out of a possibility-range is what separates a schema from a definition, and with it the whole doctrine of the concept from the logic of genus and differentia.

But this meant is meanable at all only in being represented as what regulates the possible belonging of this whole into an empirical look. Only in representing the manner in which the rule regulates the drawing of itself into a possible look can the unity of the concept be represented at all as unifying, as many-valid. If the concept is what serves as a rule, then conceptual representing means the giving-in-advance of the rule of a possible procuring of a look, in the manner of that rule's regulating. Such representing is then structurally necessarily related to a possible look, and therefore in itself a peculiar kind of rendering-sensible.

It gives no immediate intuitive look of the concept. Whatever immediate look necessarily occurs along with it is not specifically thematically meant, but is meant as a possible presentable of the presentation whose manner of regulating is represented. So in the empirical look it is precisely the rule that comes to appearance, in the manner of its regulating.

But this rendering-sensible not only gives no immediate look of the concept as unity; that unity is itself not thematically meant as a free-floating content of representation either. The conceptual unity is what it can and should be as unifying only as the regulating one. The unity is not grasped; it stands in view as what essentially determines the regulation only when one looks *away* from it toward its determining of the rule. This looking-away does not lose the unity from view but has it, precisely as regulating, in the fore-look. Concepts are thus never objects of the thinking that uses them; the same structure governs Heidegger's account of equipment, which is what it is only when looked through rather than at.

Representing the procedure of regulating as such is properly conceptual representing. What was called that until now, the representing of many-valid unity, was only a detached element of the concept — an element that remained concealed precisely in respect of its function as the rule of the specific rendering-sensible just described.

If in rendering-sensible neither the empirical look nor the isolated concept is thematically represented, but the "register" of the rule of image-procuring, then this too needs closer characterization. The rule is represented in the how of its regulating, that is, in how it dictates itself, regulating the presentation, into the presenting look. Representing this how of the regulating is the free "forming" — unbound to any determinate present thing — of a rendering-sensible as an image-procuring in the sense characterized.

Such rendering-sensible happens primarily in the imagination. "This representation of a general procedure of the imagination for procuring an image for a concept I call the schema of this concept" (A 140, B 179 f.). Schema-formation in its execution, as the way concepts are rendered sensible, is called schematism. The schema is to be distinguished from the image and yet is related to something like an image; the image-character belongs necessarily to the schema. That character has its own essence: it is neither a plain look (image in the first sense) nor a copy (image in the second). Let it therefore be called the *schema-image*. The term is Heidegger's coinage, not Kant's, and it is introduced to name what Kant's three senses of "image" leave out — the visible thing insofar as it displays a rule.

##### **§ 21. Schema und Schema-Bild**

A closer characterization of the schema-image will clarify its relation to the schema, and thereby the kind of relation the concept has to the image. Schema-formation is the rendering-sensible of concepts. How does the look of the immediately represented being relate to what is represented in the concept of it? In what sense is this look an "image" of the concept? The question is discussed on two kinds of concept: the empirically sensible, the concept of a dog, and the purely sensible or mathematical, the concept of a triangle or of a number.

Kant stresses that an "object of experience" — the accessible look of a present thing — "or an image of it," a present copy or after-image, never "reaches" the empirical concept of that thing (A 141, B 180). This not-reaching means first: not presenting adequately. But it may in no way be interpreted as the inability to be an adequate *copy* of the concept, since an empirical look of a being can have no copy-function at all with respect to its concept. The inadequacy belongs rather precisely to the schema-image, which in a genuine sense is an image of the concept. One could even say rather that the empirical look contains everything the concept contains, if not more. But it does not contain it as the concept represents it, as One holding for many. The content of the empirical look gives itself as one of arbitrarily many, singularized to just this one, which is thematically represented as such. The individual has dismissed arbitrariness and is thereby a possible *example* for the One that regulates many-valid arbitrariness as such. In that regulating the universal has its specifically articulated determinateness, and is by no means an indeterminately blurred "each and every" set over against what is singularized. Inadequacy is thus converted into a positive relation: the image falls short of the concept not by failing at a task but by performing a different one.

The representing of the rule is the schema, and as such it remains necessarily related to possible schema-images, none of which can claim uniqueness. "The concept of a dog signifies a rule according to which my imagination can delineate the shape of a four-footed animal in general, without being restricted to any single particular shape that experience offers me, or to any possible image that I can present in concreto" (A 141, B 180).

That the empirical look does not reach its empirical concept expresses the positive structural relation of schema-image to schema: the image is a possible presentation of the presentation-rule represented in the schema. Which says at the same time that the concept is nothing at all beyond the representation of this so regulating unity of the rule. What logic calls a concept is grounded in the schema; the concept "always relates immediately to the schema." Heidegger's marginal note draws the conclusion the text leaves standing: this already shows how matters stand with "the" logic. If logic's concepts are founded in schemata, and schemata are accomplishments of the imagination, then logic is not the first discipline and cannot ground metaphysics.

Kant says of the empirical object that it reaches its concept "still much less" than the "image" of a pure sensible concept reaches its. Are the schema-images of mathematical concepts then rather more adequate to their concept? Here too an adaequatio in the sense of a copying is evidently not to be thought: the schema-image of a mathematical construction counts the same whether it is drawn empirically exact or rough.

Kant evidently has in mind that a mathematical schema-image, a drawn triangle for instance, must necessarily be either acute-, right- or obtuse-angled. With that the arbitrariness is already exhausted, whereas in the presentation of a house it is greater. On the other side, the circle of presentability of an acute-angled or a right-angled triangle has a greater breadth. This schema-image thus comes, by its restriction, more into the vicinity of the unity of the concept, and by this greater breadth more into the vicinity of the universality of that unity. But however that may be, the image has the face of an individual, while the schema has "in view" the unity of the general rule of manifoldly possible presentations.

Only from this does the essential feature of the schema-image become clear: it has its look-character not only and not first from the image-content currently visible in it, but from the fact that, and the way in which, it springs out of the possible presentation represented in its regulating, and so holds the rule as it were into the sphere of possible intuitability. Only if "image" is understood in this sense of the schema-image can five points set one after another be called "an image of the number five" (A 140, B 179). The number itself never looks like the five points, nor for that matter like the signs 5 or V — though these are again, in another way, looks of the number in question. The figure 5 drawn in space has nothing in common with the number at all, whereas the look of five points is at least countable by the number five. This row of points shows the number not because it is surveyable and we can seemingly take the number off it, but because it coincides with the representation of the rule of the possible presentability of that number.

And again we do not grasp the number first on the ground of this coincidence. We always already have every number in the "representation of a method of representing a multitude — a thousand, say — in an image in accordance with a certain concept." In representing the presentation-rule, the image-possibility already forms itself, and this, not the isolated look of a manifold of points, is already the genuine look that structurally belongs to the schema: the schema-image. Whether an actually drawn or merely represented row of points is intuitively surveyable or not remains irrelevant for the "seeing" of the schema-image. Hence mathematical concepts too are never grounded on plainly visible images but on schemata: "in fact it is not images of objects but schemata that ground our pure sensible concepts" (A 140 f., B 180). Kant's own example — a thousand, which no one can survey — is what makes the point unanswerable: the image-possibility of a number is available where the image is not.

The analysis of the image-character of the schema-image of empirical and pure sensible concepts already yields that the rendering-sensible of concepts is a quite peculiar procuring of peculiar images. The schema-forming rendering-sensible in the schematism can neither be grasped on the analogy of ordinary "pictorial presentation" nor be reduced to it. The latter is so little possible that conversely the rendering-sensible first described — the immediate empirical looking at things and the production of present copies of them — is possible only on the ground of the possible rendering-sensible of concepts in the manner of schematism.

All conceptual representing is by its essence schematism. But all finite knowing is, as thinking intuition, necessarily conceptual. And so already in the immediate perception of something present, of this house, there necessarily lies the schematizing fore-look toward anything like a house in general, from which representing alone what encounters can show itself as a house and offer the look "present house." Schematism therefore happens necessarily on the ground of our knowing as finite. Hence Kant must say: "this schematism ... is a hidden art in the depths of the human soul" (A 141, B 180). And if schematism belongs to the essence of finite knowledge, and finitude centres in transcendence, then the happening of transcendence must in its innermost be a schematism. Kant therefore necessarily comes upon a "transcendental schematism," if he means to raise to light the ground of the inner possibility of transcendence. The famous phrase about a hidden art is thereby stripped of its air of resignation: the concealment is structural, not a limit on Kant's insight, since a schematism we always look through is by its manner of working never in view.

##### **§ 22. Der transzendentale Schematismus**

The general characterization of schematism as a peculiar kind of rendering-sensible has yielded that schematism belongs necessarily to transcendence. From the other side, the characterization of the full structure of ontological knowledge, which is necessarily intuition, led to the insight that a rendering-sensible belongs necessarily to transcendence — and a pure one. It was claimed that this pure rendering-sensible happens as a schematism. That claim must now be grounded by demonstrating that the necessary pure rendering-sensible of the pure understanding and its concepts is carried out in a transcendental schematism; and what that itself is will clarify itself with the unveiling of the happening.

The schema-forming rendering-sensible aims at procuring an image for the concept, so that what is meant in the concept has a regulated relation to a visibility. Only in such intuitiveness does what is conceptually meant become perceivable. The schema brings itself, that is, the concept, into an image. The pure concepts of the understanding, thought in the pure "I think," need an essentially pure visibility, if what stands over against in pure letting-stand-over-against is to be perceivable as an *against*. The pure concepts must be grounded in pure schemata that procure an image for them.

But Kant says expressly: "the schema of a pure concept of the understanding, by contrast, is something that can never be brought into any image at all" (A 142, B 181). If, however, it belongs to the essence of a schema to bring itself into an image, then the expression "image" in that sentence can only mean, and exclude, a determinate kind of image. From the outset only schema-images can be at issue. The denial of a possible imaging of the schema of the notions then means at first merely this: the presentable look whose presentation-rule is represented in the schema of the notion can never be taken from the circle of the empirically intuitive. If image means empirical look in the widest sense, the schema of the notion evidently cannot be brought "into any image at all." But the looks that the mathematical construction of concepts delineates are, as images of "magnitudes," restricted to a determinate region of the objective; and into such an image the notions, as original concepts, cannot be brought either, since they represent those rules in which objectivity as such forms itself as the prior horizon of the possible encountering of all objects. "Image" in that sentence therefore means the kinds of schema-image that belong to the schemata of empirical and mathematical concepts. Kant's flat denial is read as a restricted one, and the restriction is what opens the space for a pure image — an interpretive step of exactly the kind Heidegger's critics have in mind when they call the book violent, and one he does not disguise.

The transcendental deduction yielded that the pure concepts are essentially related to pure intuition, to time, through the mediation of the pure synthesis of the transcendental imagination, and conversely. But so far only the essential necessity of the relation between notion and time has been discussed; the innermost structure of that relation, as the innermost jointure of transcendence, has not yet been illuminated.

Time, however, as pure intuition, is what procures a look before all experience. The pure look that gives itself in such pure intuition — for Kant the pure one-after-another of the now-sequence — must therefore be called a pure image. And Kant himself says in the schematism chapter: "the pure image ... of all objects of the senses in general is time" (A 142, B 182); Heidegger's marginal note glosses "of all objects" as "of their objectivity." The same is expressed at a later and no less important place, where Kant determines the essence of the notion: the notion is "the pure concept insofar as it has its origin solely in the understanding (not in the pure image of sensibility)" (A 320, B 377).

Accordingly the schema of the pure concept of the understanding can very well be brought into an image, provided "image" is now taken as "pure image."

Time as "pure image" is the schema-image, and not merely the form of intuition standing over against the pure concepts. The schema of the notions accordingly has its own character. As schema in general it represents unities, and represents them as rules carrying themselves into a possible look. But the unities represented in the notions relate, according to the deduction, essentially and necessarily to time. The schematism of the pure concepts must therefore necessarily regulate them *into* time. And time is, as the transcendental aesthetic showed, the representation of a "single object": "different times are only parts of one and the same time. But the representation that can be given only through a single object is intuition" (A 31 f., B 47). Time is therefore not only the necessary pure image of the schemata of the pure concepts but also their only pure look-possibility; and this single look-possibility shows in itself nothing other than always only time and the time-like.

If the closed manifold of pure concepts is to have its image in this single look-possibility, then this one pure image must be formable in manifold ways. By regulating themselves into time as pure look, the schemata of the notions give themselves their image out of time, and so articulate the single pure look-possibility into a manifold of pure images. In this way the schemata "determine" time. "The schemata are therefore nothing but a priori time-determinations according to rules" (A 145, B 184), or briefly "transcendental time-determinations" (A 138, B 177); and as such they are "a transcendental product of the imagination" (A 142, B 181). This schematism forms transcendence a priori and is therefore called transcendental schematism.

In transcendence, the letting-stand-over-against of the objective that offers itself, of the against, happens through ontological knowledge, as schematizing intuition, making the transcendental affinity of the rule-unity a priori visible in the image of time and thereby receivable. The transcendental schema has, through its pure schema-image, necessarily an a priori corresponding character; and the executing interpretation of the individual pure schemata as transcendental time-determinations must exhibit this correspondence-forming character.

Kant, however, takes the closed unity of the pure concepts from the table of judgments and gives the definitions of the schemata of the individual pure concepts according to the table of notions. In accordance with the four moments of division of the categories — quantity, quality, relation, modality — the pure look of time must show four possibilities of imageability, as "time-series, time-content, time-order, time-totality" (A 145, B 184 f.). These time-characters are not so much systematically developed out of an analysis of time itself as fixed in it "according to the order of the categories." The interpretation of the individual schemata begins with a relatively detailed analysis of the pure schemata of quantity, reality and substance, then becomes ever more terse and ends in mere definitions. Time is thus articulated by an inherited table rather than analysed on its own terms, and the failure to analyse time itself is what the Third Section will make into the book's central accusation.

In a certain respect Kant has a right to such lapidary presentation. For if the transcendental schematism determines ontological knowledge in the ground of its essence, then the systematic working-out of ontological knowledge in the presentation of the system of synthetic principles a priori must necessarily come upon the schematism-character and exhibit the corresponding transcendental time-determinations. And this does happen, though only within certain limits.

It is easy to see that the more transparently the essential structure of the transcendental schematism, and of everything belonging to the whole of transcendence, is brought to light, the more clearly the ways show themselves for finding one's bearings in the darkness of these most original structures "in the depths of our soul." The general essence of schematism as such, and of the transcendental schematism in particular, is determined unambiguously enough. But that a further advance is possible Kant himself betrays by the remark: "without now detaining ourselves with a dry and tedious analysis of what is required for transcendental schemata of pure concepts of the understanding in general, we would rather present them according to the order of the categories and in connection with them" (A 142, B 181).

Is it only the dryness and tedium of the business that keeps Kant from a further analysis? The answer cannot yet be given; it comes in §35. It will also explain why the present interpretation refrains from attempting a concrete laying-out of Kant's definitions of the pure schemata. But to make visible that Kant's doctrine of the transcendental schematism is no baroque theory but is drawn from the phenomena themselves, a short and rough interpretation of the transcendental schema of one category, substance, may be given. The deferral is a promissory note on the book's decisive thesis: what stops Kant is not tedium but the fact that a genuine analysis of time would have dissolved the primacy of the understanding.

"The schema of substance is the persistence of the real in time" (A 143, B 183). For the full illumination of this schema the "First Analogy," the principle of persistence, must be drawn in.

Substance says as a notion at first only: lying at the ground, subsistence. Its schema must be the representation of lying-at-the-ground insofar as this presents itself in the pure image of time. Now time as pure now-sequence is at all times now; in every now it is now. Time thus shows the constancy of itself; as such it is "unchangeable and abiding," it "does not elapse." More sharply: time is not an abiding thing among others, but on the ground of the named essential character — being now in every now — it gives the pure look of anything like abiding as such. As this pure image, this immediate pure look, it presents lying-at-the-ground in pure intuition.

This presenting function becomes properly clear only when — what Kant omits here — the full content of the notion "substance" is heeded. Substance is a category of relation, between subsistence and inherence; it means lying-at-the-ground for something adhering. Time is therefore pure image of the notion substance only if it presents precisely this relation in the pure image.

But time as now-sequence is, precisely by being a now flowing in every now, at each time also another now. As the look of abiding it offers at once the image of pure change within abiding. Substance and its accidents are thereby read off the single structure of the now-sequence: abiding and changing are not two features of time but one, and the notion's relational character is matched exactly.

So even this rough interpretation of the transcendental schema of substance, which cannot begin to press into the more original structures, must show that what is meant in the notion substance can procure itself a pure image a priori in time. Thereby objectivity in the letting-stand-over-against, insofar as substance belongs to it as a constitutive element, becomes a priori visible and perceivable. Through this schematism the notion stands in view in advance as schematized, so that in this prior look toward the pure image of persistence a being unchangeable in change can show itself as such for experience. "To time, then, which is itself unchangeable and abiding, there corresponds in appearance the unchangeable in existence" (A 143, B 183) — that is, in presence-at-hand.

The transcendental schematism is accordingly the ground of the inner possibility of ontological knowledge. It forms what stands over against in the pure letting-stand-over-against in such a way that what is represented in pure thinking necessarily gives itself intuitively in the pure image of time. It is therefore time that, as what gives a priori, lends the horizon of transcendence from the outset the character of a perceivable offer. And not only that: as the single pure universal image, time gives the horizon of transcendence a prior enclosedness. This one pure ontological horizon is the condition of the possibility that beings given within it can have this or that particular open, ontic horizon. Time gives transcendence not only its prior unified cohesion; as what purely gives itself, it offers anything like a halt at all. It makes the "against" of objectivity, which belongs to the finitude of the transcending turning-toward, perceivable for a finite being. Two functions are thus assigned to time in a single sentence — it offers and it holds back — and the second is the more important: without a halt, nothing would resist, and without resistance nothing could be met as a being at all.

##### **§ 23. Schematismus und Subsumtion**

In the foregoing, Kant's doctrine of the schematism of the pure concepts of the understanding was interpreted deliberately in the single orientation toward the innermost happening of transcendence. But Kant follows not only the drift of the problematic that grows anew with every step; precisely at the first introduction of the decisive doctrines he holds to formulations as familiar as possible, which are provisionally to lead toward the problem. So the transcendental deduction started from the legal action in traditional metaphysics, an action decided by the demonstration that the notions must be categories — must belong by their essence to transcendence itself — if they are to be able to determine a priori the empirically accessible being. And with that the condition of the "use" of these concepts is fixed as well.

To use concepts means generally to apply them to objects, or, seen from the objects, to bring these "under" concepts. In the language of traditional logic this use is called subsumption. To use the pure concepts as transcendental determinations of time a priori — to carry out pure knowledge — means the happening of the schematism. Seen from here, the problem of the schematism can indeed be elucidated at first quite well on the guiding thread of subsumption. Only it must be noted that here, in ontological knowledge, ontological concepts are at issue from the outset, and therefore a peculiar, ontological "subsumption."

Kant did not omit, already at the first characterization of the essential unity of ontological knowledge, to point out the fundamental difference between "bringing under concepts," which concerns objects, and "bringing to concepts," which concerns the pure synthesis of the transcendental imagination. The "bringing to concepts" of the pure synthesis happens in the transcendental schematism. It "forms" the unity represented in the notion into the essential element of purely visible objectivity. In the transcendental schematism the categories first form themselves *as* categories. And if these are the genuine "original concepts," then the transcendental schematism is the original and proper concept-formation as such. The chapter thus does not explain how existing concepts get applied; it explains how the concepts that are categories come to be concepts at all.

When Kant therefore introduces the schematism chapter with the pointer to subsumption, he wants to lead thereby to transcendental subsumption as the central problem, and to show that in the essential structure of pure knowledge the question of the inner possibility of original conceptuality as such breaks open.

Empirical concepts are drawn from experience and therefore "homogeneous" with the content of the beings they determine; their application to objects, their use, is no problem. "But pure concepts of the understanding, in comparison with empirical (indeed with sensible) intuitions in general, are entirely unhomogeneous, and can never be met with in any intuition. How, then, is the subsumption of the latter under the former, hence the application of the category to appearances, possible, since no one will say that the category, e.g. causality, could also be intuited through the senses and is contained in the appearance?" (A 137 f., B 176 f.). In the question of the possible use of the categories, their own essence first becomes a problem: these concepts confront us with the question of the possibility of their "formation" at all. Talk of the subsumption of appearances "under categories" is accordingly not the formula of a solution but contains precisely the question in what sense one may here still speak of subsumption "under concepts."

Taken merely as an introduction of the problem, Kant's formulation of the schematism problem as a subsumption problem even gives a pointer to the most central intention and thereby to the core content of the chapter.

To represent conceptually means to represent something "in general." With concept-formation as such, the "universality" of representing must become a problem. But if the categories, as ontological concepts, are not homogeneous with empirical objects and their concepts, then their "universality" cannot be that of a merely gradually higher stage of the universality of a higher or highest ontic genus. What character of "generality" does the universality of ontological, that is, metaphysical concepts have? Which is just the question: what does the *generalis* mean in the characterization of ontology as *metaphysica generalis*? The problem of the schematism of the pure concepts is the question of the innermost essence of ontological knowledge. A question about a chapter's technical device thus turns out to be the question of what ontology is; and the answer — that ontological generality is not a higher genus but the horizon within which genera can be met — is the point at which the *Critique* stops being a treatise on cognition.

So it leaps to the eye: if Kant in the schematism chapter poses the problem of the conceptuality of the original concepts and solves it by determining these concepts in their essence as transcendental schemata, then the doctrine of the schematism of the pure concepts of the understanding is the decisive stage of the laying of the ground of *metaphysica generalis*.

Orientation by the idea of subsumption has its certain right as a first elucidation of the problem of the transcendental schematism. Kant may then also take from it a prefiguring of the possible solution and characterize the idea of the transcendental schematism provisionally from subsumption. If the pure concept of the understanding is entirely unhomogeneous with appearances and yet is to determine them, then there must be a mediating that bridges the unhomogeneity. "This mediating representation must be pure (without anything empirical) and yet on the one side intellectual, on the other sensible. Such is the transcendental schema" (A 138, B 177). "Hence an application of the category to appearances will be possible by means of the transcendental time-determination which, as the schema of the concepts of the understanding, mediates the subsumption of the latter under the former" (A 139, B 178).

So precisely in the nearest and outer shape of the schematism problem as a question of subsumption, the innermost significance of the transcendental schematism shows itself. There is not the slightest occasion to keep complaining, as is done, about a lack of unity and a confusedness in the schematism chapter. If anything in the *Critique of Pure Reason* is articulated with the utmost sharpness and measured in every word, it is this core piece. Because of its significance its articulation may be made explicit. Kant first introduces the schematism problem on the guiding thread of the inherited idea of subsumption (A 137, B 176 to A 140, B 179); then gives the preparatory analysis of the structure of the schema as such and of the schematism of empirical and mathematical concepts (to A 142, B 181); then the analysis of the transcendental schema in general (to A 142, B 182); then the interpretation of the individual transcendental schemata on the guiding thread of the table of categories (to A 145, B 184); then the characterization of the four classes of categories with a view to the corresponding four possibilities of a pure imageability of time (to A 145, B 186); then the determination of the transcendental schematism as the "true and only condition" of transcendence (to A 146, B 185); and finally the critical application of the essential determination of the categories grounded through the schematism, to the close of the section.

The schematism chapter is not "confused" but incomparably transparently built. It is not "confusing" but leads with unheard-of sureness into the core of the whole problematic of the *Critique of Pure Reason*. All of this becomes visible, of course, only if the finitude of transcendence is grasped as the ground of the inner possibility — and that here means: of a necessity — of metaphysics, so that the interpretation can gain a footing on this ground.

And yet Kant still writes in his last years, in 1797: "in general the schematism is one of the most difficult points. Even Herr Beck cannot find his way into it. — I hold this chapter to be one of the most important." The chapter's difficulty and its importance are, on Heidegger's reading, the same fact: it is difficult because what it treats is the hidden art by which anything is ever in view, and important for the same reason.

#### **Das fünfte Stadium der Grundlegung: Die volle Wesensbestimmung der ontologischen Erkenntnis**

In the previous stage the ground of the inner possibility of the ontological synthesis, and thereby the goal of the laying of the ground, was reached with the transcendental schematism. If a fifth stage is now attached, it can no longer carry the grounding further; it is to take explicit possession of the ground won, with a view to a possible building.

The stages passed through must here be appropriated in their unity — not by way of a subsequent adding-up but in the manner of an independent, full determination of the essence of ontological knowledge. Kant lays this decisive essential determination down in the "highest principle of all synthetic judgments." And if ontological knowledge is nothing other than the original forming of transcendence, then the highest principle must contain the most central essential determination of transcendence. On the ground so reached, the outlook onto the further tasks and consequences of Kant's laying of the ground of *metaphysica generalis* then opens.

##### **§ 24. Der oberste synthetische Grundsatz als die volle Wesensbestimmung der Transzendenz**

Kant introduces this central doctrine too in a critical stance toward traditional metaphysics, which wants to know beings "from mere concepts," that is, merely by thinking. The peculiar essence of mere thinking is delimited by general logic: mere thinking is the connecting of subject and predicate, judging. Such connecting only elucidates what is represented in the connected representations as such; it must be merely elucidating, "analytic," because it has "merely played with representations" (A 155, B 195). Mere thinking must, if it wants to be that, "remain" with the represented as such. Even in this constriction it has its own rules and principles, of which the highest counts as the "principle of contradiction." Mere thinking is not knowing at all but only an element — a necessary one — of finite knowledge. But from mere thinking, provided it is taken in advance as an element of finite knowledge, its necessary relation to something that first primarily determines full knowledge can be made visible. Kant's dialectic is thus disarmed at its source: school metaphysics fails not because it reasons badly but because thinking alone, however correct, is not yet knowledge.

If the predicate is to be an element of a knowledge, then what matters is not so much its relation to the subject — the apophantic-predicative synthesis — but the relation of the whole subject-predicate connection to "something quite other" (A 154, B 193 f.). This other is the being itself, with which knowing, and hence the judgment-relation belonging to it, is to be "in accord." Knowing must accordingly "go out beyond" that with which the mere thinking, constricted within itself, necessarily "remains." Kant calls this relation to the quite other synthesis — the veritative synthesis. Knowledge as such, insofar as it knows something quite other, is synthetic. And because the predicative-apophantic connection in mere thinking can also be called synthesis, the specific synthesis of knowledge was earlier distinguished as the supplying one.

But this going-out to the "quite other" demands a being-within a "medium" (A 155, B 194), within which this quite other — which the knowing being is not itself and of which it is not master — can encounter. And what makes this turning-toward, letting-encounter going-out possible and makes it up, Kant now circumscribes thus: "there is only one totality in which all our representations are contained, namely inner sense and its a priori form, time. The synthesis of representations rests on the imagination, but their synthetic unity, which is required for judgment, on the unity of apperception."

Here, then, the triad of elements introduced in the second stage at the first characterization of the essential unity of ontological knowledge returns explicitly. But the third and fourth stages showed how these three elements form a structural unity whose forming middle is the transcendental imagination; and what forms itself there is transcendence. When Kant now recalls this triad for the decisive illumination of transcendence, it may no longer be taken in the still dark enumeration in which it was introduced in the second stage, but must be fully present in the transparency of its structure, which revealed itself last in the transcendental schematism. And if this fifth stage merely gathers together, then the essential unity of transcendence, indicated in the second stage only as a problem, must be taken explicitly into possession as illuminated and clarified from the ground of its possibility.

Kant therefore brings the whole problem of the essence of finitude in knowledge onto the short formula of the "possibility of experience." Experience means finite, intuitively receptive knowledge of beings; beings must be given to knowledge as standing over against. But "possibility" has in the expression "possibility of experience" a characteristic ambiguity.

"Possible" experience can be meant in distinction from actual experience. But in the "possibility of experience," possible experience is as little the problem as actual experience; both are at issue with respect to what makes them possible in advance. "Possibility of experience" therefore means what makes possible a finite — that is, not necessarily but possibly actual — experience. This "possibility," which first makes the "possibly" possible, is the *possibilitas* of traditional metaphysics, synonymous with *essentia* or *realitas*. Real definitions are taken "from the essence of the thing, the first ground of possibility," and serve "for the knowledge of the thing according to its inner possibility." Kant's most famous phrase is thereby restored to its scholastic sense: conditions of the possibility of experience are not necessary presuppositions but the essence of experience.

"Possibility of experience" accordingly means primarily the single wholeness of what makes finite knowledge possible in its essence. "The possibility of experience is thus what gives objective reality to all our a priori cognitions" (A 156, B 195). Possibility of experience is therefore synonymous with transcendence, and to circumscribe transcendence in its full essential wholeness means to determine "the conditions of the possibility of experience."

"Experience," understood as experiencing in distinction from the experienced, is a receptive intuiting that must let beings be given to it. "To give an object" means to "present it immediately in intuition." And what does that mean? Kant answers: to relate the representation of the object "to experience, be it actual or possible." But this relating says that for an object to be able to give itself, a turning-toward what can be "called forth" must already have happened in advance. This prior turning-toward happens, as the transcendental deduction showed and the transcendental schematism explained, in the ontological synthesis; and this turning-toward is the condition of the possibility of experiencing.

But the possibility of finite knowledge needs a second condition. Only true knowledge is knowledge, and truth means "accord with the object" (A 157, B 196 f.). Something like a with-what of possible accord must therefore be able to encounter in advance — something that regulates in a standard-giving way. The horizon of what stands over against must be open in advance and perceivable as such. This horizon is the condition of the possibility of the object with respect to its being able to stand over against.

The possibility of finite knowledge — of the experiencing of the experienced as such — accordingly stands under two conditions, and these two must together delimit the full essence of transcendence. That delimitation is executable in a proposition stating the ground of the possibility of synthetic, that is, finitely knowing judgments, and holding as such in advance for all of them. Kant's concluding formulation of this "highest principle of all synthetic judgments" runs: "the conditions of the possibility of experience in general are at the same time conditions of the possibility of the objects of experience" (A 158, B 197).

The decisive content of this proposition lies not so much in what Kant italicized as in the "are at the same time." For what does this being-at-the-same-time mean? It brings to expression the essential unity of the full structure of transcendence. That unity lies in this: the turning-toward letting-stand-over-against, as such, forms the horizon of objectivity as such. The going-out-to that is necessary in advance and at all times in finite knowing is accordingly a constant standing-out-to, an ecstasis. But this essential standing-out precisely forms, in the standing, and holds before itself, a horizon. Transcendence is in itself ecstatic-horizonal, and it is this articulation of the in-itself single transcendence that the highest principle expresses. The vocabulary of *Sein und Zeit* enters undisguised: ecstasis and horizon are the terms of the analysis of temporality in Division Two, and Heidegger is claiming that Kant's highest principle already states the ecstatic-horizonal structure that *Sein und Zeit* worked out from Dasein.

Hence the principle can also be put briefly: what makes an experiencing possible makes possible at the same time the experienceable, or the experienced, as such. Which says: transcendence makes beings accessible at themselves to a finite being. The "being at the same time" in the formula of the highest synthetic principle means neither merely that both conditions always occur together, nor that in thinking one the other must be thought too, nor even that both conditions are identical. The principle is not a principle won by inference, one that must be posited as valid if experience is to be valid; it is the expression of the most original phenomenological knowledge of the innermost unitary structure of transcendence — the structure that was worked out in the presented stages of the essential projection of the ontological synthesis. Heidegger's own footnote adds that the principle, so read, is at the same time the rightly understood metaphysical principle of ground, and refers to *Vom Wesen des Grundes*, published the same year in the Husserl *Festschrift*.

##### **§ 25. Die Transzendenz und die Grundlegung der Metaphysica generalis**

The unveiling of the ground of the inner possibility of the essence of the ontological synthesis was determined as the task of the laying of the ground of *metaphysica generalis*. Ontological knowledge has proved to be what forms transcendence. Insight into the full structure of transcendence therefore makes it possible only now to survey the whole peculiarity of ontological knowledge — its knowing as well as its known.

The knowing must, as finite, be a receptive thinking intuiting of what gives itself, and a pure one; it is a pure schematism. The pure unity of the three elements of pure knowledge comes to expression in the concept of the transcendental schema as "transcendental time-determination."

If ontological knowing is schema-forming, it thereby creates — forms — the pure look, the image, from itself. Is not then precisely ontological knowledge, which happens in the transcendental imagination, "creative"? And if ontological knowing forms transcendence, while transcendence makes up the essence of finitude, is finitude not burst by this "creative" character? Does the finite being not become infinite through such comportment?

But is ontological knowledge "creative" in the way the *intuitus originarius* is, for which in intuiting the being is in and as its arising and can never become an object at all? Are beings "known," that is, created as such, in this "creative" ontological knowledge? Not in the least. Ontological knowledge not only does not create beings; it does not relate thematically and directly to beings at all. The objection is answered by restricting what the creating creates: what is produced is not a being and not even a representation of a being, but the openness in which beings can show up.

To what, then? What is the known of this knowing? A nothing. Kant calls it the X and speaks of an "object." How far is this X a nothing and how far nevertheless a "something"? The answer is given through a short interpretation of the two main passages where Kant speaks of this X. The first stands, characteristically, in the introduction to the transcendental deduction; the second in the section headed "On the ground of the distinction of all objects in general into phenomena and noumena," which within the structure of the *Critique* closes the positive laying of the ground of *metaphysica generalis*.

The first passage: "now we can also determine our concepts of an object in general more correctly. All representations, as representations, have their object, and can themselves in turn be objects of other representations. Appearances are the only objects that can be given to us immediately, and that in them which relates immediately to the object is called intuition. But these appearances are not things in themselves, but themselves only representations, which in turn have their object, which therefore can no longer be intuited by us and may hence be called the non-empirical, that is, transcendental object = X" (A 108 f.).

What stands over against immediately in appearance is what intuition gives. But appearances are themselves "only representations," not things in themselves; what is represented in them shows itself only in and for a receptive turning-toward. And this turning-toward must itself "in turn have its object" — indeed it must give in advance something having the character of a standing-over-against as such, in order to form the horizon within which independent beings can encounter. This toward-which of the prior turning-toward can therefore no longer be intuited by us in the sense of empirical intuition. But that does not exclude, it includes, the necessity of its immediate perceivability in a pure intuition. Hence this toward-which can be called "the non-empirical object = X." The notorious X is thus neither the thing in itself nor an unknown substrate: it is the horizon itself, and its unintuitability is empirical only, since as time it is precisely what pure intuition gives.

The second passage: "all our representations are in fact related by the understanding to some object, and since appearances are nothing but representations, the understanding relates them to a something as the object of sensible intuition; but this something is, so far, only the transcendental object. This signifies, however, a something = X of which we know nothing at all, nor can know anything in general (according to the present constitution of our understanding), but which can serve only as a correlate of the unity of apperception for the unity of the manifold in sensible intuition, by means of which the understanding unites that manifold in the concept of an object" (A 250).

The X is a "something" of which we can know nothing at all. But it is not unknowable because it lies hidden as a being "behind" a layer of appearances; it is unknowable because it can simply never become a possible object of a knowing, of the possession of a knowledge of beings. And it can never become one because it is a nothing.

Nothing means here: not a being, and yet still "something." It "serves only as a correlate," that is, it is by its essence pure horizon. Kant calls this X the "transcendental object," the against that becomes visible in and through transcendence as transcendence's horizon. But if the X known in ontological knowledge is by its essence a horizon, then this knowing must also be such that it holds the horizon open in its horizon-character. And then this something may precisely not stand in the theme of a grasping as what is directly and solely meant. The horizon must be unthematic and yet in view; only so can it press what encounters within it forward into the theme.

The X is "object in general." That does not mean a universal, indeterminate being standing over against us. The expression means what makes up in advance the projection across all possible objects as standing over against — the horizon of a standing-over-against. This horizon is of course not an object but a nothing, if object means as much as a thematically grasped being; and ontological knowledge is not a knowledge, if knowledge means the grasping of beings. Two words are being withdrawn from their ordinary use at once, and the withdrawal is what the rest of the § justifies: the "nothing" and the "knowledge" of ontological knowledge are both said in a sense the tradition has no place for.

But ontological knowledge is rightly called knowledge if truth belongs to it. And it does not merely "have" truth; it is the original truth, which Kant therefore calls "transcendental truth," and whose essence is illuminated by the transcendental schematism. "But in the whole of all possible experience lie all our cognitions, and transcendental truth, which precedes all empirical truth and makes it possible, consists in the universal relation to this whole" (A 146, B 185).

Ontological knowledge "forms" transcendence, and this forming is nothing other than the holding-open of the horizon in which the being of beings becomes visible in advance. If truth means unconcealedness of..., then transcendence is the original truth. But truth itself must fork into the unveiledness of being and the manifestness of beings. This is the single passage in the whole book where the Greek doctrine of truth and the Kantian idiom are welded together in one sentence, and it is the bridge back to *Sein und Zeit* §44: what §44 called disclosedness of being and uncoveredness of beings reappears here as unveiledness (*Enthülltheit*) and manifestness (*Offenbarkeit*), with the word *Dasein* withheld and *Unverborgenheit* named only in a conditional clause. Anyone tracing the continuity of Heidegger's doctrine of truth across 1927 and 1929 should cite this sentence and not look for the ἀλήθεια derivation here, which the book never gives. If ontological knowledge unveils the horizon, then its truth lies precisely in letting beings be encountered within the horizon. Kant says that ontological knowledge has only "empirical use" — it serves to make finite knowledge, in the sense of experience of the self-showing being, possible.

It must therefore at the least remain open whether this knowledge, always only ontologically and never ontically "creative," bursts the finitude of transcendence — or whether it does not precisely sink the finite "subject" into its proper finitude. The question is left open here and answered in the Fourth Section, where the neediness of the finite being for an understanding of being becomes the deepest mark of its finitude rather than an escape from it.

On this essential determination, ontology is nothing other than the explicit unveiling of the systematic whole of pure knowledge insofar as it forms transcendence.

Kant nevertheless wants to replace "the proud name of an ontology" (A 247, B 303) with that of a "transcendental philosophy," an essential unveiling of transcendence. And rightly so, as long as the title "ontology" is taken in the sense of traditional metaphysics, which "presumes to give synthetic a priori cognitions of things in general" and thereby overreaches into an a priori *ontic* knowledge that can belong only to an infinite being. But if this ontology lays aside its presumption and its pride — if it grasps itself in its finitude, or grasps itself as the necessary essential structure of finitude — then the expression "ontology" is first given its genuine essence and its use justified. And Kant himself uses the term in this meaning, won and secured only through the laying of the ground of metaphysics, at the decisive place of the *Critique* that lays out the outline of metaphysics as a whole (A 845, B 873). Kant's rejection of the word is thus accepted and reversed in the same breath: what he rejects is the word's pretension, and the *Kantbuch* claims to have removed the pretension and kept the word.

With the transformation of *metaphysica generalis*, however, the building-ground of traditional metaphysics comes into motion, and thereby the proper building of *metaphysica specialis* begins to totter. This further problematic is not to be touched here; it needs a preparation obtainable only through a more original appropriation of what Kant achieved, in the unity of transcendental aesthetic and logic, as a laying of the ground of *metaphysica generalis*. The Second Section thus closes by naming the demolition it has caused and declining to inspect it: God, world and soul have lost their ground, and Heidegger passes over the Dialectic entirely to press instead into the origin of the ground just laid.

## **Dritter Abschnitt: Die Grundlegung der Metaphysik in ihrer Ursprünglichkeit**

Can the laying of the ground now reached be grasped still more originally at all? Is this incessant pressing toward originality not empty curiosity, punished by the wretchedness in which every wish to know better has its fatal distinction? And above all: does it not force Kant's philosophizing before a standard that remains foreign to it, so that everything ends in a critique "from outside" that is unjust every time?

The question of the originality of Kant's laying of the ground will not want to set out on that slippery path from the start. If the discussion of originality is not to become critique in the sense of polemic but still to remain interpretation, then the idea of originality guiding it must be taken from Kant's laying of the ground itself. Kant's entering into the dimension of origin, and thereby his striving toward the source-ground of the "basic sources of knowledge," must be questioned in respect of the fore-look guiding it. But before that can happen, what the ground laid in the grounding first and last is must be clearly delimited. The rule Heidegger imposes on himself is the only defence he offers against the charge of violence: the standard of originality is Kant's own, since Kant himself named a root he did not pursue.

### **A. Die ausdrückliche Charakteristik des in der Grundlegung gelegten Grundes**

#### **§ 26. Die bildende Mitte der ontologischen Erkenntnis als transzendentale Einbildungskraft**

The laying of the ground of *metaphysica generalis* is the answer to the question of the essential unity of ontological knowledge and of the ground of its possibility. Ontological knowledge "forms" transcendence, the holding-open of the horizon that is visible in advance through the pure schemata; and these "spring" as a "transcendental product" of the transcendental imagination, which as original pure synthesis forms the essential unity of pure intuition (time) and pure thinking (apperception).

But the transcendental imagination became a central theme not first in the doctrine of the transcendental schematism; it did so already in the preceding stage, in the transcendental deduction. And because it is to take over the original unification, it had to be named already at the first characterization of the essential unity of ontological knowledge in the second stage. The transcendental imagination is accordingly the ground on which the inner possibility of ontological knowledge, and with it that of *metaphysica generalis*, is built.

Kant introduces the pure imagination as an "indispensable function of the soul." To lay free the laid ground of metaphysics explicitly therefore means to determine more closely a faculty of the human soul. That the laying of the ground of metaphysics finally arrives at such a task is "self-evident," if metaphysics belongs, in Kant's own words, to the "nature of the human being." So the "Anthropology," which Kant treated for many years in his lectures, must give information about the laid ground of metaphysics. The move is made in order to be refuted; and the refutation is what secures the Fourth Section in advance, since if the anthropology could deliver the ground, a philosophical anthropology would after all be fundamental ontology.

"The imagination (*facultas imaginandi*) is a faculty of intuitions even without the presence of the object." The imagination belongs to the faculty of intuition, and intuition is here first understood as empirical intuition of beings. As a "sensible faculty" the imagination falls under the cognitive faculties divided into sensibility and understanding, of which the first presents the "lower." The imagination is a way of sensible intuiting "even without the presence of the object": the intuited being need not itself be present, and more than that, imagining does not intuit what it receives as something actually present, and only as that, as perception does, for which the object "must be represented as present."

The imagination "can" intuit, can receive a look, without what is intuited showing itself as a being and providing the look from itself alone. So there lies in the imagination a peculiar unboundness to beings: it ranges freely in receiving looks, that is, it is the faculty of giving them to itself in a certain way. The imagination can therefore be called a faculty of forming in a peculiar double sense. As a faculty of intuiting it is forming in the sense of procuring an image, a look; as a faculty not dependent on the presence of the intuitable it itself performs, creates and forms, the image. This forming power is at once a receptive and a creating, spontaneous forming, and in this "at once" lies the proper essence of its structure. But if receptivity means as much as sensibility and spontaneity as much as understanding, then the imagination falls in a peculiar way between the two — as *phantasia* already stands between *aisthesis* and *noesis* in Aristotle's *De anima* Γ 3. This gives it a curiously shimmering character, which comes out in Kant's own determinations: in dividing the cognitive faculties into their two basic classes he counts it, despite its spontaneity, to sensibility.

On the ground of its free range, however, it is for Kant a faculty of comparing, shaping, combining, distinguishing, in general of connecting — synthesis. "Imagining" thus means all non-perceptual representing in the widest sense: thinking to oneself, devising, inventing, having ideas. The forming power is accordingly grouped with the faculty of wit and the power of distinguishing, with the faculty of comparison in general. The senses give the matter for all our representations; from it the faculty of forming representations independently of the presence of objects makes *imaginatio*, the faculty of comparing makes wit and the power of discrimination, and the faculty of connecting representations with their object not immediately but by means of a substitute makes designation.

Yet for all this assignment of the imagination to the faculty of spontaneity, it keeps its intuition-character. It is *subjectio sub aspectum*, a faculty of intuitive presentation, of giving. And intuitive representing of a non-present object can be of two kinds.

If it restricts itself to bringing back in mere making-present what was earlier perceived, then this look is in itself dependent on the earlier one the former perception offered. Such presentation, reaching back to what was earlier, derives its content from there: *exhibitio derivativa*.

But if in imagining the look of an object is freely invented, then this presentation of its look is an "original" one, *exhibitio originaria*, and the imagination is then called "productive." This original presenting is nevertheless not "creative" in the way the *intuitus originarius* is, which in intuiting creates the being itself. The productive imagination forms only the look of a possible object, one perhaps producible under certain conditions, that is, capable of being brought to presence; but this production the imagining itself never accomplishes. The productive forming of the imagination is not even "creative" in the sense that it could form the image-content simply out of nothing, out of what was never and nowhere experienced, since it is "not capable of producing a sense-representation that was never given to our sense-faculty before, but one can always point out the material for it."

That is the essential of what the Anthropology has to tell us about the imagination in general and the productive imagination in particular. It contains no more than the laying of the ground in the *Critique of Pure Reason* has already exhibited. On the contrary: that the imagination is an in-between faculty between sensibility and understanding was brought to light incomparably more originally by the discussions of the transcendental deduction and the schematism.

But the definition that the imagination can represent an object intuitively without its presence was at least not found in the grounding considerations of the *Critique*. Apart from the fact that this definition does occur expressly in the transcendental deduction, though only in the second edition (B 151), has not the working-out of the transcendental schematism shown precisely the character named in that definition?

The imagination forms in advance the look of the horizon of objectivity as such, before the experience of beings. And this forming of a look in the pure image of time is not only before this or that experience of beings but in advance and at all times before every possible one. In this offering of a look the imagination is from the outset and absolutely never dependent on the presence of a being. So little is it dependent that its pre-forming of the pure schema — of substance, that is, of persistence — first brings into view anything like constant presence, within whose horizon this or that "presence of an object" can show itself as such at all. In the transcendental schematism, then, the imagination's essence of being able to intuit without presence is grasped fundamentally more originally. And the schematism also shows the "creative" essence of the imagination in a far more original sense: ontically creative it is not at all, but it is creative as a free forming of images. The Anthropology points out that even the productive imagination is still dependent on sense-representations; in the transcendental schematism, by contrast, the imagination is originally presenting in the pure image of time and is absolutely in no need of an empirical intuition. The *Critique* thus shows both the intuition-character and the spontaneity in a more original sense. Kant's own psychology is beaten on both counts by his transcendental philosophy — which is why the ground of metaphysics cannot be read off a doctrine of faculties.

The attempt to learn something more original about the imagination as the laid ground of ontology from the Anthropology therefore remains fruitless in every way. Not only that: it is altogether a false move, because it misjudges the empirical character of Kant's anthropology and, on the other side, fails to take account of the peculiarity of the grounding-consideration and the unveiling of origin in the *Critique*.

Kant's anthropology is empirical in a double sense. First, the characterization of the soul's faculties moves within the knowledge that general experience of the human being offers. Second, the faculties themselves — the imagination, say — are considered in advance and solely with respect to the fact that, and the way in which, they relate to experienceable beings. The productive imagination the Anthropology treats always concerns only the forming of looks of empirically possible or impossible objects.

The productive imagination of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, by contrast, never relates to the forming of objects but to the pure look of objectivity as such. It is experience-free pure productive imagination, which first makes experience possible. Not every productive imagination is pure; but the pure imagination is, in the sense characterized, necessarily productive. And insofar as it forms transcendence it is rightly called the transcendental imagination.

The Anthropology does not pose the question of transcendence at all. Nevertheless the failed attempt to interpret the imagination more originally by its means has yielded that in the empirical interpretation of the soul's faculties — which at bottom can itself never be purely empirical — there always already lies a pointer to transcendental structures. But these can neither be grounded in the anthropology nor drawn from it by mere taking-over. The concession is precise and will matter later: an anthropology cannot help containing transcendental structures, since its object is a being that understands being — which is exactly why it cannot found what it presupposes.

What kind of knowing, then, is it that carries out the unveiling of transcendence, the laying free of the pure synthesis, and thereby the interpretation of the imagination? When Kant calls this kind of knowledge "transcendental," one can gather from that only that it has transcendence as its theme. But what is its methodological character? How does the regress into the origin happen? So long as the required clarity about this is lacking, no more original step in the laying of the ground can be carried out.

An explicit discussion of "transcendental method" seems no longer avoidable at this point. Yet supposing this method were clarified, the task would still remain of reading out of the ground so far laid the direction of the regress that the dimension of origin itself demands. And whether this swinging into the direction prefigured by the matters themselves succeeds depends solely on whether Kant's grounding, or the interpretation of it, is original and rich enough to take the lead in such a swing. Only an attempt actually carried out can decide that. The way into Kant's anthropology, which at first seemed self-evident, has shown itself to be a wrong turn; and all the more clearly follows the necessity of holding the further interpretation unwaveringly to the phenomenon that revealed itself as the ground of the inner possibility of the ontological synthesis — the transcendental imagination. The methodological question is thus raised and set aside in the same breath: a discussion of transcendental method would itself presuppose the ground being sought, so method must be earned by execution.

#### **§ 27. Die transzendentale Einbildungskraft als drittes Grundvermögen**

To understand the faculties of "our mind" as "transcendental faculties" means first to unveil them with respect to how they make the essence of transcendence possible. Faculty here does not have the meaning of a "basic power" present in the soul; "faculty" now means what such a phenomenon *is capable of*, in the sense of enabling the essential structure of ontological transcendence. Faculty now means as much as "possibility" in the sense laid out at §24. So understood, the transcendental imagination is not merely and not first a faculty occurring between pure intuition and pure thinking; it is, together with these, a "basic faculty," as the enabling of the original unity of both and thereby of the essential unity of transcendence as a whole. "We therefore have a pure imagination as a basic faculty of the human soul, which grounds all knowledge a priori" (A 124).

"Basic faculty" says at the same time that the pure imagination is not reducible to the pure elements with which together it forms the essential unity of transcendence. Hence Kant, at the decisive characterization of the essential unity of ontological knowledge, expressly enumerates three elements: pure intuition (time), the pure synthesis through the imagination, and the pure concepts of pure apperception. In the same connection Kant stresses that "we shall see hereafter" in what way the imagination works, "an indispensable function of the soul, without which we would have no knowledge at all."

This triad is discussed in the transcendental deduction with respect to its possible unity and grounded through the schematism; the schematism moreover brings, in introducing the idea of the pure schematism, the same enumeration of the three pure elements of ontological knowledge; and finally the discussion of the highest principle of all synthetic judgments — the definitive determination of the full essence of transcendence — is introduced with the enumeration of these three elements as "the three sources" of the possibility of pure synthetic judgments a priori. Four times at the decisive junctures the count is three; the accumulation is the whole evidence, and it is textual rather than interpretive.

Against this unambiguous characterization of the transcendental imagination as a third basic faculty alongside pure sensibility and pure understanding — a characterization growing out of the inner problematic of the *Critique* itself — stands the declaration Kant gives expressly at the beginning and at the end of his work. There are only "two basic sources of the mind, sensibility and understanding"; there are only these "two stems of our cognitive power"; besides these two sources of knowledge we have "no other." And the division of the whole transcendental investigation into a transcendental aesthetic and a transcendental logic corresponds to this thesis.

The transcendental imagination is homeless. It is not treated in the transcendental aesthetic, where as a "faculty of intuition" it properly belongs; instead it is a theme in the transcendental logic, where, so long as logic remains with thinking as such, it strictly ought not to be. But because this aesthetic and this logic are from the outset oriented toward transcendence, which is not merely the sum of pure intuition and pure thinking but an original unity of its own within which these function only as elements, the result of both must lead beyond them. Homelessness in the architectonic is thus itself the evidence: a faculty that fits into neither discipline is a faculty the two-stem doctrine cannot accommodate.

Could this result escape Kant? Or is it in the least compatible with his manner of thinking that he suppressed the triad of basic faculties in favour of the theory of the duality of stems? So little is that the case that Kant, in the very course of his laying of the ground — both at the close of the introduction to the transcendental deduction and at the beginning of its actual execution — speaks expressly of "three original sources of the soul," as though he had never fixed the duality of stems at all.

"There are, however, three original sources (capacities or faculties of the soul) that contain the conditions of the possibility of all experience and cannot themselves be derived from any other faculty of the mind, namely sense, imagination and apperception... All these faculties have, besides their empirical use, also a transcendental one, which is directed solely to form and is possible a priori" (A 94). And: "there are three subjective sources of knowledge on which the possibility of an experience in general and of the knowledge of its objects rests: sense, imagination and apperception; each of these can be considered as empirical, namely in application to given appearances, but all are also elements or grounds a priori that first make this empirical use possible" (A 115). At both places the transcendental use of these faculties is expressly emphasized alongside the empirical, which again announces the relation to the anthropology.

So the triad of basic faculties and the duality of basic sources and stems of knowledge stand hard against each other. But what is it about the two stems? Does Kant use this image by chance for the characterization of sensibility and understanding, or precisely in order to indicate that they grow out of a "common root"?

The interpretation of the laying of the ground has shown that the transcendental imagination is not merely an external band knotting two ends together. It is originally unifying: as a faculty of its own it forms the unity of the two others, which themselves have an essential structural relation to it.

What if this originally forming middle were that "unknown common root" of the two stems? Is it a chance that Kant, at the first introduction of the imagination, says of it that "we are seldom even once conscious" of it? The book's central thesis is put as a question, twice, and never as an assertion; and Heidegger's footnote is equally careful, granting that Fichte, Schelling and Jacobi assigned the imagination an essential role while declining to say whether they grasped what Kant saw, and stating that the interpretation here "moves in the opposite direction" from German idealism. The opposition is exact: idealism took the imagination as the productive absolute, whereas here it is the root of *finitude*.

### **B. Die transzendentale Einbildungskraft als Wurzel der beiden Stämme**

If the laid ground is not anything like a present soil but has the character of a root, then it must be ground in such a way that it lets the stems grow out of itself and lends them hold and stability. And with that the sought direction is already won in which the originality of Kant's laying of the ground can be discussed within its own problematic. The grounding becomes more original if it does not simply accept the laid ground but unveils how this root is root *for the two stems*. Which means nothing less than leading pure intuition and pure thinking back to the transcendental imagination.

But does the questionableness of such an undertaking not leap to the eye, quite apart from its success? Is not all knowledge degraded to mere imagining by such a reduction of the cognitive faculties of the finite being to the imagination? Does the essence of the human being not dissolve into a semblance?

If, however, the origin of pure intuition and pure thinking as transcendental faculties is to be shown out of the transcendental imagination as a faculty, that does not mean wanting to demonstrate that pure intuition and pure thinking are a product of imagining and as such only something imagined. The unveiling of origin means rather that the structure of these faculties is rooted in the structure of the transcendental imagination, and rooted in such a way that the imagination can "imagine" anything only in structural unity with the two of them.

Whether what is formed in the transcendental imagination is a mere semblance in the sense of "mere imagination" must at least be left open. What counts as "merely imagined" is at first what is not actually present. But what is formed in the transcendental imagination is by its essence not something present at all, if the transcendental imagination can never be ontically creative; so what is formed in it can essentially never be "mere imagination" in that sense. On the contrary, the horizon of objects formed in the transcendental imagination — the understanding of being — first makes anything like a distinction between ontic truth and ontic semblance possible at all. The objection is turned around: imagination cannot be accused of producing illusion, since it produces the standard by which illusion is distinguished from truth.

But does not ontological knowledge, whose essential ground the transcendental imagination is supposed to be, have, as essentially finite, along with its truth also a corresponding untruth? Indeed: the idea of *transcendental untruth* harbours one of the most central problems of finitude — a problem that is not only unsolved but not even posed, because the basis for posing it must first be worked out. And that can happen only when the essence of finite transcendence, and thereby of the transcendental imagination, is sufficiently unveiled. But pure intuition and pure thinking are never declared something imagined by having their essential possibility led back to the essential structure of the transcendental imagination. The transcendental imagination does not imagine something like pure intuition; it makes pure intuition possible in what it can "actually" be. The phrase *transzendentale Unwahrheit* is Heidegger's own, not Kant's, and the sentence is one of the two places in the book where it appears. It is the point at which the doctrine of truth of *Sein und Zeit* §44 — that untruth belongs to Dasein's constitution as originally as truth — is transposed into the Kantian idiom and then explicitly declared unposed. Whoever cites GA 3 on truth should cite this admission alongside §25's formula.

But as little as the transcendental imagination is itself something merely imagined because it "forms" as root, so little can it be thought as a "basic power" in the soul. Nothing lies further from this regress into the essential origin of transcendence than a monistic-empirical explanation of the remaining faculties of the soul out of the imagination. That intention forbids itself of its own accord, since in the end the essential unveiling of transcendence first decides in what sense one may speak of "soul" and "mind" at all, and how far these concepts originally hit the ontological-metaphysical essence of the human being.

The regress to the transcendental imagination as the root of sensibility and understanding means rather this alone: to project the constitution of transcendence anew onto the ground of its possibility, with a view to the essential structure of the transcendental imagination won within the grounding problematic. The ground-laying regress moves into the dimension of "possibilities," of possible enablings. And in that lies above all that in the end what has so far been known as transcendental imagination dissolves into more original "possibilities," so that the designation "imagination" becomes of itself inappropriate. The term is announced in advance as provisional — which answers the standard objection that Heidegger makes a faculty of fancy into the ground of being. What is being named is not a faculty at all, and by §34 the name will have given way to time.

The further unveiling of the originality of the laying of the ground will therefore still less than the stages already presented want to lead to an absolute basis of explanation. The strangeness of the laid ground, which must have pressed itself on Kant, cannot vanish; it will increase with growing originality, if for the human being as a finite being his metaphysical nature is at once the most unknown and the most actual.

If the transcendental imagination can be demonstrated as the root of transcendence, then the problematic of the transcendental deduction and of the schematism first gains its transparency. The question posed there about pure synthesis aims at an original unification in which the unifying must be equal in advance to the elements to be unified. But such a forming of an original unity is possible only if the unifying lets what is to be unified spring forth by its own essence. The root-character of the laid ground therefore first makes the originality of pure synthesis, its letting-spring-forth, intelligible.

In the interpretation that follows the orientation to the traversed path of the grounding is retained, but the individual stages are no longer described; and the specific connection of pure imagination, pure intuition and pure thinking is to be unveiled only so far as Kant's laying of the ground itself contains indications for it.

#### **§ 28. Die transzendentale Einbildungskraft und die reine Anschauung**

Kant calls the pure intuitions space and time "original representations." The "original" here is not understood ontically-psychologically and does not concern presence, the innateness of these intuitions in the soul; it characterizes the way in which these representations represent. The expression corresponds to the *originarius* in the title *intuitus originarius* and means: letting-spring-forth. Of course the pure intuitions, belonging as they do to the finitude of the human being, cannot let a being spring forth in their representing.

And yet they are forming in the peculiar way that they re-present in advance the look of space and time as wholes manifold in themselves. They receive the look, but this receiving is in itself precisely the forming self-giving of what gives itself. The pure intuitions are by their essence an "original," that is, a letting-spring-forth presentation of the intuitable: *exhibitio originaria*. But in this presenting lies the essence of the pure imagination. Pure intuition can be "original" only because it is itself by its essence pure imagination, which formingly gives looks — images — from itself.

The rootedness of pure intuition in the pure imagination becomes fully clear when we ask after the character of what is intuited in pure intuition. Interpreters mostly deny too coarsely and too quickly that anything at all is intuited in pure intuition, since it is after all only "form of intuition." What is glimpsed in pure intuition is a whole single in itself, though not empty, whose parts are always only limitations of itself. And this single whole must let itself be glimpsed in advance precisely with respect to this togetherness of its mostly unset-off manifold. Pure intuition must glimpse the unity in an originally unifying, that is, unity-giving way. Kant therefore rightly speaks here not of a synthesis but of a "synopsis" (A 94 f.), and says expressly that it was transcendental synopsis he treated in the transcendental aesthetic.

The wholeness of what is intuited in pure intuition does not have the unity of the universality of a concept, and the unity of this intuitional wholeness therefore cannot spring from the "synthesis of the understanding" either. It is a unity glimpsed in advance in image-giving imagining. The "syn" of the wholeness of space and time belongs to a faculty of forming intuition. If pure synopsis makes up the essence of pure intuition, it is possible only in the transcendental imagination — all the more as this is the origin of everything "synthetic" at all. "Synthesis" must here be taken widely enough to embrace both the synopsis of intuition and the "synthesis" of the understanding.

Kant says once, plastically and immediately, in a *Reflexion*: "space and time are the forms of pre-forming in intuition." Heidegger defends this reading against Adickes, who would read "connection" for "pre-forming." In advance they form the pure look that serves as the horizon of the empirically intuitable. But if pure intuition, in the manner of its intuiting, announces the specific essence of the transcendental imagination, is not what is pre-formed in it, as something formed in the imagination (*imaginatio*), itself imaginative? This characterization of what is intuited in pure intuition is no formal consequence of the preceding analysis but lies enclosed in the essential content of what is accessible in pure intuition. The imaginative character of space and time then has nothing unheard-of or strange about it, provided one holds fast that pure intuition and pure imagination are at issue: what is formed in imagining is, as shown, not necessarily an ontic semblance.

Kant would have seen little of the essential structure of pure intuition, indeed could not have grasped it at all, if the imaginative character of what is intuited in it had not become visible to him. Unambiguously he says: "the mere form of intuition, without substance, is in itself no object but the merely formal condition of one (as appearance), like pure space and pure time, which are indeed something as forms for intuiting but are not themselves objects that are intuited (*ens imaginarium*)" (A 291, B 347). What is intuited in pure intuition as such is an *ens imaginarium*. So pure intuiting is in the ground of its essence pure imagination. Kant's own parenthesis, tucked into the table of divisions of nothing, is the textual warrant for the thesis; and it is the strongest single piece of evidence in the Third Section.

The *ens imaginarium* belongs among the possible forms of "nothing," of what is not a being in the sense of the present. Pure space and pure time are "something," to be sure no "objects." If one says curtly that in pure intuition "nothing" is intuited and that it therefore lacks objects, this interpretation is at first only negative and moreover ambiguous, so long as it is not made clear that Kant uses "object" here in the determinately restricted meaning in which the being itself showing itself in appearance is meant. On that usage, not every arbitrary "something" is an object. The pure intuitions are, as "forms for intuiting," indeed "intuitions without things," but they nevertheless have their intuited. Space is nothing actual, no being accessible in perception, but "the representation of a mere possibility of being-together" (A 374).

The tendency to deny pure intuition an object in the sense of something intuited is reinforced in particular by the possibility of appealing to a genuine phenomenal character of pure intuition, without however determining that character sufficiently. In knowing comportment toward present things ordered "spatio-temporally" we are directed only to those things — and yet space and time cannot be denied away. The positive question must therefore be: how are space and time *there* in that comportment? When Kant says they are intuitions, one wants to reply that precisely they are not intuited. Certainly, not intuited in the sense of thematic grasping, but in the manner of an originally forming giving. Precisely because what is purely intuited is what and how it is as something essentially to be formed — in the characterized double meaning of the pure look to be created — pure intuiting cannot intuit its "intuited" in the manner of a thematically grasping reception of something present.

Only the original interpretation of pure intuition as pure imagination therefore gives the possibility of illuminating positively what the intuited of pure intuition is. As the prior forming of a pure, unthematic and, in Kant's sense, non-objective look, pure intuition makes possible precisely that the empirical intuiting of spatio-temporal things, which moves within its horizon, does not first have to intuit space and time in the sense of a grasping that would first establish these manifolds.

Only through this interpretation of pure intuition is the transcendental character of transcendental intuition clarified, if the innermost essence of transcendence is grounded in the pure imagination. As the transcendental aesthetic stands at the beginning of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, it is at bottom unintelligible; it has only a preparatory character and can properly be read only from the perspective of the transcendental schematism.

As untenable as the attempt of the Marburg Kant interpretation is — to grasp space and time as "categories" in the logical sense and to dissolve the transcendental aesthetic into logic — so genuine is one motive that suggested that attempt: the insight, not clarified there, that the transcendental aesthetic taken by itself cannot be the whole that lies enclosed in it by possibility. But from the peculiar "syn"-character of pure intuition it does not follow that pure intuition belongs to the synthesis of the understanding; interpreting that "syn"-character leads rather to the origin of pure intuition out of the transcendental imagination. And the dissolution of the aesthetic into logic becomes still more questionable if it turns out that the specific object of transcendental logic, pure thinking, is rooted in the transcendental imagination as well. Cohen and Natorp are conceded their diagnosis and refused their remedy: the Aesthetic is indeed incomplete on its own, but what completes it lies below both disciplines rather than in one of them.

#### **§ 29. Die transzendentale Einbildungskraft und die theoretische Vernunft**

The attempt to demonstrate an origin of pure thinking, and thereby of theoretical reason in general, out of the transcendental imagination looks hopeless at first simply because such a project might be held self-contradictory. Kant says expressly that the imagination is "always sensible" (A 124). How is it, as an essentially sensible, that is, lower faculty, to form the origin for the higher and "upper" one? That in finite knowing the understanding presupposes sensibility and thereby imagination as a "substratum" is intelligible; but that it itself should by its essence spring from sensibility — this opinion cannot conceal an obvious absurdity.

Before any formalistic argument, however, it must be held fast that no empirically explanatory derivation of a higher faculty of the soul from a lower is at issue. Insofar as the grounding-consideration does not discuss faculties of the soul at all, the rank-order between "lower" and "upper" that grows out of such an attitude cannot be guiding — not even for an objection. And above all: what does "sensible" mean?

The essence of sensibility as Kant first determined it was deliberately delimited at §5. Sensibility means as much as finite intuition, and finitude consists in the receiving of what gives itself; what gives itself there and how it gives itself remains open. Not every sensible, that is, receptive intuition must already be a sensing, empirical one. The "lower" character of bodily conditioned sense-affection does not belong to the essence of sensibility. So the transcendental imagination as pure finite intuition not only can but, as a basic determination of finite transcendence, must be "sensible."

This sensibility of the transcendental imagination cannot, however, be claimed for assigning it to the class of the lower faculties of the soul — least of all if as transcendental it is supposed to be the condition of the possibility of all faculties. And with that the heaviest objection, because at first the most "natural" one, against a possible origin of pure thinking out of the transcendental imagination falls away.

Reason can now no longer be claimed as "higher." But another difficulty immediately announces itself. That pure intuition springs from the transcendental imagination as a faculty of intuition is still comprehensible. But that thinking, which is sharply distinguished from all intuition, should have its origin in the transcendental imagination appears impossible, even when no weight may any longer be placed on a rank-order of sensibility and understanding.

Yet thinking and intuiting, though distinguished, are not separated from one another like two entirely unhomogeneous things. Both belong, as kinds of representing, to the same genus of re-presenting as such; both are ways of representing of... The insight into the primary representational character of thinking is no less decisive for the interpretation that follows than the right understanding of the sensible character of the imagination.

In an original unveiling of the essence of the understanding its innermost essence must be brought into view: its dependence on intuition. This dependence is the being-understanding of the understanding. And this "being" is, what and how it is, in the pure synthesis of the pure imagination. To this one may reply: certainly the understanding is related "through" the pure imagination to pure intuition; but that does not for all the world mean that the pure understanding is itself transcendental imagination and nothing independent.

That it *is* something independent, moreover, logic attests, which has no need to treat of the imagination. And Kant precisely introduces the understanding always in the shape that the apparently "absolutely" available logic has determined for it. From this independence of thinking the analysis must surely start out, if the origin of thinking out of the imagination is to be exhibited.

That traditional logic does not treat of the pure imagination is indisputable. Whether logic has no need to treat of it at all, if it understands itself, must at least remain open. That Kant takes the starting point of his questions again and again from logic can likewise not be denied. But it is equally questionable whether logic's making thinking in a determinate sense its sole theme already guarantees that this logic can delimit, or even so much as hit, the full essence of thinking.

Does not Kant's own interpretation of pure thinking in the transcendental deduction and in the doctrine of the schematism show that not only the functions of judgment but also the pure concepts as notions present merely artificially isolated elements of the pure synthesis, which for its part is an essentially necessary "presupposition" for the "synthetic unity of apperception"? Does not Kant precisely dissolve the formal logic by which he constantly orients himself, as by an absolute, into what he calls transcendental logic, which has the transcendental imagination as its central theme? Does the rejection of traditional logic not go so far that Kant must say — characteristically only in the second edition — "and thus the synthetic unity of apperception is the highest point to which one must attach all use of the understanding, even the whole of logic and, after it, transcendental philosophy; indeed this faculty *is* the understanding itself" (B 133 note)?

So the preconceptions about the independence of thinking, suggested by the factual existence of the apparently highest and underivable discipline of formal logic, may not become decisive for a judgment about the possibility of an origin of pure thinking out of the transcendental imagination. The essence of pure thinking must rather be sought in what the laying of the ground has itself already brought to light about it. Only from the original essence of the understanding, never from the "logic" that disregards this essence, can its possible origin be decided.

The characterization of thinking as judging is apt, but only a more remote determination of its essence. "Nearer" to that essence comes the characterization of thinking as the "faculty of rules" (A 126) — nearer because from there a way leads to the basic determination of the understanding as "pure apperception."

"Faculty of rules" means: representingly holding before oneself in advance the unities that give the lead to all possible representing unification. But these unities represented as regulating — notions, or categories — must not only be played into their own affinity; that affinity must itself be embraced in advance in an abiding unity by a still more anticipating re-presenting.

The representing of this abiding unity as the sameness of the rule-whole of affinity is the basic feature of the letting-stand-over-against. In such representing turning-toward, the "self" is as it were taken out into the turning-toward; and in such turning-toward, or in the "self" externalized with it, the "I" of this "self" is necessarily manifest. In this way the "I represent" "accompanies" all representing — not as a side-act of knowing directed at thinking itself: the "I" "goes along" in the pure turning-toward. And insofar as it itself is only what it is in this "I think," the essence of pure thinking as well as that of the I lies in "pure self-consciousness." But this "being-conscious" of the self can only be illuminated from the *being* of the self, not the reverse — and certainly not made superfluous by it. The reversal is the whole distance between Heidegger and every philosophy of consciousness: self-consciousness is founded in the self's manner of being, not the manner of being in the consciousness.

The "I think" is moreover always an "I think substance," "I think causality"; or "in" these pure unities it always already runs: "I think substance," "I think causality," and so on. The I is the "vehicle" of the categories, insofar as in its prior turning-toward it brings them to where they can unify as represented regulating unities.

The pure understanding is accordingly a representing pre-forming of the unity-horizon "from itself," a representingly forming spontaneity whose happening lies in the "transcendental schematism." Kant expressly calls this schematism "the procedure of the understanding with these schemata" and speaks of the "schematism of our understanding." But the pure schemata are "a transcendental product of the imagination." How is that to be reconciled? The understanding does not bring the schemata forth but "proceeds with them"; yet this proceeding is not an activity it also happens to perform. This pure schematism, grounded in the transcendental imagination, precisely makes up the original being-understanding, the "I think substance." The apparent own accomplishment of the pure understanding in thinking the unities is, as spontaneously forming representing, a pure basic act of the transcendental imagination — all the more as this representing turning-toward is not a thematic meaning of the unity but the unthematic holding-before-oneself of what is represented, which happens in a forming, producing representing.

When Kant calls this pure turning relating-to "our thought," then "thinking" in that thought does not mean judging; it is thinking in the sense of the freely forming and projecting, though not arbitrary, "thinking to oneself" of something. This original "thinking" is pure imagining.

The imagining-character of pure thinking becomes still clearer if we try, from the essential determination of the understanding now reached — pure self-consciousness — to come nearer to its essence and grasp it as reason. Here too the distinction of judging understanding and inferring reason, borrowed from formal logic, may not be decisive; what is decisive is what emerges in the transcendental interpretation of the understanding.

Kant calls the pure understanding a "closed unity." But whence does the projected whole of affinity take its wholeness? Insofar as the wholeness of a representing as such is at issue, what gives that wholeness must itself be a representing; and this happens in the forming of the Idea. Because the pure understanding is the "I think," it must in the ground of its essence have the character of a "faculty of ideas," that is, of a reason; for without reason we have "no coherent use of the understanding." Ideas "contain a certain completeness," they represent the "form of a whole" and are therefore rule-giving in a more original sense. The Dialectic is thereby drawn into the grounding: the ideas of reason are not merely the source of transcendental illusion but what gives the understanding its wholeness, and the Kantbuch needs them because a horizon must be a whole.

One might object that Kant, precisely in laying out the transcendental ideal, which "must serve as rule and archetype," says expressly that it is "quite otherwise with those creatures of the imagination which painters and physiognomists claim to have in their heads" (A 570, B 598) — that here the connection of ideas of pure reason with the imagination is expressly refused. But this passage says only that the transcendental ideal "must always rest on determinate concepts" and cannot be an arbitrary and "floating sketch" of the *empirical* productive imagination. That does not exclude that precisely those "determinate concepts" are possible only in the transcendental imagination.

One might go along with the interpretation of theoretical reason so far as it brings out the representing free forming in pure thinking. But if the interpretation wanted to infer from that an origin of pure thinking out of the transcendental imagination, it would have to be objected that spontaneity makes up only one moment of the imagination, so that thinking shows a kinship with it but never a full coincidence of essence. For the imagination is also and precisely a faculty of intuition, of receptivity — and it is this not merely in addition to its spontaneity: it is the original and not first composed unity of receptivity and spontaneity.

Now it was shown of pure intuition that on the ground of its purity it possesses the character of spontaneity, and that as pure spontaneous receptivity it has its essence in the transcendental imagination.

If pure thinking is of the same essence, then as spontaneity it must at the same time announce the character of a pure receptivity. But does Kant not throughout identify understanding and reason with spontaneity outright?

When Kant equates the understanding with spontaneity, that as little excludes a receptivity of the understanding as the equation of sensibility — finite intuition — with receptivity excluded a spontaneity belonging to it. In the end it is only the view to *empirical* intuition that justifies the emphatic and exclusive characterization as receptivity, and correspondingly the view to the "logical" function of the understanding within empirical knowledge that justifies the exclusive emphasis on its spontaneity and "function."

In the domain of pure knowledge, by contrast — within the problem of the possibility of transcendence — the pure, that is, the spontaneously self-giving receiving of what gives itself could not remain hidden. Must not a pure receptivity likewise show itself irresistibly in the transcendental interpretation of pure thinking, for all its spontaneity? Evidently; and it has long since shown itself in the interpretation of the transcendental deduction and the schematism.

To see the essential intuition-character of pure thinking, one need only grasp and hold fast the genuine essence of finite intuiting as a receiving of what gives itself. Now the basic character of the "unity" of transcendental apperception emerged as this: constantly unifying in advance, it is *against* all randomness. In the representing turning-toward, therefore, this "against" and nothing else is received. The freely forming projecting of affinity is in itself a representingly receptive subjecting-of-oneself to it. The rules represented in the understanding as the faculty of rules are not grasped as something present "in consciousness"; the rules of connecting are represented precisely as binding, in their bindingness. And if anything like a regulating rule is there only in a receptive letting-oneself-be-regulated, then the "Idea," as representation of the rules, can only represent in the manner of a receiving. Freedom and receptivity coincide rather than conflict: to be bound by a rule one gives oneself is to receive what one gives, and that structure is exactly the one §30 will find in respect.

In this sense pure thinking is in itself, not subsequently, receptive — that is, pure intuition. This structurally unitary receptive spontaneity must therefore spring from the transcendental imagination in order to be able to be what it is. The understanding as pure apperception has the "ground of its possibility" in a "faculty" that "looks out into an infinity of self-made representations and concepts." The transcendental imagination projects formingly in advance the whole of possibilities into which it "looks out," in order thereby to hold before itself the horizon within which the knowing self — and not only the knowing self — acts. Only for that reason can Kant say: "human reason is by its nature architectonic, that is, it considers all cognitions as belonging to a possible system."

The intuition-character belonging to pure thinking as such can be the less surprising if one considers that the pure intuitions time and space are just as "unintuitive" as the rightly understood categories, that is, their pure schema, so long as "intuitive" means only: perceivable through a sense-organ.

But the necessity that announces itself in the standing-over-against of the objective horizon is possible as an encountering "necessitation" only insofar as it meets in advance a being-free for it. In the essence of the pure understanding, of pure theoretical reason, freedom already lies, insofar as freedom means placing oneself under a self-given necessity. Understanding and reason are free not because they have the character of spontaneity, but because this spontaneity is a *receptive* spontaneity, that is, transcendental imagination.

Together with the reduction of pure intuition and pure thinking to the transcendental imagination, it should become evident that the transcendental imagination reveals itself more and more as a structural possibility, in its enabling of transcendence as the essence of the finite self. It thereby loses not only the character of an empirically gathered faculty of the soul; the restriction of its essence hitherto to being the root of the theoretical faculty as such also falls away. And so the last step in unveiling the "originality" of the laid ground must be ventured.

#### **§ 30. Die transzendentale Einbildungskraft und die praktische Vernunft**

Kant says already in the *Critique of Pure Reason*: "practical is everything that is possible through freedom" (A 800, B 828). But insofar as freedom belongs to the possibility of theoretical reason, theoretical reason is in itself, as theoretical, practical. And if finite reason as spontaneity is receptive and therefore springs from the transcendental imagination, then practical reason too is necessarily grounded in it. The origin of practical reason may nevertheless not be "inferred" by an argument, however well justified; it needs express unveiling through an illumination of the essence of the "practical self." A methodological scruple is doing real work here: the argument from theoretical reason to practical is available and is refused, because a structure of transcendence must be shown in the phenomenon rather than deduced.

On what was said about the "I" of pure apperception, the essence of the self lies in "self-consciousness." But as what, and how, the self is in this "consciousness" is determined from the being of the self, to which the manifestness of the self belongs; and this manifestness is only what it is insofar as it co-determines the being of the self. If the practical self is now questioned as to the ground of its possibility, what must first be delimited is that self-consciousness which makes this self possible as a self. With a view to this practical, that is, moral self-consciousness we must then investigate how far its essential structure points back to the transcendental imagination as its origin.

The moral I, the proper self and essence of the human being, Kant also calls the person. In what does the essence of the personality of the person consist? Personality itself is the "idea of the moral law" "with the respect inseparable from it." Respect is the "receptivity" for the moral law, that is, what makes possible a receiving of this law as a moral one. But if respect constitutes the essence of the person as the moral self, then on what has been said it must present a mode of self-consciousness. How far is it one?

Can it function as a mode of self-consciousness if by Kant's own designation it is a "feeling"? Feelings, states marked by pleasure and displeasure, belong after all to sensibility. But sensibility is not necessarily determined by bodily states, so that the possibility of a pure feeling — not determined by affection but "self-effected" — remains open. The general essence of feeling as such must therefore first be asked after; only the illumination of that essence lets it be decided how far "feeling" at all, and thus respect as a pure feeling, can present anything like a mode of self-consciousness. The same move that freed sensibility from sensation in §5 is now made for feeling, and with the same consequence: a pure feeling is not a contradiction, and Kant's moral philosophy therefore need not be read as a rationalism with an emotional appendix.

Already in the "lower" feelings of pleasure a peculiar basic structure shows itself. Pleasure is not only pleasure after something and in something but always at once a being-pleased, a way in which the human being experiences himself as pleased, in which he *is* pleased. So in every feeling, sensible in the narrower sense or not, there lies this articulated structure: feeling is a having-a-feeling-for... and as this at once a self-feeling of the one who feels. And the way in which the self-feeling makes the self manifest — lets it be — is always essentially co-determined by the character of that for which the one feeling has a feeling in feeling himself. How far does respect correspond to this essential structure of feeling, and why is it a pure feeling?

Kant gives the analysis of respect in the *Critique of Practical Reason*; the interpretation that follows lifts out only the essential.

Respect is as such respect for the moral law. It does not serve for judging actions and does not first set in after the moral deed, as the way in which we take a position on an action performed. Respect for the law rather first constitutes the possibility of the action: respect for... is the way in which the law first becomes accessible to us at all. In that lies at the same time that this feeling of respect does not serve, as Kant puts it, to "ground" the law. The law is not what it is because we have respect for it; conversely, this respectful having-a-feeling-for the law, and thus this determinate way of making the law manifest, is the way in which the law can come toward us as such at all.

Feeling is a having-a-feeling-for..., such that in it the feeling I at the same time feels itself. In respect for the law, therefore, the respecting I must at the same time become manifest to itself in a determinate way — and not subsequently and occasionally: respect for the law, this determinate way of making manifest the law as the determining ground of acting, is in itself a making-manifest of myself as the acting self. That before which respect is respect, the moral law, reason as free gives to itself. Respect for the law is respect for oneself as that self which is not determined by self-conceit and self-love. Respect relates in its specific making-manifest to the person: "respect always goes only to persons, never to things."

In respect for the law I place myself under the law. The specific having-a-feeling-for that lies in respect is a subjecting-oneself. In respect for the law I subject myself to myself, and in this subjecting-myself-to-myself I am as myself. As what — or more exactly as *whom* — am I manifest to myself in the feeling of respect?

Subjecting myself to the law, I subject myself to myself as pure reason. In this subjecting-myself-to-myself I raise myself to myself as the self-determining free being. This peculiar subjecting self-raising of oneself to oneself reveals the I in its "dignity." Negatively put: in respect for the law that I as a free being give myself, I cannot despise myself. Respect is accordingly the mode of the self-being of the I on the ground of which it does not "throw away the hero in its soul." Respect is the mode of the self's being-responsible to itself — proper selfhood.

The subjecting self-projecting onto the whole basic possibility of proper existing that the law gives is the essence of acting selfhood, of practical reason. Kant's moral self is thereby read in the vocabulary of *Sein und Zeit*: projection onto a basic possibility, and *eigentliches Selbstsein* [proper selfhood] — which is Heidegger's own term for authentic existence and does no work in Kant.

The interpretation of the feeling of respect shows not only how far respect constitutes practical reason; it makes clear at the same time that the concept of feeling in the sense of an empirically meant faculty of the soul has vanished, and that a transcendental basic structure of the transcendence of the moral self has stepped into its place. It is in this ontological-metaphysical sense that the expression "feeling" must be understood, if what Kant means by characterizing respect as "moral feeling" and as "feeling of my existence" is to be exhausted.

No further step is now needed to see that this essential structure of respect lets the original constitution of the transcendental imagination emerge within itself.

The subjecting immediate surrender to... is pure receptivity; the free self-giving of the law is pure spontaneity; and both are in themselves originally one. The single structure that §29 found in the understanding's binding of itself to a rule it gives itself is found again in the moral self's binding of itself to a law it gives itself; and the identity of the two structures is the demonstration.

And again only this origin of practical reason out of the transcendental imagination makes intelligible how far in respect both the law and the acting self are not grasped objectively, and yet are manifest in a more original, non-objective and unthematic way as ought and as acting, and form the unreflected acting self-being.

#### **§ 31. Die Ursprünglichkeit des gelegten Grundes und Kants Zurückweichen vor der transzendentalen Einbildungskraft**

The "highest principle of all synthetic judgments" delimited the full essence of the transcendence of pure knowledge, and the transcendental imagination revealed itself as the essential ground of that essence. But the more original interpretation of this essential ground first shows the range of the highest principle: it speaks of the essential constitution of the human essence as such, insofar as that essence is determined as finite pure reason.

This original essential constitution of the human being, "rooted" in the transcendental imagination, is the "unknown" into which Kant must have looked when he spoke of the "root unknown to us." For the unknown is not what we simply know nothing of, but what presses toward us within the known as the disquieting. And yet Kant did not carry out the more original interpretation of the transcendental imagination; he did not even set about it, despite the clear prefigurings of such an analytic that he himself first recognized. On the contrary:

Kant shrank back before this unknown root. Heidegger's own marginal note concedes the sentence's whole condition: it holds only for whoever grants that Kant somehow went *toward* the transcendental imagination, since only then is there a back to shrink to. The most famous thesis in the book is thus made explicitly conditional in the author's copy — and the note adds that §59 of the *Critique of Judgment* confirms the reading and shows the shrinking back again.

In the second edition of the *Critique*, the transcendental imagination as it came to light in the passionate drive of the first draft is pushed aside and reinterpreted — in favour of the understanding. Everything that had been its transcendental grounding achievement in the first edition had of course to be preserved, if the whole laying of the ground was not to collapse in on itself. In what sense the pure imagination returns in the *Critique of Judgment*, and above all whether it returns still in the exhibited express relation to the grounding of metaphysics as such, cannot be discussed here.

First, Kant deleted in the second edition the two main passages where he had expressly listed the imagination as a third basic faculty alongside sensibility and understanding. The first (A 94) is replaced by a critical discussion of Locke's and Hume's analysis of the understanding, as if Kant saw his own procedure in the first edition — wrongly — as still in the vicinity of empiricism.

The second passage (A 115) falls away through the reworking of the transcendental deduction as a whole.

Indeed, even the place where Kant first introduces the imagination in the *Critique* at all, as an "indispensable function of the soul," he altered afterwards, though only in his own copy, in a telling way: instead of "function of the soul" he now wants it to read "function of the understanding." With that the pure synthesis is assigned to the pure understanding. The pure imagination has become dispensable as a faculty of its own, and the possibility is apparently cut off that precisely it could be the essential ground of ontological knowledge — which the schematism chapter, left unchanged in the second edition, shows clearly enough. A marginal note enters the qualification that matters: the understanding to which the synthesis is transferred is the *transcendentally* grasped understanding, not the understanding of formal logic. The revision is therefore not a lapse into logicism but a redistribution within transcendental philosophy — and the tension with the unrevised schematism chapter is the evidence that the redistribution does not work.

But the transcendental imagination revealed itself not first in the schematism chapter, in the fourth stage, but already in the transcendental deduction, the third stage, as the forming middle of pure knowledge. If in the second edition it was to be removed from its central function as a basic faculty, then above all the transcendental deduction had to undergo a complete reworking. The transcendental imagination is the disquieting unknown that became the motive for the new version of the deduction; and only from this motive does the goal of the reworking become visible. A long marginal note weighs the gain and the loss with unusual balance: with the elimination of the imagination the distinction of sensibility and understanding becomes sharper and more unambiguous, the *logos* comes more into its right — though as transcendental, always related to intuition — the objectivity of the object is more decidedly referred to the "I combine," and certainty becomes more definitively the basic feature of metaphysics. But the possibility of making the necessary unity of the two faculties intelligible is taken away: they lie there like two blocks, which is precisely not the point of an investigation that is even supposed to be a theory of knowledge. The separation is the first fundamental task — but only a first.

The change from "function of the soul" to "function of the understanding" characterizes Kant's new position toward the transcendental imagination. It is no longer "function" as a faculty of its own but only "function" as an accomplishment, a dependent one, of the faculty of understanding. Whereas in the first edition all synthesis — synthesis as such — springs from the imagination as a faculty irreducible to sensibility or understanding, in the second edition the understanding alone takes over the role of origin for all synthesis.

Already at the beginning of the second-edition deduction it is said that synthesis "is an act of the spontaneity of the power of representation," which one "must call understanding, to distinguish it from sensibility" (B 130). Note already here the indifferent expression "power of representation."

"Synthesis" in general is now the name of an "act of the understanding"; "the faculty of combining a priori" is the "understanding" (B 135); hence the talk of the "pure synthesis of the understanding" (B 140, 153). And it does not stop at a tacit assignment of the synthesis-function: Kant says expressly that "the transcendental synthesis of the imagination is an effect of the understanding on sensibility" (B 152), and the "transcendental act of the imagination" is grasped as the "synthetic influence of the understanding on inner sense" (B 154), that is, on time.

But do these places not at the same time show that the transcendental imagination is still preserved? Certainly; its complete striking-out in the second edition would have been altogether too strange, especially as the "function" of the imagination remained indispensable for the problematic, and as the imagination is still named in the unreworked parts of the *Critique* standing before and after the transcendental deduction.

Only — the transcendental imagination is in the second edition there in name alone. "It is one and the same spontaneity that there, under the name of imagination, here of understanding, brings combination into the manifold of intuition" (B 162 note). Imagination is now only the name for the empirical, that is, intuition-related synthesis, which, as the passages cited show clearly enough, in substance belongs as synthesis to the understanding. The "synthesis" is only *called* "imagination" insofar as it relates to intuition; at bottom it is understanding — indeed, as a marginal note adds, the understanding itself.

The transcendental imagination no longer functions as an independent basic faculty that originally mediates sensibility and understanding in their possible unity; this in-between faculty now falls, as it were, through between the only two basic sources of the mind still held to. Its office is transferred to the understanding. And when Kant, only in the second edition, introduces an apparently characteristic proper name for the transcendental imagination under the title *synthesis speciosa*, precisely that expression proves that it has forfeited its earlier independence: it is so called only because in it the understanding relates itself to sensibility, and without this relation it remains *synthesis intellectualis*.

But why did Kant shrink back before the transcendental imagination? Did he perhaps not see the possibility of a more original laying of the ground? On the contrary. The preface to the first edition delimits the task of such a grounding with all clarity, distinguishing "two sides" of the transcendental deduction, an "objective" and a "subjective" one.

Spoken from the interpretation of the deduction given above, that means: the deduction poses the question of the inner possibility of transcendence and unveils through its answer the horizon of objectivity. The analysis of the objectivity of possible objects is the "objective" side of the deduction.

But objectivity forms itself in the turning-toward letting-stand-over-against, which happens in the pure subject as such. The question of the faculties essentially involved in this turning-toward, and of their possibility, is the question of the subjectivity of the transcending subject as such. That is the "subjective" side of the deduction.

Because it mattered to Kant above all first to make transcendence visible at all, in order to illuminate from it the essence of transcendental — ontological — knowledge, the objective deduction is "also essentially belonging to my purposes. The other aims at considering the pure understanding itself, according to its possibility and the cognitive powers on which it itself rests, hence at considering it in subjective relation; and although this discussion is of great importance with respect to my chief end, it does not belong essentially to it; because the chief question always remains what and how much understanding and reason can know free of all experience, and not: how is the faculty of thinking itself possible?" (A xvi f.).

The transcendental deduction is in itself necessarily objective and subjective at once, for it is the unveiling of transcendence, which first forms the turning-toward an objectivity that is essential for a finite subjectivity. The subjective side of the deduction can therefore never be missing; but its express working-out can be postponed. And if Kant decided on that, he could have done so only out of a clear insight into the essence of such a working-out.

In the cited characterization of the subjective deduction it is moreover clearly said that it must go back to "cognitive powers on which the understanding itself rests." Kant sees further, entirely clearly, that this regress into the origin can be no empirically explanatory psychological consideration that posits a ground only "hypothetically." This task of a transcendental unveiling of the essence of the subjectivity of the subject is not first set subsequently in the preface; Kant speaks already in preparing the deduction of this "path as yet quite untrodden," which necessarily brings a "darkness" with it. He wants to give no "prolix" theory of subjectivity, though the "deduction of the categories" "necessitates" penetrating "deep into the first grounds of the possibility of our knowledge in general" (A 98).

Kant thus knew of the possibility and the necessity of a more original laying of the ground, but it did not lie in his immediate intention. That, however, could not be the ground for striking out the transcendental imagination, which after all forms the unity of transcendence and its objectivity. The transcendental imagination must itself give the occasion for Kant's turning away from it as a transcendental basic faculty in its own right. The argument is careful here where it most needs to be: postponement would explain silence, not deletion, so the motive must lie in the matter and not in Kant's plan of work.

Because Kant did not carry out the subjective deduction, the subjectivity of the subject remained guiding for him in the constitution and characterization that traditional anthropology and psychology offered. For these the imagination was simply a lower faculty within sensibility. And indeed the result of the transcendental deduction and the schematism — the insight into the transcendental essence of the pure imagination — was by itself not strong enough to let the subjectivity of the subject as a whole be seen in a new light.

How, after all, is the lower faculty of sensibility to be able to make up the essence of reason? Does not everything fall into confusion if the lowest is set uppermost? What is to become of the venerable tradition in which *ratio* and *logos* claim the central function in the history of metaphysics? Can the primacy of logic fall? Can the architectonic of the laying of the ground, the division into transcendental aesthetic and transcendental logic, be maintained at all, if what they have as their theme is at bottom transcendental imagination?

Is the "Critique of Pure Reason" not deprived of its theme by itself, if pure reason flips over into transcendental imagination? Does this laying of the ground not lead before an abyss? The abyss is the word for the finding: a grounding whose ground is neither reason nor sense, but a power for which the tradition has no place and whose name is borrowed from a lower faculty, has no floor under it. It is the same word that governs Heidegger's later thinking of the ground, and it is here that it first does work.

Kant brought the "possibility" of metaphysics before this abyss in the radicalism of his questioning. He saw the unknown. He had to shrink back. For it was not only that the transcendental imagination frightened him; it was that in the meantime pure reason *as reason* had drawn him still more strongly into its spell. Two causes are given, and the second is the weightier: the retreat is not a failure of nerve but the pull of a positive discovery elsewhere.

Through the laying of the ground of metaphysics Kant first won a clear insight into the character of the "universality" of ontological-metaphysical knowledge. Only now did he have staff and stick in hand to traverse the domain of "moral philosophy" critically and to replace the indeterminate empirical universality of the popular-philosophical doctrines about morality by the essential originality of ontological analyses, which alone can supply a "metaphysics of morals" and its grounding. In the struggle against the shallow and veiled empiricism of the ruling moral philosophy, the decisive demarcation of the pure a priori against everything empirical gained growing significance. And insofar as the essence of the subjectivity of the subject lies in its personality, while personality is synonymous with moral reason, the reason-character of pure knowing and of acting had to consolidate itself: all pure synthesis, and synthesis as such, must as spontaneity fall to the faculty that is free in the proper sense — acting reason.

The pure reason-character of personality thus more and more unveiling itself could of course not touch the finitude of the human being for Kant either, since a being determined by morality and by ought can essentially never become and be "infinite." What did grow for Kant was the problem of seeking finitude now precisely in the pure rational being itself, and not first in its being determined by "sensibility"; for only so could morality be grasped as pure — not conditioned, let alone created, by the factically empirical human being.

This personal-ontological problem of a finite pure reason as such could tolerate nothing in its vicinity that recalled the specific constitution of a determinate kind of realization of a finite rational being. But the imagination was exactly such a thing, counting not only as specifically human but moreover as a sensible faculty.

The problematic of a pure reason, so strengthening itself, had to push the imagination aside altogether and thereby cover over its transcendental essence all the more.

It is unmistakable that the problem of this distinction between a finite rational being as such and the particular realization of one, the human being, presses forward in the transcendental deduction of the second edition. Indeed the very first "improvement" Kant makes in the second edition, on the first page of his work, makes it clear: in characterizing finite knowledge, and specifically finite intuition, the words "at least for us humans" are inserted. That is to show that although every finite intuition is a receptive one, the receiving need not necessarily, as with us humans, be mediated through sense-organs. The insertion that §5 read as a mere clarification is read here as symptom: Kant is beginning to think a finite rational being that is not human, and the imagination is the faculty that cannot survive the generalization.

The darkness and "strangeness" of the transcendental imagination, the ground laid free in the first grounding, and the bright power of pure reason have worked together to veil again the perspective into the more original essence of the transcendental imagination that broke open, as it were, only for a moment.

This is the core content, grasped from the fundamental problem of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, of the observation long since established in Kant interpretation and usually expressed thus: Kant turned from the "psychological" interpretation of the first edition to the more "logical" one of the second.

It must nevertheless be noted that the grounding in the first edition was never "psychological," as little as that in the second became "logical." Both are rather transcendental, that is, necessarily both "objective" and "subjective." Only, in the transcendentally subjective grounding, the second edition decided for the pure understanding against the pure imagination, in order to save the rule of reason. In the second edition the subjective, "psychological" deduction so little recedes that it rather sharpens itself precisely in the direction of the pure understanding as the faculty of synthesis. It now becomes superfluous to lead the understanding back to more original "cognitive powers." A marginal note supplies the reason: the understanding and the judgment are in the second edition grasped as transcendental from the outset, that is, as related to intuition — so nothing further is needed behind them.

The interpretation of the stages of the laying of the ground carried out above in exclusive orientation to the first edition has constantly moved the finitude of human transcendence into the centre of the problematic. If Kant in the second edition posed the problem of finitude more comprehensively, by extending the concept of a finite rational being so that it no longer coincides with the concept of the human being, is that not ground enough — precisely where a central interpretation of the work is intended — to hold to the second edition? On what has been said, the second edition is not "better" because it proceeds more "logically"; on the contrary, it is in a rightly understood sense still more "psychological," and in exclusive direction toward pure reason as such.

But is judgment not thereby passed on the interpretation presented here, and all the more on the more original reading of the transcendental imagination that grew out of it? Why, though, was the finitude of pure knowledge moved into the problem from the outset? Because the metaphysics whose ground is to be laid belongs to the "nature of the human being." For the laying of the ground of metaphysics, therefore, the *specific* finitude of human nature is decisive. The seemingly external question whether in interpreting the *Critique* the second edition deserves preference over the first or the reverse is only the pale reflection of the question decisive for Kant's laying of the ground and its interpretation: is the transcendental imagination, as the ground laid, bearing enough to determine originally — unitarily and as a whole — precisely the finite essence of the subjectivity of the human subject; or is the problem of a *human* pure reason made more graspable as a problem, and brought nearer a possible solution, by eliminating the transcendental imagination? So long as this question is not decided, the attempted more original interpretation of the transcendental imagination necessarily remains incomplete. The edition question is thereby converted from a philological into a systematic one, and Heidegger declines to award himself the victory: whether the imagination can bear the weight is not settled by the fact that Kant named it.

### **C. Die transzendentale Einbildungskraft und das Problem der menschlichen reinen Vernunft**

That in the *Critique of Pure Reason* as a laying of the ground of metaphysics it is from the outset, and only, human pure reason that is at issue may first be made clear by a characteristic piece of evidence. The formula for the problem of the possibility of *metaphysica generalis* runs: "how are synthetic judgments a priori possible?" And Kant says of the solution of this task: "the above task cannot be solved otherwise than thus: that we first consider it in relation to the faculties of the human being through which he is capable of the extension of his knowledge a priori, and which make up in him what one may specifically call his pure reason. For if by a pure reason of a being in general one understands the faculty of knowing things independently of experience, hence of sense-representations, then it is not at all determined in what way such knowledge is possible in that being (in God, say, or in another higher spirit), and the task is then indeterminate. As regards the human being, by contrast, every knowledge of his consists of concept and intuition."

These sentences stand in the treatise "On the Progress of Metaphysics," in whose working-out Kant certainly had the problematic of metaphysics as such immediately and as a whole before his eyes. In a laying of the ground of metaphysics, then, the "specific" finitude of human subjectivity is the problem. It cannot be drawn into the consideration merely in passing as one possible "case" of a finite rational being in general. Kant's own late text is thus produced against the second edition's tendency to generalize, and it is the strongest evidence Heidegger has for holding to the first.

To the finitude of the human being belongs sensibility in the meaning of receptive intuition. As pure intuition, that is, as pure sensibility, it is a necessary element of the structure of transcendence that marks finitude out. Human pure reason is necessarily a pure *sensible* reason. This pure reason must be sensible in itself; it does not first become so by being tied to a body. Conversely, the human being as a finite rational being can "have" his body in a transcendental, that is, metaphysical sense only because transcendence as such is a priori sensible. The order of grounding is the point: the body does not make us sensible; our sensibility, understood as finitude, is what makes having a body possible.

If the transcendental imagination is to be the original ground of the possibility of human subjectivity — and precisely in its unity and wholeness — then it must make possible something like a pure sensible reason. But pure sensibility, in the universal meaning in which it must be grasped in a laying of the ground of metaphysics, is time.

Time as pure sensibility is supposed to stand in an original unity with the "I think" of pure apperception? The pure I, which on the generally prevailing interpretation Kant places outside all temporality and against all time, is supposed to be "temporal"? And all of this on the ground of the transcendental imagination? How does the imagination relate to time at all?

#### **§ 32. Die transzendentale Einbildungskraft und ihr Bezug zur Zeit**

The transcendental imagination was set out as the origin of pure sensible intuition, and with that it is in principle demonstrated that time as a pure intuition springs from the transcendental imagination. But an analytic illumination of its own is needed for the way in which precisely time is grounded in the transcendental imagination.

Time "flows constantly" as the pure one-after-another of the now-sequence, and pure intuition intuits this one-after-another non-objectively. To intuit means to receive what gives itself, and pure intuition gives itself in receiving what is to be received.

Receiving is at first understood as the receipt of something present, at hand. But this narrow concept of receiving, still oriented by empirical intuition, must be kept away from pure intuition and its character of receiving. That the pure intuition of the pure one-after-another of the nows cannot be the receiving of something present is easily seen: were it that, it could at best "intuit" the current now on each occasion, but never the now-sequence as such and the horizon forming itself in it. Strictly, in the mere receiving of something "present" not even a single now can be intuited, since a now has an essentially unbroken stretch into its just-now and its right-away. The receiving of pure intuition must give the look of the now from within itself, and give it so that it looks ahead to the right-away and looks back to the just-now. The now is thereby denied the character of a point, and with that the standard picture of the now-sequence as a line of instants is disarmed from within: each now already reaches beyond itself in both directions, so that the receiving of it is already a forming.

Only now does it unveil itself more concretely that, and how far, the pure intuition the transcendental aesthetic treats of cannot from the outset be the receiving of something "present." The receptive self-giving in pure intuition is fundamentally not related to something merely present, and least of all to a being at hand.

But if pure intuiting has this free-ranging character, does it already follow that it is "at bottom" pure imagination? That follows at most insofar as pure intuition itself forms what is to be received in it. But that this original forming should in itself be at once a looking, a looking-ahead and a looking-back — surely that has nothing to do with the transcendental imagination.

If only Kant himself had not expressly exhibited this threefold-single character of forming in the imagining of the imagination.

In his metaphysics lectures, in the rational psychology, Kant articulates the "forming power" as follows: this faculty brings forth representations either of the present time, or of past time, or of future time. Accordingly the forming faculty consists, first, in the faculty of *Abbildung*, which are representations of the present time, *facultas formandi*; second, in the faculty of *Nachbildung*, representations of past time, *facultas imaginandi*; third, in the faculty of *Vorbildung*, representations of future time, *facultas praevidendi*.

The word *Abbildung* needs a short explanation. It does not mean the production of a copy but the look that can be taken immediately off the present object itself. The forming-off does not mean an after-forming but the giving of an image in the sense of the immediate perceiving of the object's own look.

Without Kant's speaking of the transcendental imagination at this place, one thing becomes clear: the forming of "imagining" is in itself related to time. And pure imagining, which is called pure because it forms what it forms from itself, must, as itself related to time, first form time. Time as pure intuition means neither merely what is intuited in pure intuiting nor merely an intuiting that lacks its "object." Time as pure intuition is in one the forming intuiting of what it intuits — and only this gives the full concept of time. Kant's own three-part psychology of imagining is thus made to yield the three ecstases: the faculty divides according to present, past and future, and the division is not incidental to imagining but its structure.

But pure intuition can form the pure one-after-another of the now-sequence as such only if it is in itself an off-forming, fore-forming and after-forming imagination. Time may therefore in no way, and precisely not in Kant's sense, be thought as an arbitrary field into which the imagination happens to have strayed for the purposes of its activity. Within the horizon in which we "reckon with time," time must indeed be taken as pure now-sequence; but this now-sequence is by no means time in its originality. The transcendental imagination rather lets time as now-sequence spring forth, and as this letting-spring-forth it is the original time.

But can so far-reaching an interpretation of the transcendental imagination as original time be picked out of Kant's few hints? Given the incalculable consequences that follow from it, it must be grounded more concretely and more securely.

#### **§ 33. Der innere Zeitcharakter der transzendentalen Einbildungskraft**

The imagination is called in the first edition the faculty of "synthesis in general." If the inner time-character of the imagination is to be exhibited, we must investigate where Kant expressly treats of synthesis. That happens in the section preparing the execution of the transcendental deduction on its two ways, headed "On the a priori grounds for the possibility of experience" (A 95 ff.). The place of the thematic analysis of synthesis as such is thus not arbitrary. And when Kant designates the discussion of synthesis in particular as a "preliminary reminder," what is meant is not an incidental and at bottom dispensable remark: what is treated there must be kept in view in advance for the transcendental deduction and the transcendental schematism.

The three elements of pure knowledge are pure intuition, pure imagination and pure understanding. Their possible unity, the essence of their original unification, is the problem; hence an illumination of synthesis with a view to these three pure elements is needed.

Kant therefore articulates his "preliminary reminder" into three parts: of the synthesis of apprehension in intuition; of the synthesis of reproduction in imagination; of the synthesis of recognition in the concept.

Are these modes of synthesis three in number because three elements belong to the essential unity of pure knowledge? Or does this triad have a more original ground, one that at the same time illuminates why they are, precisely as modes of pure synthesis, single, and so on the basis of their more original unity can "form" the essential unity of the three elements? Are there three modes of synthesis because in them time comes to appearance, and because they express the threefold unity of time as present, having-been and future? And if the original unification of the essential unity of ontological knowledge happens through time, while the ground of the possibility of pure knowledge is the transcendental imagination, does the latter not then reveal itself as the original time?

But in naming the second mode "synthesis of reproduction in imagination," Kant already says in the very enumeration that the imagination is only one element among others and by no means the root of intuition and concept. That is correct.

But just as indisputably the transcendental deduction, for which this analysis of the threefold synthesis is to supply the foundation, shows that the imagination is not merely one faculty among the others but presents their mediating middle. That the transcendental imagination is the root of sensibility and understanding was shown only by the more original interpretation; and no use may be made of that result here. Rather the working-out of the inner time-character of the three modes of synthesis is to supply the last decisive proof that the interpretation of the transcendental imagination as the root of the two stems is not only possible but necessary. The methodological care is worth marking: the thesis about the root is set aside so that the argument from the threefold synthesis can stand on its own, and the two demonstrations therefore do not lean on each other.

Several things must be clarified in advance and held as guiding for a general understanding of Kant's analysis. First, Kant's manner of expression needs closer determination. What does synthesis "of" apprehension, "of" reproduction, "of" recognition mean? Not that apprehension and the rest are subjected to a synthesis, nor that apprehension or reproduction or recognition carries out a synthesis, but that the synthesis as such has the character either of apprehension or of reproduction or of recognition. The expressions mean: synthesis in the mode of apprehension, of reproduction, of recognition — synthesis as apprehending, as reproducing, as recognizing. Kant thus treats of the synthesis, the faculty of synthesis, with a view to these three modes as the ones specifically proper to it.

Second, it must be noted that the modes of synthesis are in each part first clarified by a description of the way in which they function in empirical intuition, in empirical imagining and in empirical thinking. But this preparatory characterization intends to show that in pure intuition, pure imagining and pure thinking a correspondingly pure apprehending, reproducing and recognizing synthesis is already constitutive; and thereby that these modes of pure synthesis make up the condition of the possibility of the empirical synthesis in knowing comportment toward beings.

Third, it must be seen that — though not always formulated clearly enough or in advance — the proper goal of the interpretation of the three modes lies in exhibiting their inner and essential belonging-together in the essence of pure synthesis as such.

And finally it must not be forgotten what Kant himself expressly demands be "laid at the ground of what follows": all our representations are subject to time. But if all intuiting, imagining and thinking representation is governed throughout by the threefold synthesis, is it not the time-character of this synthesis that makes everything subject to it in a unified way in advance?

**The pure synthesis as pure apprehension.** In empirical intuition, as the immediate receiving of a "this-here," something manifold always shows itself; what the look this intuition wins presents "contains" a manifold. And this could never "be represented as such if the mind did not distinguish the time in the succession of impressions upon one another." Our mind must, distinguishing the time, already constantly say in advance "now and now and now," so that "now this" and "now that" and "all of this now at once" can be encountered. Only in such a distinguishing of the nows does it become possible to "run through" the impressions and take them together.

Intuition is a representation of a manifold — a *repraesentatio singularis* — only if as receptive it takes up and embraces the offer of the manifold "straight off" in one. Intuition is in itself "synthetic," and this synthesis has the peculiarity that within the horizon of the one-after-another of the now-sequence it takes a look, an image, off the offer of impressions straight off. It is an immediate off-forming in the sense clarified.

But we necessarily have a pure apprehending synthesis as well, because without it we could not have the representation of time, this pure intuition, at all. The pure apprehending synthesis is not first carried out within the horizon of time; it first forms anything like the now and the now-sequence. Pure intuition is "original receptivity," a receiving of what it releases from itself as receiving. Its "offering" is a "generating"; and what the purely intuiting offering — forming as giving a look — generates, forming as creating, is the immediate look of the now as such, of the current present in general.

Empirical intuition goes straight toward the being present in the now; the pure apprehending synthesis goes toward the now, the present itself, and in such a way that this intuiting going-toward forms in itself that toward which it goes. The pure synthesis as apprehension is, in offering "present in general," time-forming. The pure synthesis of apprehension therefore has in itself time-character.

But Kant says expressly: "there is thus in us an active faculty of the synthesis of this manifold, which we call imagination, and whose act exercised immediately upon perceptions I call apprehension" (A 120). Synthesis in the mode of apprehension springs from the imagination; the pure apprehending synthesis must therefore be addressed as a mode of the transcendental imagination. And if this synthesis is time-forming, then the transcendental imagination has in itself pure time-character. But insofar as the pure imagination is an "ingredient" of pure intuition, so that a synthesis of imagination already lies in intuition, what Kant next calls "imagination" cannot be identical with the transcendental imagination. The distinction saves the argument from an obvious objection: the imagination named as the second element is the empirical one, and the transcendental imagination is what is at work already in the first.

**The pure synthesis as pure reproduction.** Kant again begins the analysis with a pointer to the reproductive synthesis in empirical representing. The "mind" can represent beings — something earlier perceived, say — "even without the presence of the object." Such making-present, or as Kant says "imagining," presupposes that the mind has the possibility of bringing the earlier represented being back representingly, so as to represent it in a unity of being with the being currently perceived. This bringing-back-again — reproduction — is thus a kind of uniting.

But this reproducing synthesis can unite only if the mind does not "lose from its thoughts" what is to be brought back in it. In such a synthesis there therefore lies necessarily the not-losing, the being-able-to-retain. But a being experienced earlier can be retained only if the mind "distinguishes the time" and thereby has in view anything like the "earlier" and the "back then." The being experienced earlier would be lost constantly with every now if it were not retainable at all. So that empirical synthesis in the mode of reproduction may be possible, the no-longer-now as such must in advance and before all experience be capable of being brought back again and united with the current now. That happens in the pure reproduction as a mode of pure synthesis. And if empirical reproductive synthesis belongs primarily to empirical imagining, then pure reproduction is pure synthesis of the pure imagination.

But does not the pure imagination count as essentially *productive*? How is a reproductive synthesis to belong to it? Pure reproduction — does that not mean productive reproduction, and so a wooden iron?

But is pure reproduction a productive reproducing? It is indeed: it forms the possibility of reproduction as such, by bringing the horizon of the earlier into view and holding it open in advance as such. The pure synthesis in the mode of reproduction forms having-been as such. Which says: the pure imagination is with respect to this mode of synthesis time-forming. It can be called pure after-forming, not because it goes after a past being or after one experienced earlier, but insofar as it discloses the horizon of possible going-after — having-been — and so "forms" this "after" as such. Heidegger's footnote defends the received text of A 102 against Riehl, who proposed emending "reproductive" to "productive" in the phrase about the transcendental acts of the mind; the emendation would remove precisely the sense Kant wants, that the productive, that is, pure imagination is purely reproductive in making reproduction possible at all.

But where does the pure synthesis-character stay in this forming of time in the mode of the "back then"? The originally forming retaining of the "back then" is in itself the retaining forming of the no-longer-now, and this forming unites itself as such with the now on each occasion. Pure reproduction is essentially single with the pure synthesis of intuition as the one forming the present. "The synthesis of apprehension is therefore inseparably bound up with the synthesis of reproduction" (A 102) — for every now is now already just-now. For the synthesis of apprehension to give the current look straight off in an image, it must be able to retain the present manifold it has run through as such; it must at the same time be pure synthesis of reproduction.

But if the synthesis of apprehension, like that of reproduction, is an act of the transcendental imagination, then the imagination must be grasped as what functions synthetically, "inseparably" in itself, as the faculty of "synthesis in general" according to both these modes. And in this original unity of the two modes it can then also be the origin of time as the unity of present and having-been. Did this original unity of the two modes not obtain, then "not even the purest and first fundamental representations of space and time could arise."

And if time is the threefold-single whole of present, having-been and future, while Kant adds a third mode to the two now demonstrated as time-forming, and if moreover all representing, hence also thinking, is to be subject to time, then this third mode of synthesis must "form" the future.

**The pure synthesis as pure recognition.** The analysis of this third synthesis is incomparably more extensive than the first two, and yet one will at first look in vain for what has just been inferred by a seemingly compelling argument. The synthesis of pure recognition is to constitute the third element of pure knowledge, pure thinking. But what has recognition to do with the future? How is pure thinking, the I of pure apperception, to have time-character at all, when Kant precisely sets the "I think" and reason in general most sharply against all time-relations?

"Pure reason, as a merely intelligible faculty, is not subject to the form of time and hence not to the conditions of temporal succession" (A 551, B 579). And does Kant not show, immediately after the schematism chapter and by way of introducing the determination of the highest principle of all synthetic judgments, that time-character must remain excluded from the "highest principle of all analytic judgments," the principle of contradiction, which delimits the essence of mere thinking? The "at the same time" (*hama*) can have no place in the formula of this principle; otherwise "the proposition would be affected by the condition of time." "Now the principle of contradiction, as a merely logical principle, must not restrict its claims to temporal relations, and such a formula is therefore entirely contrary to its intention."

Is it any wonder, then, that nothing of a time-character of this third mode of synthesis is found in Kant? Yet here neither empty conjecture and inference is of use, nor may what one first finds in reading the discussion of this third synthesis be alone decisive.

Kant begins the presentation of the third mode too with a characterization of empirical recognition, setting in with the synthesis as reproduction: "without the consciousness that what we think is the very same as what we thought a moment before, all reproduction in the series of representations would be in vain" (A 103). The reproducing synthesis is to set and hold what it brings in a unity with the being manifest in the current perception.

But when the mind comes back from its regress into the past to the currently present being, in order to set the one in unity with the other, who tells it that this now present being is the same as the one it left, as it were, when it carried out the making-present? The reproducing synthesis by its essence comes up against something that it claims as the same being — the one experienced in present perception before, during and after its own execution. But perception itself always goes only straight toward what is present as such. Does not the whole succession of representing break apart into single representations, so that the returning synthesis of reproduction must at every moment set what it brings into unity with some other being currently at hand? What is the point of the unity of apprehending intuition and reproducing imagination, if what they want to give as one and the same is, so to speak, without a place?

Is this place first created after a perception has been carried out and a recollection has attached itself to it, wanting to set what is recollected as one with what is present "in the current state"? Or are these two modes of synthesis in advance already oriented toward the being as something present in sameness?

Evidently. For at the ground of both syntheses there already lies, guiding them, a uniting of the being with a view to its sameness. This synthesis toward the same — the holding-before-oneself of the being as a same — Kant rightly calls the synthesis "in the concept," since the concept is the representing of a unity that "holds for many" as the same. "For this one consciousness is what unites the manifold, intuited little by little and then also reproduced, into one representation."

So it shows: what appears as the third synthesis in the characterization of the empirical genesis of concept-formation is precisely the first — the synthesis that first gives the lead to the two characterized before it. It springs ahead of them, as it were. Kant gives this synthesis of identification a very apt name: its uniting is a *recognizing*, a reconnoitring. As pure it scouts out in advance and is "peering through" toward what must be held forth in advance as the same, so that the apprehending and the reproducing synthesis can find a closed circle of beings within which they can bring and receive as beings what they supply and come upon. Heidegger is exploiting the military sense of *rekognoszieren*, to reconnoitre: recognition is not re-cognizing something already known but scouting ahead of an advance, and that is why it is the mode of the future.

But this scouting, forward-pressing synthesis of identification, as empirical, necessarily presupposes a pure identification. Which says: as a pure reproduction forms the possibility of a bringing-back-again, so pure recognition must correspondingly offer the possibility for anything like identifying. And if this pure synthesis recognizes, then it at the same time does not scout out a being that it can hold before itself as the same, but scouts out the horizon of holdability-before-oneself as such. Its scouting is, as pure, the original forming of this holding-before, that is, of the *future*. So the third mode of synthesis too proves to be essentially time-forming. And insofar as Kant assigns the modes of off-forming, after-forming and fore-forming to the empirical imagining, the forming of the held-before as such — pure fore-forming — is an act of the pure imagination.

Where it at first seemed hopeless, indeed absurd, to illuminate the inner forming of the pure concept as essentially time-determined, not only has the time-character of the third mode of pure synthesis come to light; this mode of pure fore-forming even shows, according to its inner structure, a primacy over the two others, with which it nonetheless essentially belongs together. What if in this apparently wholly time-averted Kantian analysis of the pure synthesis in the concept the most original essence of time were to come to appearance — that time temporalizes itself primarily out of the future? The thesis of *Sein und Zeit* on the primacy of the future within the ecstatic unity of temporality is thus found in the last place anyone would look for it, in Kant's account of the concept; and the finding is put as a question rather than a claim, because Kant nowhere says it.

However that may be, the task of demonstrating the inner time-character of the transcendental imagination is solved. If the transcendental imagination as the pure forming faculty forms time in itself, that is, lets it spring forth, then there is no evading the thesis stated above: the transcendental imagination is the original time.

The universal character of pure sensibility, of time, has now likewise been shown. The transcendental imagination can therefore bear and form the asserted original unity and wholeness of the specific finitude of the human subject as a pure sensible reason.

But do pure sensibility (time) and pure reason not remain simply unhomogeneous, and the concept of a pure sensible reason a non-concept? The misgivings against the attempt to grasp the selfhood of the self as in itself temporal — not merely to characterize the empirical grasping of the empirical subject as time-determined — seem insurmountable.

But if the self will not let itself be exhibited as temporal, does the reverse way perhaps have a prospect of success? How stands it with the demonstration that time as such has the character of selfhood? That can the less miscarry as it is undisputed that time is "nothing outside the subject" — which says, after all, that in the subject it is everything.

Yet what does "in the subject" mean here? Time is surely not present "in the subject" the way cells are in the brain, and the constant appeal to the subjectivity of time yields little. Has Kant himself seen only this negative point, that time is "nothing outside the subject"? Has he not shown in the transcendental deduction and the schematism chapter that time is essentially involved in the innermost essential structure of transcendence? And does transcendence not determine the selfhood of the finite self? Must this essence of subjectivity not be kept in view if the much-discussed "subjective" character of time is even to be legitimately asked after? If Kant met time in the "depth" of the essential ground of transcendence, will what the transcendental aesthetic says introductorily about time be the last word — or is what is discussed there only a pointer to the more original essence of time? Can the time-character of the subject in the end be illuminated only from the rightly grasped subject-character of time? The reversal is the move that makes §34 possible: instead of asking whether the I is in time, ask what time is such that it can be the being of a self.

#### **§ 34. Die Zeit als reine Selbstaffektion und der Zeitcharakter des Selbst**

At the place where he first circumscribes the essential unity of pure knowledge, in the second stage of the laying of the ground, Kant remarks that space and time "must always affect" the concept of the representations of objects. What does this at first dark thesis mean — that time affects a concept, and specifically the concept of the representations of objects?

The interpretation begins by clarifying the "concept of the representations of objects." The expression means first the "universal" that characterizes every representing of objects as such, and that is the letting-stand-over-against. This, the thesis says, is necessarily affected by time. But so far it was only said that time and space form the horizon within which the affections of the senses meet and concern us. Now time itself is to affect. But all affection is the announcing-of-itself of a being already at hand; time, however, is neither at hand nor "outside" at all. Where does it come from, if it is to affect?

Time is pure intuition only in this way: that it fore-forms from itself the look of the one-after-another and holds this, as such, toward itself as the forming receiving. This pure intuition concerns itself with what it has formed in itself, and does so without the help of experience. Time is by its essence pure affection of itself. Indeed more: it is precisely what forms anything like a "from-itself-out-toward...," in such a way that the toward-which so forming itself looks back and into the aforementioned toward. The paradox of an affection with nothing outside to do the affecting is resolved by making the affecting and the affected the same movement — which is why "self-affection" is not one species of affection but the origin of the structure of affection as such.

Time as pure self-affection is not an effecting affection that strikes a self already at hand; as pure it forms the essence of anything like being-concerned-with-oneself. And insofar as it belongs to the essence of the finite subject to be able to be concerned as a self, time as pure self-affection forms the essential structure of subjectivity.

Only on the ground of this selfhood can the finite being be what it must be: dependent on receiving.

Only now can it become clear what the dark sentence says — that time necessarily affects the concept of the representations of objects. To affect the letting-stand-over-against as such, as pure turning-toward, purely, means to bring before it anything like an "against-it," the counter — before "it," that is, before the pure letting-stand-over-against, before pure apperception, before the I itself. Time belongs to the inner possibility of this letting-stand-over-against; as pure self-affection it originally forms finite selfhood in such a way that the self can be something like self-consciousness.

In working out the presuppositions decisive for the inner problematic of the *Critique*, the finitude of knowledge was moved into the centre. The finitude of knowledge rests in the finitude of intuiting, in receiving. Pure knowledge, the knowing of what stands over against as such, the pure concept, is therefore grounded in a receptive intuition. But to receive purely means to be affected free of experience, that is, to affect oneself.

Time as pure self-affection is that finite pure intuition which bears and makes possible the pure concept, the understanding, which stands in essential service to intuition.

The idea of pure self-affection, which as has now become clear determines the innermost essence of transcendence, was therefore not first introduced by Kant in the second edition. There it is merely formulated more explicitly, and characteristically already in the transcendental aesthetic. That passage must of course remain dark so long as the interpretation lacks the perspective the preceding presentation of the stages and their more original grasp was to secure; within that perspective it is almost "self-evident."

"Now that which, as representation, can precede any act of thinking something is intuition; and if it contains nothing but relations, it is the form of intuition, which, since it represents nothing except insofar as something is posited in the mind, can be nothing other than the way in which the mind is affected by its own activity, namely this positing of its representation, hence through itself: that is, an inner sense as regards its form" (B 67 f.).

Sense means finite intuition, and the form of sense is pure receiving. The inner sense does not receive "from outside" but from the self. The inner affection must in pure receiving come from the pure self, that is, form itself in the essence of selfhood as such and thereby first make up that selfhood. Pure self-affection gives the transcendental original structure of the finite self as such. It is therefore not at all as though a mind existed and among other things also related something to itself and exercised self-positings; rather this "from-itself-out-toward... and back-onto-itself" first constitutes the mind-character of the mind as a finite self. A footnote defends the received "its representation" against the proposed emendation, since what is posited in the mind is not a representation belonging to the mind but the representing that re-presents the pure relations of the now-sequence and lets them come toward the receiving.

With that it is manifest at a stroke that time as pure self-affection does not occur "alongside" pure apperception "in the mind," but as the ground of the possibility of selfhood already lies within pure apperception, and so first makes the mind a mind.

The pure finite self has in itself time-character. And if the I, pure reason, is by its essence temporal, then it must be from this time-character that the decisive determination Kant gives of transcendental apperception first becomes intelligible.

Time and the "I think" no longer stand over against each other as incompatible and unhomogeneous: they are the same. Through the radicalism with which he first interpreted transcendentally, in his laying of the ground of metaphysics, both time on its own and the "I think" on its own, Kant brought the two together into their original sameness — without of course expressly seeing that sameness as such. The claim is exactly calibrated: Kant produced the identity and did not see it, which is neither a discovery credited to him nor an invention imposed on him.

May one then read past as unconcernedly as hitherto the fact that Kant predicates the same essential predicates of both time and the "I think"?

In the transcendental deduction the transcendental essence of the I is characterized thus: "for the standing and abiding I (of pure apperception) makes up the correlate of all our representations" (A 123). And in the schematism chapter, where the transcendental essence of time comes to light, Kant says of time: "time does not elapse," time "which is itself unchangeable and abiding" (A 143, B 183); and later: "time abides and does not change" (A 182, B 224 f.).

Naturally, one will reply, this coincidence of essential predicates for time and for the I is not surprising, since Kant only wants to say that the I, and time too, are not "in time." Granted; but does it follow from this that the I is not temporal — or does it rather emerge that the I is so very "temporal" that it *is* time itself and becomes possible according to its ownmost essence only as time?

What, then, does it mean that the "standing and abiding" I makes up the "correlate" of all our representations? First this: the standing and abiding I carries out the letting-stand-over-against of something that is not only a relation of the toward-onto... but a correlation of the back-toward-into..., and so forms the counter. But why does Kant say that the "standing and abiding I" forms this letting-stand-over-against? Does he want to impress on us that this forming I always underlies all psychic occurrences and "persists" as something exempt from all change in them? Is Kant — who, supported by his own laying of the ground of ontology, worked out the paralogism of substantiality — supposed to have meant by the "standing and abiding I" something like a soul-substance? Or does he only want to confirm that this I is not temporal but, though not as substance, yet in a certain sense infinite and eternal? But why does this supposed confirmation stand precisely where he delimits the finitude of the I, its letting-stand-over-against? For the simple reason that this "standing and abiding" of the I belongs essentially to that letting-stand-over-against.

These words "standing" and "abiding" are no ontic assertions about the unchangingness of the I; they are transcendental determinations meaning this: only insofar as the I as such holds before itself in advance anything like constancy and abiding does it form the horizon of sameness within which anything objective can be experienced as the same in change. The "standing" I is so called because as "I think," that is, "I re-present," it holds before itself anything like stand and standing-stock. As I it forms the correlate of constancy as such.

But this pure procuring of the pure look of present in general is precisely the essence of time itself as pure intuition. The "standing and abiding" I means as much as: the I, in the original forming of time — as original time — forms the letting-stand-over-against and its horizon. The very predicates that the tradition takes as proof of the I's timelessness are shown to be predicates of time, and the classic argument for the atemporality of the transcendental subject is turned into its opposite.

About the timelessness and eternity of the I nothing is thereby decided; within the transcendental problematic it is not even asked. But "standing and abiding" in this transcendental sense the I is as long as it is temporal, that is, as a finite self.

If then the same predicates are asserted of time, this means not only that time is not "in time," but this: if time as pure self-affection first lets the pure one-after-another of the now-sequence spring forth, then what springs from it, and is glimpsed as it were on its own in the ordinary reckoning of time, can essentially not be what would suffice to determine the full essence of time.

If, accordingly, it is to be decided about the "temporality" or timelessness of the I, the original essence of time as pure self-affection must be taken as the guiding thread. And everywhere that Kant, with full right, denies time-character to pure reason and to the I of pure apperception, he says merely that reason is not subject to the *form* of time.

Only in this sense does the deletion of the "at the same time" from the formulation of the principle of contradiction hold good. Kant argues thus: if the "at the same time," and with it "time," stood in the principle of contradiction, then the principle would be restricted to the being accessible in experience within time. But this principle regulates all thinking of any arbitrary something; hence time-determination has no place in it. A footnote registers that Kant himself wavered: the 1770 dissertation argues the opposite way, that the principle of contradiction itself presupposes time, since A and not-A conflict only when thought *simul* of the same thing — an argument Kant used there to prove the intuitive character of time, and one Mendelssohn contested in a letter.

Yet as certainly as the "at the same time" is a time-determination, so little need it mean the within-time-ness of beings. The "at the same time" expresses rather that time-character which belongs originally, as prior "recognition," as fore-forming, to all identification as such — and identification underlies the possibility as well as the impossibility of contradiction, founding both.

With his orientation to the non-original essence of time Kant had to deny time-character to the principle of contradiction; for it is contrary to sense to want to determine essentially, by means of a product derived from time, what is originally time itself. Precisely because the self in its innermost essence is originally time itself, the I cannot be grasped as "temporal," that is, here, as within time. Pure sensibility (time) and pure reason are not only homogeneous; they belong together in the unity of the same essence, which makes possible the finitude of human subjectivity in its wholeness. The book's central promise is thereby redeemed: a pure sensible reason is not a non-concept, because sensibility at its root is time and reason at its root is a self that is time.

#### **§ 35. Die Ursprünglichkeit des gelegten Grundes und das Problem der Metaphysik**

Kant's laying of the ground of metaphysics asks after the ground of the inner possibility of the essential unity of ontological knowledge. The ground it comes upon is the transcendental imagination, which presses forward as an in-between faculty against the initial positing of two basic sources of the mind. But the more original interpretation of this laid ground unveiled this in-between faculty not only as the originally unifying middle but that middle as the root of the two stems.

With that the way opened to the original source-ground of the two basic sources. Interpreting the transcendental imagination as root — illuminating how the pure synthesis lets the two stems grow out of itself and holds them — led of itself back into the rooting of this root: to original time. Only original time, as the original threefold-unifying forming of future, having-been and present, makes possible the "faculty" of pure synthesis, that is, what it is capable of: the unification of the three elements of ontological knowledge, in whose unity transcendence forms itself.

The modes of pure synthesis — pure apprehension, pure reproduction, pure recognition — are three in number not because they have a relation to the three elements of pure knowledge, but because, originally single in themselves, they make up in time-forming fashion the temporalizing of time itself. Only because these modes are originally single in the threefold-single time does the possibility of the original unification of the three elements of pure knowledge lie in them. And that is why the originally unifying, the seemingly merely mediating in-between faculty of the transcendental imagination, is nothing other than original time. This rooting in time is the sole thing by virtue of which the transcendental imagination can be the root of transcendence at all.

Original time makes possible the transcendental imagination, which is in itself essentially spontaneous receptivity and receptive spontaneity. Only in this unity can pure sensibility as spontaneous receptivity and pure apperception as receptive spontaneity belong together and form the unitary essence of a finite pure sensible reason.

If, however, the transcendental imagination is struck out as a basic faculty of its own, as happens in the second edition, and its function transferred to the understanding as mere spontaneity, then the possibility vanishes of grasping pure sensibility and pure thinking in respect of their unity in a finite human reason — indeed even of making that a problem. And because the transcendental imagination, on the ground of its unrendable original structure, opens the possibility of a laying of the ground of ontological knowledge and thereby of metaphysics, the first edition remains nearer the innermost drift of the problematic of such a grounding. With respect to this most central question of the whole work it therefore deserves, in principle, the preference over the second. Every reinterpretation of the pure imagination into a function of pure thinking — a reinterpretation that "German idealism," following the second edition, pushed still further — misjudges its specific essence. The verdict deferred at the end of §31 is now delivered, and the reason given is precisely the one the earlier § demanded: the imagination is preferred not because Kant named it but because only it lets the unity of a finite human reason be posed as a problem.

Original time lets the pure forming of transcendence happen. From the more original unveiling of the laid ground we now first understand, retrospectively, the innermost drift of the five stages of the laying of the ground and the significance ascribed to its core piece, the transcendental schematism.

Because transcendence temporalizes itself in original time, the ontological cognitions are "transcendental time-determinations."

This necessarily central function of time announces itself in Kant at first always only in the way that time is introduced as the universal form of all representing. But what remains decisive is the connection in which this happens. The "preliminary reminder" to the transcendental deduction is to show how far the three modes of pure synthesis are originally single in themselves. Kant does not manage to bring them to light expressly as time-forming and therefore as single in original time; nevertheless the fundamental function of time is emphasized precisely here, in the analysis of the second synthesis, the reproduction in imagination.

What makes up the "ground a priori of a necessary synthetic unity" of a possible representing bringing-back of the being to what is currently present? "One soon comes upon this, if one considers that appearances are not things in themselves but the mere play of our representations, which in the end come down to determinations of inner sense" (A 101).

Does that mean that the being is nothing in itself and dissolves into a playing of representing? By no means. Kant wants to say: the encountering of the being itself happens for a finite being in a representing whose pure representations of objectivity as such are played into one another. This being-played-into-one-another runs out to an end, that is, is so determined in advance that it can play itself out within a play-*space* at all. And this space forms itself through pure determinations of inner sense. But pure inner sense is pure self-affection, original time. What forms the horizon of transcendence are the pure schemata as transcendental time-determinations. The German lets *Spiel* in "play of our representations" and *Spielraum* meet, and the pun is doing real work: the "mere play" that idealist readers take for a reduction of beings to representations names instead the room in which beings can appear.

Because Kant saw the problem of the inner possibility of the essential unity of ontological knowledge in this perspective from the outset, and held fast to the central function of time, he could refrain from an express discussion of time when presenting the unity of transcendence on the two ways of the transcendental deduction.

Now it seems that in the second edition Kant takes back this transcendental primacy of time in the forming of transcendence along with the transcendental imagination, and so disowns the core piece of the laying of the ground, the transcendental schematism.

In the second edition a "General remark on the system of the principles" — that is, on the whole of ontological knowledge — is inserted (B 288 ff.). It begins: "it is something very remarkable that we cannot see the possibility of any thing according to the mere category, but must always have an intuition at hand in order to display in it the objective reality of the pure concept of the understanding." Here in brief words the essential necessity of the pure rendering-sensible of the notions, their presentation in a "pure image," is pronounced. But that this pure image is pure intuition as time is not said.

On the contrary, the next paragraph begins with clear reference to that sentence: "still more remarkable, however, is that in order to understand the possibility of things in accordance with the categories, and thus to establish the objective reality of the latter, we need not merely intuitions but always *outer* intuitions" (B 291). Here the transcendental function of space comes to appearance. It is unmistakable that Kant himself stands here before a new insight: space enters into the pure schematism. Nevertheless the schematism chapter is in no way altered in this sense in the second edition. Must it not still be concluded that the primacy of time has fallen? That conclusion would not only be premature; it would be a complete misinterpretation of the whole interpretation carried out so far to read out of this passage that time is not alone in originally forming transcendence.

But if, one might reply, transcendence is not to be grounded in time alone, then it is only consistent for Kant to strike out the pure imagination along with the limitation of time's primacy. In this consideration, however, it is forgotten that pure space as pure intuition is no less transcendentally rooted in the transcendental imagination than "time" is, so long as time is understood merely as what forms itself in pure intuition as what is purely intuited — the pure one-after-another of the now-sequence. With time so understood, space does indeed always and necessarily stand in a certain sense on a level.

But it is not as this formation that time is the original ground of transcendence; it is so as pure self-affection. And as such it is also the condition of the possibility of the representing forming, the making-manifest, of pure space. From the insight into the transcendental function of pure space, the rejection of the primacy of time by no means follows. What grows out of it is only the positive task of showing that in a certain corresponding way space too belongs to the self as finite, and that the self, on the ground of original time, is according to its essence "spatial." The concession is real and the task is left undone — the promissory note is one Heidegger never redeemed, and the spatiality of Dasein in *Sein und Zeit* §70, which grounds spatiality in temporality, is what he had at the time to offer for it.

The second edition's recognition that space too belongs in a certain way to the transcendental schematism only makes clear that the schematism cannot be grasped in its innermost essence so long as time is grasped only as the pure now-sequence. It must be understood as pure self-affection; otherwise its function in the forming of the schema lacks all intelligibility.

With this we come up against a peculiarity, and not an accidental one, of Kant's whole laying of the ground of metaphysics. Precisely what is unveiled in the regress to the source-ground does reveal itself in its transcendence-forming essence; but the faculties of the mind involved, and so the pure intuition time as well, are not expressly and primarily determined from this transcendental function. They give themselves, during the grounding and even at its close, still entirely in the provisional version of the first approach. Because Kant, in presenting the transcendental schematism, had no worked-out interpretation of the original essence of time ready, the illumination of the pure schemata as transcendental time-determinations had to remain so terse and opaque; for time taken as pure now-sequence offers no possible way at all to a "temporal" interpretation of the notions. The question deferred at §22 — whether it was only dryness and tedium that stopped Kant — is answered here: he broke off because the concept of time he had could not carry the analysis he needed.

If an interpretation merely renders what Kant expressly said, then it is from the outset no interpretation at all, insofar as the task of one remains to make visible what Kant brought to light in his laying of the ground beyond the express formulation. But that Kant himself could no longer say — as indeed in every philosophical knowledge what must become decisive is not what it says in its pronounced propositions but what it lays before our eyes, as still unsaid, through what is said.

So it was the basic intention of this interpretation of the *Critique of Pure Reason* to make the decisive content of the work visible by trying to exhibit what Kant "wanted to say." In this procedure the interpretation makes its own a maxim that Kant himself wanted applied to the interpretation of philosophical investigations, laid down at the close of a reply to the Leibnizian Eberhard's criticism: the *Critique of Pure Reason* may well be the proper apology for Leibniz, even against his admirers who exalt him with praise that does him no honour, as it can be for various older philosophers whom many a historian of philosophy, for all the praise given them, lets talk sheer nonsense, because he does not divine their intention — neglecting the key to all interpretations of pure products of reason from mere concepts, the critique of reason itself, and, in poring over the words of what they said, being unable to see what they *wanted to say*. Heidegger's licence for the whole book is thus taken from Kant, and taken from a text in which Kant claims to have understood Leibniz better than Leibniz understood himself. The *Kantbuch* does to Kant what Kant did to Leibniz, and says so.

To wring from what the words say what they want to say, every interpretation must necessarily use violence. But such violence cannot be roving arbitrariness. The power of an idea shining ahead must drive and guide the interpretation. Only by virtue of such an idea can an interpretation venture what is always presumptuous — to entrust itself to the concealed inner passion of a work, in order to be placed by it into the unsaid and forced to say it. And that is a way on which the guiding idea itself comes to light in its power to illuminate. The concession made in the preface to the second edition of the *Kantbuch* is made here in advance and turned into a method: violence is admitted, and the only defence offered against arbitrariness is the illuminating power of the result — the same criterion the Introduction set for the idea of fundamental ontology.

Kant's laying of the ground of metaphysics leads to the transcendental imagination. This is the root of the two stems, sensibility and understanding; as such it makes possible the original unity of the ontological synthesis. But this root is rooted in original time. The original ground becoming manifest in the laying of the ground is time.

Kant's laying of the ground of metaphysics sets in with *metaphysica generalis* and so becomes the question of the possibility of an ontology as such — which poses the question of the essence of the constitution of being of beings, that is, of being in general. A marginal note enters a reservation that reaches beyond the book: Kant is nevertheless driven in this by *metaphysica specialis*, by theology.

On the ground of time the laying of the ground of metaphysics grows. The question of being, the basic question of a laying of the ground of metaphysics, is the problem of "Being and Time."

This title contains the guiding idea of the foregoing interpretation of the *Critique of Pure Reason* as a laying of the ground of metaphysics. But the idea attested by this interpretation gives the prefiguring of the problem of a fundamental ontology. Fundamental ontology does not understand itself as something supposedly "new" over against something allegedly "old"; it is rather the expression of the attempt to appropriate originally what is essential in a laying of the ground of metaphysics — to help that grounding, by a retrieval, to its own more original possibility. The book's title-page thesis is stated only here, at the end of the Third Section, and it is stated as a result rather than a premise: *Sein und Zeit* is what the Kant interpretation was for, and the interpretation was to prove that the pairing of those two words is not Heidegger's invention but the unsaid of the *Critique*.

## **Vierter Abschnitt: Die Grundlegung der Metaphysik in einer Wiederholung**

By the retrieval of a fundamental problem we understand the disclosure of its original, hitherto concealed possibilities, through whose working-out the problem is transformed and only so preserved in its problem-content. But to preserve a problem means to keep it free and awake in those inner forces that make it possible as a problem in the ground of its essence.

Retrieval of the possible does not mean taking up what is current, what "there is well-founded prospect" of "making something of." This possible is always only the all too actual, which everyone handles in whatever business happens to be running. The possible in that sense is precisely what prevents a genuine retrieval and thereby any relation to history at all. Two senses of the possible are being separated, and the whole Fourth Section depends on the separation: the possible as the feasible, and the possible as what a work harboured and did not realize. Only the second can be retrieved, because only it is still ahead.

A rightly understood retrieval must nevertheless first have assured itself of what makes up the proper result of the earlier laying of the ground, here Kant's. And from what is sought as the "result" of the grounding in the *Critique of Pure Reason*, and from how what is found is determined, it must be possible to gather how far the understanding of the possible that guides all retrieval reaches, and whether it is equal to what can be retrieved.

### **A. Grundlegung der Metaphysik in der Anthropologie**

#### **§ 36. Der gelegte Grund und das Ergebnis der Kantischen Grundlegung der Metaphysik**

In running through the individual stages of Kant's laying of the ground it emerged how it finally comes upon the transcendental imagination as the ground of the inner possibility of the ontological synthesis, of transcendence. Is this establishing of the ground, or its more original interpretation as temporality, the result of Kant's grounding? Or does his grounding yield something else? For establishing that result the effort of laying the grounding before our eyes in its inner happening and the sequence of its steps would not have been needed; citing the relevant passages about the central function of the transcendental imagination in the deduction and the schematism would have sufficed. But if the result does not consist in the knowledge that the transcendental imagination makes up the ground, what else is the grounding to yield?

If the result of the laying of the ground does not lie in its "result," then it must be asked what the grounding, in its happening as such, reveals for the problem of a grounding of metaphysics. What happens in Kant's laying of the ground? Nothing less than this: the grounding of the inner possibility of ontology accomplishes itself as an unveiling of transcendence, that is, of the subjectivity of the human subject.

The question of the essence of metaphysics is the question of the unity of the basic faculties of the human "mind." Kant's laying of the ground yields: the grounding of metaphysics is a questioning about the human being — anthropology. The conclusion is stated flatly, and it is a trap; the whole of part A exists to show why it cannot be taken as it stands.

But did the regress to Kant's anthropology not fail at the first attempt to grasp his grounding more originally? Certainly, insofar as it showed that what the anthropology offers by way of an interpretation of knowledge and its two sources is brought to light in a more original shape by the *Critique* itself. But from that it now only follows that the anthropology Kant worked out is an empirical one and not one sufficient for the transcendental problematic, not a pure one. And that precisely sharpens the demand for an adequate, that is, "philosophical anthropology" for the purposes of a grounding of metaphysics.

That the result of Kant's grounding lies in the insight into the necessary connection between anthropology and metaphysics can even be documented unambiguously from Kant's own statements. His laying of the ground aims at a grounding of the "metaphysics in its final purpose," *metaphysica specialis*, to which the three disciplines cosmology, psychology and theology belong. But the grounding must, as a critique of pure reason, understand pure reason in its innermost essence, if metaphysics as a "natural disposition of the human being" is to be grasped in its possibility and limit. And the innermost essence of human reason announces itself in the interests that move it at all times as human. "All interest of my reason, the speculative as well as the practical, unites in the following three questions: what can I know? what ought I to do? what may I hope?" (A 804 f., B 832 f.). A marginal note names the doctrine at work: philosophy as *teleologia rationis humanae*.

These three questions are the ones to which the three disciplines of *metaphysica specialis* are assigned. Human knowing concerns nature in the widest sense of what is at hand (cosmology); doing is the human being's acting and concerns his personality and freedom (psychology); hoping aims at immortality as blessedness, at union with God (theology). Heidegger's own marginal note calls the assignment "askew," since freedom belongs to cosmology, being thought from causality — a correction worth noting, since the neat mapping of the three questions onto the three disciplines is doing argumentative work here.

These three original interests determine the human being not as a natural being but as a "citizen of the world." They make up the object of philosophy "in cosmopolitan intent," the domain of proper philosophy. Hence Kant says in the introduction to his logic lectures, where he develops the concept of philosophy as such: "the field of philosophy in this cosmopolitan meaning can be brought to the following questions: 1. What can I know? 2. What ought I to do? 3. What may I hope? 4. What is the human being?"

Here a fourth question appears alongside the three named. But is this fourth question about the human being not externally appended to the first three, and moreover superfluous, if one considers that *psychologia rationalis*, as a discipline of *metaphysica specialis*, already treats of the human being?

Kant did not simply patch the fourth question onto the first three. He says: "at bottom one could reckon all of this to anthropology, because the first three questions relate to the last."

With that Kant himself has unambiguously pronounced the proper result of his laying of the ground of metaphysics, and the attempt at a retrieval has thereby received a clear directive for its task. Kant speaks only of anthropology in general; but after what was discussed above it is beyond doubt that only a philosophical anthropology can take over the grounding of proper philosophy, of *metaphysica specialis*. Will the retrieval of Kant's grounding therefore not have to pursue the systematic working-out of a "philosophical anthropology" as its proper task, and hence first determine the idea of one?

#### **§ 37. Die Idee einer philosophischen Anthropologie**

What belongs to a philosophical anthropology? What is anthropology at all, and through what does it become philosophical? Anthropology means knowledge of the human being. It comprises everything investigable concerning the nature of the human being as this bodily-psychical-spiritual being. Into its domain fall not only the properties of the human being establishable as at hand, as this determinate species distinguished from animal and plant, but also his hidden dispositions, the differences of character, race and sex. And since the human being does not merely occur as a natural being but acts and creates, anthropology must also try to grasp what he as acting "makes of himself," can and ought to make. His ability and his ought rest in the end on basic positions the human being as such can take up, which we call "world-views," and whose "psychology" embraces the whole of the knowledge of the human being.

What flows together in anthropology as somatic, biological and psychological consideration of the human being, as characterology, psychoanalysis, ethnology, pedagogical psychology, culture-morphology and typology of world-views, is not only unsurveyable in content but above all fundamentally different in kind of questioning, in claim to grounding, in intention of presentation and form of communication, and finally in guiding presuppositions. And insofar as all of this, and ultimately the whole of beings, can in some way be related to the human being and accordingly reckoned to anthropology, anthropology becomes so comprehensive that its idea sinks into complete indeterminacy.

Anthropology today has for a long time not been merely the title of a discipline; the word designates a basic tendency of the present position of the human being toward himself and within the whole of beings. On this basic position something is known and understood only when it has found an anthropological explanation. Anthropology seeks not only the truth about the human being but now claims the decision about what truth as such can mean. The claim is what makes anthropology a metaphysical position rather than a science, and it is why refuting it requires more than pointing out its indeterminacy.

No age has known so much and so manifold about the human being as the present. No age has presented its knowledge of the human being in so penetrating and captivating a way. No age has been able to offer this knowledge so quickly and easily. But also no age knew less what the human being is than the present. To no age has the human being become so questionworthy as to ours. The four sentences are the most quoted in the book, and their structure is the argument: the accumulation of knowledge and the loss of the question are the same event, because knowing more about a being is not asking after its manner of being.

But is not precisely this breadth and unrest of anthropological questioning suited to let a philosophical anthropology arise and to lend the efforts toward one a particular force? Is not the discipline on which the whole of philosophy must concentrate won with the idea of a philosophical anthropology?

Max Scheler spoke of this philosophical anthropology years ago: "in a certain sense all central problems of philosophy can be led back to the question what the human being is and what metaphysical place and position he occupies within the whole of being, the world and God." But Scheler also saw with particular sharpness that the manifold of determinations about the essence of the human being cannot simply be packed together into a common definition: "the human being is so broad, so colourful, so manifold a thing that the definitions all turn out a little short. He has too many ends." So Scheler's effort, intensified in his last years and set going with new fruitfulness, was directed not only at winning a unitary idea of the human being but just as much at working out the essential difficulties and entanglements of that task. The book is dedicated to Scheler's memory, and this is where the debt is discharged: the position is treated as the most serious form of the idea being criticized, and its author is credited with having seen the difficulty best.

But perhaps the fundamental difficulty of a philosophical anthropology lies not first in the task of winning the systematic unity of the essential determinations of this manifold being, but in its very concept — a difficulty about which even the richest and loudest anthropological knowledge can no longer deceive.

Through what does an anthropology become philosophical at all? Is it only that its cognitions differ from those of an empirical anthropology in degree of generality — where it remains constantly questionable at which degree of generality empirical knowledge stops and philosophical knowledge begins?

An anthropology can certainly be called philosophical insofar as its method is philosophical, in the sense of an essential consideration of the human being. That aims at distinguishing the being we call human from plant and animal and the remaining regions of beings, and thereby at working out the specific essential constitution of this determinate region. Philosophical anthropology then becomes a regional ontology of the human being and remains as such coordinate with the other ontologies that share out the whole domain of beings with it. So understood, philosophical anthropology is not without further ado, and above all not on the ground of the inner structure of its problematic, the centre of philosophy.

But anthropology can also be philosophical insofar as it as anthropology determines either the goal of philosophy or its starting point, or both at once. If the goal of philosophy lies in working out a world-view, then an anthropology will have to delimit the "position of the human being in the cosmos." And if the human being counts as that being which, in the order of grounding an absolutely certain knowledge, is the absolutely first given and most certain, then the construction of philosophy so planned must place human subjectivity in the central starting point. The first task can unite with the second, and both, as anthropological considerations, can make use of the method and results of a regional ontology of the human being.

But precisely from these several possibilities of delimiting the philosophical character of an anthropology the indeterminacy of the idea already follows. And it increases when the multiplicity of empirical-anthropological cognitions is kept in view, which underlies every philosophical anthropology at least at its starting point.

As natural and self-evident as the idea of a philosophical anthropology may be despite its ambiguity, and as irresistibly as it keeps asserting itself, so inevitably is "anthropologism" in philosophy fought against again and again. The idea of philosophical anthropology is not only insufficiently determined; its function within the whole of philosophy remains unclarified and undecided.

This deficiency has its ground in the inner limit of the idea. For the idea is not itself expressly grounded from the essence of philosophy; it is posited with a view to the goal of philosophy, grasped at first externally, and to philosophy's possible starting point. So the determination of this idea ends in anthropology's presenting a possible collecting-basin for the central philosophical problems — a characterization whose externality and philosophical questionableness leaps to the eye.

But if anthropology does in a certain way gather all the central problems of philosophy into itself, why can these be led back to the question what the human being is? Can they be led back only if one has the idea of undertaking it, or must they be led back to it? And if they must, where lies the ground of this necessity? Perhaps in the fact that the central problems of philosophy come from the human being — not merely in the sense that the human being poses them, but so that in their inner content they have a relation to him? But how far are all central philosophical problems at home in the essence of the human being? What are the central problems at all, and where lies their centre? What does philosophizing mean, that its problematic has such a centre at home in the essence of the human being? The question at issue is not whether anthropology is central but what would have to be true for it to be central; and no anthropology can answer that, because the answer would have to come from the essence of philosophy.

So long as these questions are not unrolled and determined in their inner systematics, not even the inner limit of the idea of a philosophical anthropology becomes visible. Without discussing them the ground for deciding about the essence, right and function of a philosophical anthropology within philosophy is altogether lacking.

Again and again attempts at a philosophical anthropology will offer themselves in intelligible arguments and be able to assert the central position of this discipline, without grounding it from the essence of philosophy. And again and again the opponents of anthropology will be able to appeal to the point that the human being does not belong in the centre of beings but that "beside" him there is a "sea" of beings — a rejection of anthropology's central position that is no more philosophical than the assertion of it. Realism and anthropologism are dismissed together, and dismissed by the same criterion: neither derives its claim about the human being's place from what philosophy is.

So a critical reflection on the idea of a philosophical anthropology yields not only its indeterminacy and its inner limit; above all it makes clear that ground and framework are altogether lacking for a fundamental question about its essence.

It would therefore be premature to grasp Kant's fourth question as an anthropological one and to hand the grounding of metaphysics over to a philosophical anthropology, merely because Kant leads the three questions of proper metaphysics back to the fourth, what the human being is. Anthropology does not ground metaphysics simply because it is anthropology.

But was the proper result of Kant's grounding not precisely this connection of the question about the essence of the human being with the grounding of metaphysics? Must this connection not guide the task of a grounding to be retrieved?

The critique of the idea of philosophical anthropology shows, however, that it does not suffice simply to pose the fourth question. On the contrary, the indeterminacy of this question indicates that even now the decisive result of Kant's laying of the ground has not been taken into possession.

#### **§ 38. Die Frage nach dem Wesen des Menschen und das eigentliche Ergebnis der Kantischen Grundlegung**

More and more it shows that we do not come nearer the proper result of Kant's grounding so long as we hold to any definition or formulated thesis. We come near Kant's proper philosophizing only if, still more resolutely than hitherto, we ask not after what Kant *says* but after what *happens* in his laying of the ground. Solely at laying free this happening did the more original interpretation carried out above aim.

What, then, has actually emerged in the happening of Kant's grounding? Not that the transcendental imagination is the laid ground, nor that this grounding becomes a question about the essence of human reason, but that Kant, in unveiling the subjectivity of the subject, shrinks back before the ground he himself laid. The result of the book is thus not a doctrine but an event within Kant's text, and the shrinking back — treated in §31 as a failure — is now converted into the finding.

Does this shrinking back not belong to the result too? What happens in it? An inconsistency, perhaps, to be charged against Kant? Is the shrinking back and the not-going-to-the-end only something negative? By no means. It makes manifest rather that Kant in his laying of the ground digs away from under himself the very ground on which he had initially placed the critique. The concept of pure reason, and the unity of a pure sensible reason, become a problem. The questioning into the subjectivity of the subject, the "subjective deduction," leads into darkness. Kant does not appeal to his anthropology not only because it is empirical and not pure, but because in carrying out the grounding, and through the grounding itself, the *manner* of questioning about the human being becomes questionable.

It is not the answer to the question what the human being is that must be sought; what matters is first to ask how, in a grounding of metaphysics, the human being can and must be asked after at all.

The questionability of the questioning about the human being is the problematic pressing to light in the happening of Kant's laying of the ground. And only now does it show: Kant's shrinking back before the ground he himself unveiled, the transcendental imagination — a shrinking back with a view to saving pure reason, to holding fast his own ground — is that movement of philosophizing which reveals the breaking-in of the ground and thereby the abyss of metaphysics.

Only from this result does the original interpretation carried out above gain its right and the grounding of its necessity. Not an empty pressing toward the more original, not wanting to know better, but solely the task of laying free the innermost drift of the laying of the ground, and thereby its ownmost problematic, guided all the efforts of the interpretation.

But if the laying of the ground neither pushes aside the question what the human being is nor gives it a round answer, but first makes it visible in its questionability, how then does it stand with Kant's fourth question, to which *metaphysica specialis* and thereby proper philosophizing are to be led back?

We shall be able to pose this fourth question as it wants to be posed only if we work it out as a question from the now-won understanding of the result of the grounding, and renounce a premature answer.

It must be asked: why can the three questions be "related" to the fourth? Why "could one reckon all of this to anthropology"? What is common to these three questions, in what respect are they unitary, such that they can be led back to the one fourth? And how must this fourth question itself question, so that it can take those three up unitarily into itself and bear them?

The innermost interest of human reason unites the three questions in itself. In them a can, an ought and a may of human reason stand in question.

Where a can is questionable and wants to delimit itself in its possibilities, it stands already within a not-being-able. An omnipotent being does not need to ask: what can I, that is, what can I not? It not only does not need to ask so; by its essence it cannot pose this question at all. But this not-being-able is no lack but the untouchedness by every lack and every "not." Whoever asks "what can I?" thereby announces a finitude. And whatever is moved in its innermost interest by this question reveals a finitude in the innermost of its essence.

Where an ought is questionable, the questioning being hovers between yes and no; it is driven about by what it ought not. A being fundamentally interested in an ought knows itself in a not-yet-having-fulfilled, and in such a way that what it ought at all is questionable to it. This not-yet of a fulfilment itself still undetermined makes known that a being whose innermost interest stands with an ought is at bottom finite.

Where a may becomes questionable, what is granted to the questioner or withheld from him reaches in. What is asked after is what can be placed in expectation and what cannot. But all expecting reveals a want. And if this neediness grows out of the innermost interest of human reason, then that reason attests itself as essentially finite. Kant's three questions are thus read not for their content but for their form: each asks after a limit, and asking after a limit is possible only for what has one.

But human reason does not merely betray finitude in these questions; its innermost interest goes to finitude itself. It is concerned not with removing the can, the ought and the may — with extinguishing finitude — but conversely with becoming certain of this finitude, in order to hold itself in it.

Finitude therefore does not simply hang on pure human reason: its finitude is a becoming-finite, a "care" about being-able-to-be-finite. From this follows: human reason is not only finite because it poses the three questions; conversely, it poses these questions because it is finite, and so finite that in its being-reason it is concerned with this finitude itself. And because these three questions ask after this one thing, finitude, they "can be related" to the fourth: what is the human being? The word *Sorge* enters here in quotation marks, imported from *Sein und Zeit*; finitude is not a state but something a finite being has to be, and that is what makes it the object of the three interests rather than their limit.

But the three questions can not only be related to the fourth; they are in themselves nothing other than this question, that is, they must by their essence be related to it. Yet this relation is essentially necessary only if the fourth question has given up the generality and indeterminacy nearest to hand and has been brought to that univocity according to which what is asked after in it is the finitude *in* the human being.

As such a question it is not merely rightly subordinated to the first three; it changes into the first, which releases the other three out of itself.

But with this result, despite all the determinacy now given to the question about the human being — and precisely through it — the problem of this question first sharpens. It becomes questionable what kind of question about the human being this question is, and whether it can be an anthropological question at all. Only now does the result of Kant's laying of the ground clarify itself with the sharpness that lets a more original possibility of retrieval become visible in it.

The laying of the ground of metaphysics is grounded in the question about the finitude in the human being, and in such a way that this finitude can now first become a problem. The grounding is an "analytic," a dissolving of our — that is, finite — knowledge into its elements. Kant calls it a "study of our inner nature." But this study is not an arbitrary, directionless questioning about the human being, and is "even a duty for the philosopher," only if the problematic essentially guiding it is grasped originally and comprehensively enough, and if from there the "inner nature" of "our" self is made a problem as the finitude in the human being.

"Philosophical anthropology" may bring in ever so much and ever so essential knowledge about the human being; it can never place itself in the right of a fundamental discipline of philosophy merely because it is anthropology. On the contrary, it harbours in itself the constant danger that the necessity remains covered over of first developing the question about the human being, with a view to a grounding of metaphysics, as a question.

That and how "philosophical anthropology" nevertheless presents a task of its own kind — outside the problem of a grounding of metaphysics — cannot be discussed here. The refusal is not a dismissal: anthropology is denied the foundational role and left its own legitimacy, which is exactly the distinction that the Introduction's demarcation of fundamental ontology from all anthropology required.

### **B. Das Problem der Endlichkeit im Menschen und die Metaphysik des Daseins**

It was in order to bring this fundamental problem to light — the necessity of the question about the finitude in the human being with a view to a grounding of metaphysics — that the preceding interpretation of the *Critique of Pure Reason* was undertaken. Accordingly finitude was what had to be brought to mind in advance at the outset of the interpretation and then constantly during its execution. If Kant in his laying of the ground digs the previously posited ground away from under himself, that now means: what was lifted out at the beginning of the interpretation as Kant's unspoken "presuppositions" — the essence of knowledge and its finitude — gains the character of the decisive problem. Finitude, and the peculiarity of the question about it, first decide from the ground up about the inner form of a transcendental "analytic" of the subjectivity of the subject.

#### **§ 39. Das Problem einer möglichen Bestimmung der Endlichkeit im Menschen**

How is the finitude in the human being to be asked after? Is that a serious problem at all? Does human finitude not lie before us a thousandfold, everywhere and at all times?

It might seem enough to name what is finite in the human being, to cite any of his imperfections. On that path we win at most evidence that the human being is a finite being. But we learn neither in what the essence of his finitude consists, nor how this finitude determines the human being as the being he is from the ground up and as a whole.

Even if one could succeed in summing all human imperfections and abstracting what is common to them, nothing of the essence of finitude would be grasped, because it remains questionable in advance whether the imperfections of the human being let his finitude be seen immediately at all, or whether they are not rather remote factual consequences of the essence of his finitude and therefore intelligible only from it. And if even the impossible were possible — to demonstrate rationally that the human being is created — then characterizing him as *ens creatum* would again only prove the fact of his finitude, not exhibit its essence and determine that essence as the basic constitution of the being of the human being. So it is not at all self-evident how the question about the finitude in the human being — the most everyday announcement of his essence — is to be posed. The investigation so far has yielded only this one thing: the present question about finitude is no arbitrary investigation of human properties. It grows out of the task of the laying of the ground of metaphysics, and is demanded by that task itself as a basic question. Accordingly the problematic of the grounding must contain in itself the directive to the direction in which the question about the finitude of the human being has to move. Creation is the traditional route to human finitude and it is refused, because it establishes finitude as a fact about a being rather than as that being's manner of being; and with it goes every theological grounding of the question.

And if the task of a grounding of metaphysics admits of a more original retrieval, then through that retrieval the essential connection between the grounding-problem and the question about finitude that it guides must come to light more clearly and sharply.

Kant's laying of the ground of metaphysics set in with the grounding of what underlies proper metaphysics, *metaphysica specialis*: with the grounding of *metaphysica generalis*. But this, as "ontology," is already the form, hardened into a discipline, of what in antiquity, last in Aristotle, remained standing as a problem of *prōtē philosophia*, of proper philosophizing. The question about the *on hē on*, beings as such, there holds itself in an admittedly dark connection with the question about beings as a whole, the divine. The title "metaphysics" designates a problem-concept in which not only the two basic directions of questioning about beings but at the same time their possible unity is questionable. And it is here still left aside whether the two directions of questioning exhaust the whole problematic of a fundamental knowledge of beings at all.

If the question about the finitude in the human being is to determine itself out of a more original retrieval of the grounding of metaphysics, then Kant's question must be twisted out of the orientation to the hardened discipline and systematics of school metaphysics and transposed into the free field of its own problematic. In that lies too that the Aristotelian formulation of the question cannot be taken over as something finished.

With the *ti to on* the question about beings is indeed posed; but to pose a question does not yet mean to master the problematic lying in it and to work it out. How far the problem of metaphysics remains veiled in the question *ti to on* can be recognized from the fact that from this question it cannot be gathered at all how the problem of the finitude in the human being lies enclosed within it, insofar as it is to be grasped as a problem. Still less is a directive to be won, merely by pronouncing and repeating this question, for how the finitude in the human being is to be asked after. Retrieval of the problem of grounding *metaphysica generalis* therefore does not mean repeating after Aristotle the question what beings as such are. The retrieval must develop this question — which we call briefly the question of being — into a problem. And this development has to show how far the problem of the finitude in the human being, and the investigations prefigured by it, belong necessarily to the mastering of the question of being. Put fundamentally: the essential connection between being as such — not beings — and the finitude in the human being must be raised to light.

#### **§ 40. Die ursprüngliche Ausarbeitung der Seinsfrage als Weg zum Problem der Endlichkeit im Menschen**

The fundamental question of the old *physiologoi* about beings in general — about the *logos* of *physis* — developed, and this is the inner development of ancient metaphysics from its beginnings to Aristotle, out of the indeterminacy and fullness of its initial universality into the determinacy of the two directions of questioning that according to Aristotle make up proper philosophizing.

However dark their connection remains, in one respect a rank-order between the two can be lifted out. Insofar as the question about beings as a whole and in their principal regions already presupposes a certain grasp of what beings as such are, the question about the *on hē on* must be ordered before the question about beings as a whole. The question what beings in general as such are is the first in the order of the possible execution of a fundamental knowledge of beings as a whole. Whether this primacy also falls to it in the order of the decisive self-grounding of metaphysics is a question here only to be named. The reservation is not idle: the marginal note at §35 said that Kant is driven throughout by theology, and the question left open here is whether *metaphysica specialis*, not general ontology, is what the grounding really answers to.

But is the general question *ti to on* not so indeterminate that it asks nothing at all any more and refuses every hold for where and how an answer to it is to be sought?

In the question what beings as such are, what is asked after is what determines beings as beings at all. We call it the being of beings, and the question about it the question of being. It investigates what determines beings as such. This determining is to be known in the how of its determining, interpreted as this and that, that is, grasped. But to be able to grasp the essential determinedness of beings by being, the determining itself must be sufficiently graspable: being as such, and not first beings as such, must beforehand be grasped. So in the question *ti to on* there lies the more original question: what does the being already pre-understood in that question mean?

If the question *ti to on* is unwieldy enough, how is the more original and yet "more abstract" one to let a concrete problematic spring forth?

That such a problematic is present is documented by a pointer to something taken all too self-evidently in philosophy from of old. In every kind of comportment toward the beings manifest to us we determine and interrogate them, first, with a view to their what-being (*ti estin*). This what-being philosophy calls *essentia*, essence. It makes a being possible in what it is; hence for the thing-hood of a thing (*realitas*) the designation *possibilitas*, inner possibility, also stands. To the question what a being is, its look (*eidos*) gives information, and the what-being of a being is therefore called *idea*.

At every being, second, the question awakens — or is always already answered — whether it, the being of this determinate what-being, is, or is rather not. We determine beings accordingly also with respect to their "that-being" (*hoti estin*), which philosophy usually grasps terminologically as *existentia*, actuality.

At every being there thus "is" what-being and that-being, *essentia* and *existentia*, possibility and actuality. Does "being" mean the same in each case here? If not, what is it due to that being is split into what-being and that-being? Is this all too self-evidently gathered distinction there the way there are dogs and also cats, or does a problem lie here that must finally be posed — one that evidently can only be posed if it is asked what being as such is? The most familiar distinction in the whole of metaphysics is turned into a question by the simplest means: by asking what entitles the tradition to treat a split within being as a fact about beings.

Without working out this question, is not every horizon lacking for the attempt to "define" the essence of essence and to "explain" the actuality of the actual? And is not the meaning of being as being-true always intertwined with this articulation of being into what-being and that-being — an articulation dark as regards its ground of possibility and the kind of its necessity — a being-true that comes to light palpably in every "is" of every spoken and unspoken sentence, and not only there?

Is what lies enclosed in the problem-word "being" not already more than enough, and too weighty? May the indeterminacy of the question of being persist any longer, or must a still more original step toward working out this question be ventured?

How is the question "what does being mean?" to find its answer if it remains dark whence the answer can be expected at all? Must it not first be asked toward what we take our look, in order to determine being as such from there and so win a concept of being from which the possibility and the necessity of the essential articulation of being become intelligible? So the question of "first philosophy," what beings as such are, must be driven back, by way of the question what being as such is, to the still more original one: *from where* is anything like being — and with the whole richness of the articulations and relations enclosed in it — to be grasped at all?

If then an inner connection obtains between the laying of the ground of metaphysics and the question about the finitude in the human being, the more original working-out of the question of being now reached will announce its essential relation to the problem of finitude more elementally.

At first, however, that relation remains opaque, especially as one may not be inclined to expect any such relation of the question just unrolled. It may be present in the Kantian questions named — what may I hope, and the rest. But how is the question of being, and in its now-developed shape as a question about the possibility of grasping being at all, to have an essential relation to the finitude in the human being? The question of being may gain a sense within the abstract ontology of a metaphysics oriented by Aristotle and so claim the right of a learned, more or less wilful special problem; but an essential relation to the finitude in the human being is not evident.

Yet if the original shape of the being-problem has so far been clarified in orientation to the Aristotelian question, that does not say that the *origin* of this problem lies there. On the contrary: proper philosophizing can come upon the question of being only if this question belongs to the innermost essence of philosophy, which itself only is as a decisive possibility of human Dasein. A marginal note tightens the modality: a conditioned necessity. Philosophy is not a permanent human capacity but something a finite being may or may not take up, and the question of being is bound to that taking-up.

When the possibility of grasping something like being is asked after, this "being" is not devised and forced into a problem, perhaps in order to take up again a question of the philosophical tradition. What is asked after is the possibility of grasping what we all as human beings already and constantly understand. The question of being, as a question about the possibility of the concept of being, springs for its part from the pre-conceptual understanding of being. So the question about the possibility of the concept of being is driven back one step further, to the question about the essence of the understanding of being as such. The more originally grasped task of a grounding of metaphysics accordingly transforms itself into the illumination of the inner possibility of the understanding of being. And working out the question of being so grasped first brings the decision whether and in what way the being-problem carries an inner relation to the finitude in the human being. A marginal note at this transformation is severely self-critical: "quite relapsing into the sense of the transcendental formulation of the question." The later Heidegger's objection to his own book is registered in its own margins — that asking after the *possibility* of the understanding of being still asks in Kant's manner, from the side of the being that understands.

#### **§ 41. Das Seinsverständnis und das Dasein im Menschen**

That we human beings comport ourselves toward beings is evident. Set the task of representing beings, we can at any time cite arbitrary beings: what we are not and what is not our like; what we ourselves are; and what we are not ourselves but which is nevertheless, as a self, our like. Beings are familiar to us — but being? Does dizziness not befall us when we are to determine it, even only expressly grasp it? Is being not something like nothing? Indeed no less a figure than Hegel said: "pure being and pure nothing are therefore the same."

With the question about being as such we venture to the edge of complete darkness. But we must not evade prematurely; rather we must bring the full peculiarity of the understanding of being nearer to ourselves. For as impenetrable as the darkness lying over being and its meaning is, so certain does it remain that we at all times and in the whole field of the manifestness of beings understand something like being — that we concern ourselves with the what- and how-being of beings, experience and dispute their that-being, decide about the being-true of beings and miss it. In every uttering of a sentence, for instance "today is a holiday," we understand the "is" and thereby something like being.

In the cry "Fire!" there lies: fire has broken out, help is needed, let him save himself — bring his own being to safety — who can. But even when we do not express ourselves about beings and comport ourselves toward them silently, we understand their characters of what-being, that-being and being-true, played into one another though veiled.

In every mood, in which "one feels this way or that," our Da-sein becomes manifest to us. So we understand being and yet lack the concept of it. This pre-conceptual understanding of being is, for all its constancy and breadth, mostly quite indeterminate. The specific manner of being of material things, of plants, animals, human beings, numbers is familiar to us, but this familiar is as such uncognized. More: the being of beings, pre-conceptually understood in its whole breadth, constancy and indeterminacy, gives itself in complete unquestionableness. Being as such becomes so little a question that it looks as though there "were" no such thing. Mood is admitted as a mode of the manifestness of Dasein alongside assertion and silent comportment — the one place in the book where the doctrine of *Befindlichkeit* [attunement, finding-oneself] from *Sein und Zeit* surfaces, and it is doing evidential work: being is understood even where nothing is said.

The understanding of being so marked out holds itself in the undisturbed and unendangered plane of the purest self-evidence. And yet, did the understanding of being not happen, the human being could never be as the being he is, however wonderful the faculties with which he might be equipped. The human being is a being that is in the midst of beings, and in such a way that the beings he is not, and the being he himself is, have always already become manifest to him. This manner of being of the human being we call *Existenz*. Only on the ground of the understanding of being is existence possible. A marginal note reaches decades ahead: "history as the destiny of Ereignis" — the later name for what happens when being is understood at all.

In comporting himself toward the beings he is not himself, the human being finds beings already there as that by which he is borne, on which he is dependent, and of which at bottom, for all culture and technology, he can never become master. Dependent on the beings he is not, he is at the same time at bottom not master of the being he himself is on each occasion. The double powerlessness is exact: neither the world nor the self is at our disposal, and the second is the more important, since it is what makes existence a burden rather than a limitation.

With the existence of the human being a break-in into the whole of beings happens, such that only now do beings become manifest at themselves, that is, as beings — in varying breadth, at different levels of clarity, in different degrees of security. But this privilege — not merely to be at hand among other beings, without these ever becoming manifest to one another as such, but to be delivered over to beings in the midst of them as such and handed over to oneself as a being — this privilege of existing harbours in itself the *need* of requiring the understanding of being. Privilege and need are one; that is the point on which the whole Fourth Section turns. The distinction of the human being is not a capacity added to his being but a destitution, and it is why the metaphysics of Dasein cannot be a doctrine of human excellence.

The human being would not be able to be the thrown being as a self if he could not let beings as such *be* at all. But to be able to let beings be what and how they are, the existing being must always already have projected what it encounters upon its being a being. Existence means dependence on beings as such in the being-handed-over to the beings on which one is so dependent.

Existence as a manner of being is in itself finitude, and as this it is possible only on the ground of the understanding of being. Anything like being "is there," and must be, only where finitude has become existent. So the understanding of being, which uncognized in its breadth, constancy, indeterminacy and unquestionableness governs human existence throughout, reveals itself as the innermost ground of his finitude. The understanding of being does not have the harmless generality of a frequently occurring property of the human being alongside many others; its "generality" is the originality of the innermost ground of the finitude of Dasein. Only because the understanding of being is the most finite in the finite can it also make possible the so-called "creative" capacities of the finite human essence. And only because it happens in the ground of finitude does it have the marked breadth and constancy — and also concealment. The paradox of the Second Section is finally resolved here: the "creative" character of ontological knowledge does not burst finitude, because what makes it possible is the neediness of a being that must understand being in order to be at all.

On the ground of the understanding of being the human being is the Da, the there, with whose being the opening break-in into beings happens, so that beings can announce themselves as such for a self. More original than the human being is the finitude of the Dasein in him. The sentence is the book's answer to the question "what is the human being?" and it answers by displacing the subject of the question: Dasein is not another word for the human being but what in the human being makes him possible, so that the question cannot be anthropological at all.

The working-out of the basic question of *metaphysica generalis*, the *ti to on*, was thrown back onto the more original idea of the inner essence of the understanding of being, which first bears, drives and steers all explicit questioning about the concept of being. But the more original grasp of the fundamental problem of metaphysics was striven for in order to make visible the connection of the grounding-problem with the question about the finitude in the human being. And now it shows: we need not first ask after a relation of the understanding of being to the finitude in the human being — the understanding of being *is* the innermost essence of finitude. With that we have won the concept of finitude that underlies a problematic of the grounding of metaphysics. And if this grounding rests on the question what the human being is, the questionability of that question is now removed at a first level: the question about the human being has gained in determinacy.

If the human being is human only on the ground of the Dasein in him, then the question about what is more original than the human being can in principle be no anthropological one. All anthropology, the philosophical included, has already posited the human being as human. Two marginal notes sharpen the point: the being in question is *ex-sistente*, and Da-sein is nothing "being" in the ontic sense. The demarcation announced in the Introduction is thus finally earned, and earned not by ranking fundamental ontology above anthropology but by showing that its theme is not a being at all.

The problem of the laying of the ground of metaphysics finds its root in the question about the Dasein in the human being — about its innermost ground, about the understanding of being as essentially existent finitude. This question about Dasein asks of what essence the being so determined is; and insofar as its essence lies in existence, the question about the essence of Dasein is the *existential* one. But every question about the being of a being, and above all the question about the being of that being to whose constitution of being finitude as understanding of being belongs, is metaphysics.

Accordingly the laying of the ground of metaphysics is grounded in a metaphysics of Dasein. Is it to be wondered at that a laying of the ground of metaphysics must at least itself be metaphysics — and a distinguished one?

Kant, in whose philosophizing the problem of the possibility of metaphysics was as awake as never before and not again since, would have understood far too little of his innermost willing had this connection not lit up for him. In the brightness and calm that the completion of the *Critique of Pure Reason* immediately granted him he pronounced it, writing in 1781 to his friend and student Marcus Herz: "this kind of investigation will always remain difficult. For it contains the metaphysics of metaphysics."

This word strikes down finally every attempt to find even partially an "epistemology" in the *Critique of Pure Reason*; but it also obliges every retrieval of a laying of the ground of metaphysics to come clear about this "metaphysics of metaphysics" far enough that it can be brought onto a concrete ground granting the happening of the grounding a possible path. The phrase from a private letter is made to carry the whole reading, and it does the work of the title: a *metaphysics of metaphysics* is exactly what "Kant and the problem of metaphysics" names, where the ambiguity of the genitive — announced in the preface to the third edition — is the point.

### **C. Die Metaphysik des Daseins als Fundamentalontologie**

No anthropology that still understands its own questioning and that questioning's presuppositions can claim even to develop the problem of a laying of the ground of metaphysics, let alone to carry it out. The question necessary for such a grounding — what the human being is — is taken over by the metaphysics of Dasein.

The expression is ambiguous in a positive sense. The metaphysics of Dasein is not only metaphysics *about* Dasein; it is the metaphysics that necessarily *happens as* Dasein. And in that lies: it can never become a metaphysics "about" Dasein the way zoology treats of animals. The metaphysics of Dasein is no fixed and ready "organon" at all; it must at all times form itself anew, transforming its own idea, in the working-out of the possibility of metaphysics.

In its destiny it remains bound to the concealed happening of metaphysics in Dasein itself, by virtue of which the human being first counts, or forgets, the day and hour, the years and centuries of his attempts.

The inner requirements of a metaphysics of Dasein and the difficulty of determining it Kant's attempt has sufficiently shown. But its ownmost result, rightly understood, lies precisely in the unveiling of the problem-connection between the question about the possibility of the ontological synthesis and the unveiling of the finitude in the human being — that is, in the demand for a reflection on how a metaphysics of Dasein is to be concretely realized.

#### **§ 42. Die Idee einer Fundamentalontologie**

The laying of the ground of metaphysics must, in the setting of its task and in the setting-in, the course and the goal of that task's execution, be guided solely and with constant sharpening by the basic question of the laying of the ground. This basic question is the problem of the inner possibility of the understanding of being, out of which all explicit questioning about being is to be able to grow. The metaphysics of Dasein led by this grounding-question is to unveil Dasein's constitution of being in such a way that this becomes visible as the inner enabling of the understanding of being.

The unveiling of the constitution of being of Dasein is ontology. Insofar as in it the ground of the possibility of metaphysics — the finitude of Dasein as its foundation — is to be laid, it is called *fundamental ontology*. In the content of this title the problem of the finitude in the human being, with a view to the enabling of the understanding of being, is included as the decisive thing. The title is thus explained by its second half rather than its first: what makes the ontology fundamental is not that it comes first among ontologies but that it lays the foundation of metaphysics, and that foundation is finitude.

Fundamental ontology is nevertheless only the first stage of the metaphysics of Dasein. What belongs to the latter as a whole, and how it is on each occasion historically rooted in factical Dasein, cannot be discussed here. Now the task is only to clarify the idea of fundamental ontology by which the foregoing interpretation of the *Critique of Pure Reason* is guided; and the characterization is to be given only in basic outlines, to show once more the simple sequence of steps in which one presented attempt at executing this idea — *Sein und Zeit* — moves.

The constitution of being of every being, and that of Dasein in a distinguished sense, becomes accessible only in understanding, insofar as understanding has the character of projection. And because understanding — as fundamental ontology precisely shows — is not merely a kind of knowing but primarily a basic moment of existing as such, the explicit carrying-out of projecting, and especially in ontological grasping, must necessarily be *construction*.

But construction here does not mean the free-floating devising of something. It is rather a projecting in which precisely the prior guidance as well as the take-off point of the projection must be predetermined and secured. Dasein is to be constructed in its finitude, and with a view to the inner enabling of the understanding of being. Every fundamental-ontological construction makes itself true in what its projection lets be seen — in how it brings Dasein to its manifestness and lets its inner metaphysics *be there*. Truth here is not correspondence but disclosive power, and that is the only warrant the analytic of Dasein can offer: it is confirmed by what it lets be seen, exactly as the interpretation of Kant was confirmed by its power to illuminate.

The distinguishing mark of fundamental-ontological construction is that it is to lay free the inner possibility of something which, as the most familiar of all, governs all Dasein and yet is indeterminate and even all too self-evident. This construction can be understood as the assault, growing within Dasein itself, of Dasein upon the metaphysical primal fact in it — the fact that the most finite in its finitude is indeed familiar and yet not grasped.

The finitude of Dasein — the understanding of being — lies in forgottenness.

And this forgottenness is not accidental and temporary; it forms itself necessarily and constantly. All fundamental-ontological construction aiming at the unveiling of the inner possibility of the understanding of being must, in projecting, wrest what it takes into its projection out of forgottenness.

The fundamental-ontological basic act of the metaphysics of Dasein as the laying of the ground of metaphysics is therefore a *re-membering*.

But genuine recollection must at all times internalize what is recollected, that is, let it come toward one again more and more in its innermost possibility. With respect to the execution of a fundamental ontology that means: it lays its main effort on letting the sole and constant guidance from the side of the question of being take effect undiminished, so as to hold the existential analytic of Dasein assigned to it on the right track. The forgottenness of being is thus already the theme in 1929, and the "retrieval" of the Fourth Section is its counter-move; the later doctrine of *Seinsvergessenheit* is here still a feature of Dasein's constitution rather than of the history of being.

#### **§ 43. Der Einsatz und der Gang der Fundamentalontologie**

The Dasein in the human being determines him as that being which, being in the midst of beings, comports itself toward them as such, and as this comportment toward beings is essentially otherwise determined in its own being than all the other beings manifest in Dasein.

An analytic of Dasein must from the outset see to making the Dasein in the human being visible first precisely within that manner of being of the human being which by its essence is laid out to hold Dasein and its understanding of being — original finitude — down in forgottenness. This manner of being, decisive when seen fundamental-ontologically, we call *everydayness*. Its analytic has at the same time the methodological intention of not letting the interpretation of the Dasein in the human being fall from the start into the domain of an anthropological-psychological description of his "lived experiences" and "faculties." That does not declare anthropological-psychological knowledge "false"; but it must be shown that for all its correctness it does not suffice to keep in view, from the beginning and constantly, the problem of the existence of Dasein — its finitude — as the guiding problematic of the question of being demands.

The existential analytic of everydayness does not want to describe how we handle knife and fork. It is to show that and how all dealing with beings — for which it looks exactly as though there were only beings — is already grounded in the transcendence of Dasein, in being-in-the-world. With that transcendence there happens the concealed and mostly indeterminate projection of the being of beings as such, so that beings reveal themselves proximally and for the most part unarticulated and yet intelligible as a whole. In this the difference between being and beings as such remains concealed, and the human being himself occurs as one being among the rest. The joke about knife and fork answers the reproach that the analytic of everydayness is a phenomenology of trivialities; what is at issue in the handling of equipment is never the equipment but the concealed projection of being that lets it be handled.

But being-in-the-world is not first the relation between subject and object; it is what makes such a relation possible beforehand, insofar as transcendence carries out the projection of the being of beings. This projecting — understanding — is made visible in the existential analytic at first only within the circle its setting-in opens. What matters is not so much pursuing understanding at once into the innermost constitution of transcendence as illuminating its essential unity with the attunement and the thrownness of Dasein.

All projection — and hence also all "creative" acting of the human being — is *thrown*, that is, determined by Dasein's dependence, not master of itself, on the beings already there as a whole. But thrownness does not restrict itself to the concealed happening of coming-to-Dasein; it governs Da-sein as such. That is expressed in the happening exhibited as *falling*. This does not mean occurrences in human life assessable at best negatively and by cultural criticism, but a character — single with thrown projection — of the innermost transcendental finitude of Dasein. The two most misread terms of *Sein und Zeit* are defended in the same breath and by the same argument: falling is not moral decline and care is not worry, because both name the structure of a finitude that is transcendental rather than ontic.

At working out the unity in the transcendental primal structure of the finitude of Dasein in the human being the course of existential ontology, setting in with the analysis of everydayness, aims — and at this alone. In transcendence Dasein announces itself as needing the understanding of being. Through this transcendental neediness it is at bottom "cared for" that anything like Da-sein can be at all; it is the innermost finitude bearing Dasein.

The unity of the transcendental structure of the innermost neediness of Dasein in the human being has received the name "care." Nothing at all depends on the word, but everything on the understanding of what the analytic of Dasein tried to exhibit with what is so named. But if one takes the expression "care" — against and despite the express directive that no ontic characterization of the human being is at issue — in the sense of a world-view or ethical assessment of "human life," instead of as a designation for the structural unity of the in-itself finite transcendence of Dasein, then everything falls into confusion, and nothing at all becomes visible of the problematic that alone guides the analytic.

It remains to be considered, of course, that the working-out of the innermost essence of finitude demanded with a view to grounding metaphysics must itself in principle always be finite and can never become absolute. But from this only the following follows: the ever-renewed reflection on finitude cannot succeed by playing standpoints off against one another and mediating between them, in order after all to win the secretly posited "in itself true" absolute knowledge of finitude. There remains only the working-out of the problematic of finitude as such, which reveals itself according to its ownmost essence when it is made accessible by a setting-in unwaveringly guided by the originally grasped basic question of metaphysics — a setting-in that of course can never be claimed as the only possible one. The concession is not a formality: a finite thinking of finitude cannot be the last word about it, and the analytic of Dasein therefore renounces in advance the completeness that would contradict its own theme.

From this it already becomes clear that the metaphysics of Dasein as laying of the ground of metaphysics has its own truth, still all too veiled in its essence. A world-view — that is, always a popular-ontic — position, and all the more any theological one, does not as such come into the dimension of the problem of a metaphysics of Dasein at all, whether it agrees or rejects; for, as Kant says, the "critique of reason can never become popular, but also has no need to be" (B xxxiv).

If accordingly a critique wants to set in against the transcendental interpretation of "care" as the transcendental unity of finitude — and who would deny its possibility and necessity? — then it must first be shown that the transcendence of Dasein, and thus the understanding of being, is not the innermost finitude in the human being; then that the grounding of metaphysics does not have this innermost relation to the finitude of Dasein at all; and finally that the basic question of the laying of the ground of metaphysics does not lie enclosed in the problem of the inner possibility of the understanding of being. Objections are not refused but given their conditions, and the conditions are severe: nothing counts as a criticism of *Sein und Zeit* that does not attack one of these three claims.

The fundamental-ontological analytic of Dasein deliberately attempts, immediately before the unitary interpretation of transcendence as "care," to work out "anxiety" as a "decisive basic attunement," in order to give a concrete indication that the existential analytic is constantly guided by the question — which awakens it — about the possibility of the understanding of being. Anxiety counts as the decisive basic attunement not with a view to some world-view proclamation of a concrete ideal of existence; it has its decisive character solely from the view toward the being-problem as such. The placement in *Sein und Zeit* is offered as proof: anxiety stands where it stands because it is the attunement in which the nothing is met, not because Heidegger has a doctrine about dread.

Anxiety is that basic attunement which places one before the nothing. But the being of beings is understandable at all — and therein lies the deepest finitude of transcendence — only if Dasein in the ground of its essence holds itself into the nothing. This holding-oneself-into the nothing is no arbitrary and occasionally attempted "thinking" of the nothing but a happening that underlies all finding-oneself in the midst of what already is, and that must be illuminated in its inner possibility in a fundamental-ontological analytic of Dasein. A marginal note from a much later hand adds that this nihilating comportment is itself grounded in *Gelassenheit*, releasement — the term of the 1950s, entered against a sentence of 1929.

"Anxiety" so understood, that is, fundamental-ontologically, takes from "care" the harmlessness of a categorial structure from the ground up. It gives care the sharpness necessarily proper to the fundamental existential, and so determines the finitude in Dasein not as a property at hand but as the constant, though mostly concealed, trembling of everything that exists.

But the working-out of care as the transcendental basic constitution of Dasein is only the first stage of fundamental ontology. For the further course toward its goal, the determining guidance from the side of the question of being must take effect with growing inexorability.

#### **§ 44. Das Ziel der Fundamentalontologie**

The further, decisive step of the existential analytic is the concrete illumination of care as temporality. And because the problematic of the laying of the ground of metaphysics has an inner relation to the finitude in the human being, it could seem as though the working-out of "temporality" stood in the service of a concrete determination of the finitude of the human being as a "temporal" being — since the "temporal" usually counts as the finite.

But already the fact that in the sense of the vulgar and within its limits legitimate determination of time we grasp *all* finite beings, not only the human being, as "temporal" must lead to the conclusion that the interpretation of Dasein as temporality cannot move in the field of the vulgar experience of the temporal.

Nor is that interpretation set up because present-day philosophy — Bergson, Dilthey, Simmel — tries more thoroughly than earlier philosophy to grasp "life" in its liveliness more "livingly" by determining its time-character. Lebensphilosophie is thus named and put aside: the interest in time there is an interest in the fullness of experience, and it has nothing to do with the question why being is understood from time.

Rather: if the interpretation of Dasein as temporality is the goal of fundamental ontology, it must be motivated solely by the being-problem as such. And only with that does the fundamental-ontological sense of the question about time — the sense alone guiding in *Sein und Zeit* — first open up.

The fundamental-ontological laying of the ground of metaphysics in *Sein und Zeit* must understand itself as retrieval. The passage from Plato's *Sophist* that opens the investigation does not serve as decoration but as an indication that in ancient metaphysics the gigantomachy over the being of beings has broken out. In this battle it must become visible — however generally and ambiguously the question of being may be posed there — in what way being as such is understood. But insofar as in this gigantomachy the question of being is first fought for as such and *not yet* worked out in the way characterized, as the problem of the inner possibility of the understanding of being, neither the interpretation of being as such nor the horizon of interpretation necessary for it can expressly come to light. All the more insistently must one listen, in retrieving the problem, into the way philosophizing in this first battle over being expresses itself about being as it were spontaneously. What is to be heard is not what the Greeks said about being but what their manner of saying it presupposed — the same procedure applied to Plato and Aristotle that §35 applied to Kant.

The present investigation cannot of course give a thematic presentation, let alone an interpretation, of the basic movements of this gigantomachy. A pointer at what is palpable must suffice.

What lies in this, that ancient metaphysics determines the *ontōs on* — the being that is as beings alone can be — as *aei on*, as what always is? The being of beings is evidently understood here as constancy and permanence. What projection lies in this understanding of being? The projection upon time; for even "eternity," taken as the *nunc stans*, is as the "constant" "now" through and through comprehensible only from time.

What lies in this, that what properly is is understood as *ousia*, *parousia*, in a meaning that at bottom means presence, immediate and always current possession, holding?

This projection betrays: being means constancy in presence.

Do the time-determinations not accumulate here, and precisely in the spontaneous understanding of being? Does the immediate understanding of being not hold itself through and through in an original but also self-evident projection of being upon time?

Does not then all struggle over being move in advance within the horizon of time?

Is it then to be wondered at if the ontological interpretation of the what-being of beings expresses itself in the *to ti ēn einai*? Does this "what it always already was" not enclose the moment of constant presence, and now even in the character of priority?

And does it then suffice to elucidate the "a priori," which in the tradition of ontology counts as a character of the determinations of being, simply by saying that this "earlier" has "naturally" nothing to do with "time"? Certainly not with the time the vulgar understanding of time knows. But is this "earlier" thereby positively determined, and is the troublesome time-character thereby removed? Does it not return as a sharpened problem? The single most compressed statement of the thesis of *Sein und Zeit* stands in these questions: that the a priori is temporal in a sense the tradition's own concept of time cannot accommodate, which is why the tradition can only deny the time-character rather than explain it.

And is it only a more or less happy habit, arisen somewhere and sometime, that in dividing beings — that is, in distinguishing beings with respect to their being — we "of ourselves" determine them as temporal, atemporal and supratemporal?

But where lies the ground for this spontaneous and self-evident understanding of being from time? Is there so much as the attempt to ask, in the sense of a worked-out problem, why this is so and why it must even happen?

The essence of time as Aristotle exhibited it, in the way decisive for the subsequent history of metaphysics, gives no answer. On the contrary: it can be shown that precisely this analysis of time is guided by an understanding of being which — concealed from itself in its doing — understands being as constant presence and accordingly determines the "being" of time from the "now," that is, from that character of time which in it is always and constantly present, and hence in the ancient sense properly is. The circle is the finding: the tradition's account of time is derived from an understanding of being that is itself an unexamined projection upon time, so that time has never been thought except in terms of what it made thinkable.

Time does count for Aristotle too as something happening in the "soul," in the "mind." But neither is the determination of the essence of the soul, the mind, the spirit, the consciousness of the human being primarily and decisively guided by the grounding-problematic of metaphysics, nor is time interpreted with a view to that problematic, nor is the interpretation of the transcendental basic structure of Dasein as temporality grasped and carried out as a problem at all.

Out of the philosophizing "recollection" of the concealed projection of being upon time, as the innermost happening in the understanding of being of ancient and subsequent metaphysics, a retrieval of the basic question of metaphysics receives the task of carrying out the regress into the finitude in the human being demanded by this problematic, in such a way that temporality becomes visible in Da-sein as such as its transcendental primal structure.

On the way to this goal of fundamental ontology — and at the same time in the service of working out the finitude in the human being — the existential interpretation of conscience, guilt and death becomes necessary. And the transcendental interpretation of historicity on the ground of temporality is at the same time to give a preliminary concept of the manner of being of that happening which happens in the retrieval of the question of being. Metaphysics is nothing that is merely "created" by human beings in systems and doctrines; the understanding of being, its projection and its rejection, happens in Dasein as such. "Metaphysics" is the basic happening in the break-in into beings that happens with the factical existence of anything like a human being. The most difficult chapters of *Sein und Zeit* are thereby given a single justification: death and conscience are not existential edification but the places where the finitude that grounds the understanding of being becomes explicit.

The metaphysics of Dasein that is to bring itself to development in fundamental ontology is not claimed as a new discipline within the frame of those already at hand; what announces itself in it is the will to awaken the insight that philosophizing happens as the explicit transcendence of Dasein.

And if the problematic of the metaphysics of Dasein is named as that of "Being and Time," it can now be clear, from the clarification of the idea of fundamental ontology, that in this title the "and" harbours the central problem within it. "Being" as well as "time" need not give up their previous meaning, but a more original interpretation must ground the right and the limit of that meaning. The title of the earlier work is thus read the same way the *Kantbuch* has read every title it has touched: the conjunction is the thesis, and the two nouns are what has to be transformed to make it sayable.

#### **§ 45. Die Idee der Fundamentalontologie und die Kritik der reinen Vernunft**

Kant's laying of the ground of metaphysics had to come upon time as the basic determination of finite transcendence, as the first resolute questioning about the inner possibility of the manifestness of the being of beings — if the understanding of being in Dasein as it were of itself projects being upon time. A marginal note asks of that clause: what does that mean? But his grounding also had to be driven back, beyond the vulgar concept of time, to the transcendental understanding of time as pure self-affection, which in its essence is one with pure apperception and in this unity makes possible the wholeness of a pure sensible reason.

Not because time functions as "form of intuition" and is interpreted as such at the beginning of the *Critique*, but because the understanding of being had to project itself upon time out of the ground of the finitude of the Dasein in the human being, does time gain — in essential unity with the transcendental imagination — the central metaphysical function in the *Critique of Pure Reason*. A marginal note repeats the debt left standing at §35: how is the question of space included here, and the "spatiality" of Da-sein in *Sein und Zeit*?

The *Critique* thereby itself shakes the rule of reason and understanding. "Logic" is deprived of the primacy it has had in metaphysics from of old. Its idea becomes questionable.

If the essence of transcendence is grounded in the pure imagination, or more originally in temporality, then precisely the idea of a "transcendental logic" is a non-concept — especially when, contrary to Kant's original intention, it is set on its own and taken absolutely. The book's most consequential negative result is stated in a single sentence, and it is aimed at the discipline that had claimed Kant for a century: transcendental logic is not a deepening of logic but a name for a problem logic cannot pose.

Kant must have divined something of this collapse of the rule of logic in metaphysics when he could say of the basic characters of being, "possibility" (what-being) and "actuality" (which Kant calls "Dasein"): "possibility, existence and necessity no one has yet been able to explain except through obvious tautology, if one wanted to draw their definition solely from the pure understanding" (A 244, B 302).

And yet! Did Kant not give the rule back to the understanding in the second edition of the *Critique*? Did metaphysics not, in consequence of that, become "logic" with Hegel as radically as never before?

What does the struggle against the "thing in itself" beginning in German idealism mean but the growing forgetting of what Kant fought for — that the inner possibility and the necessity of metaphysics, that is, its essence, are at bottom borne and preserved by the more original working-out and the sharpened preservation of the problem of finitude? The thing in itself is thus defended not as an unknowable object but as the mark of finitude in Kant's system; and its elimination by idealism is read as the elimination of finitude, which is why the *Kantbuch* had to say that its interpretation moves in the opposite direction from German idealism.

What has become of Kant's effort when Hegel elucidates metaphysics as logic thus: "logic is accordingly to be grasped as the system of pure reason, as the realm of pure thought. This realm is truth as it is without veil in and for itself. One can therefore express oneself thus, that this content is the presentation of God as he is in his eternal essence before the creation of nature and of a finite spirit."

Are there more penetrating proofs of how little self-evident the metaphysics belonging to the nature of the human being is — and thereby the "nature of the human being" itself? Hegel's sentence is left to do its own damage: a philosophy that describes its content as God before the creation of a finite spirit has abandoned the finitude in which alone, on this reading, metaphysics can be grounded.

Shall we want to understand the fundamental-ontological interpretation of the *Critique of Pure Reason* presented here so as to think ourselves, in possession of it, cleverer than the great forebears? Or does our own effort, if we may compare it at all, in the end also contain a concealed evading of something that we — and not by chance — no longer see? The charge levelled at Kant in §31 is turned on the interpreter, and left as a question; the possibility that this book too shrinks back before what it uncovered is admitted without being resolved.

Perhaps the fundamental-ontologically oriented interpretation of the *Critique* has sharpened the problematic of a laying of the ground of metaphysics — though only so far that it stops short before what is decisive. So there remains only the one thing: to hold the investigation open by questioning.

Does a "transcendental dialectic" not follow the transcendental analytic in the wider sense, to whose interpretation the investigation restricted itself? And even if this may at first be only the critical application of the won insight into the essence of *metaphysica generalis* to the rejection of the traditional *metaphysica specialis*, does a positive problematic not lie in this seemingly merely negative characterization of the transcendental dialectic too?

And what if this problematic concentrated itself in the same question that, though concealed and unworked-out, already guided all previous problematic of metaphysics — in the problem of the finitude of Dasein?

Kant says that the "transcendental illusion" to which traditional metaphysics owes its possibility is a *necessary* one. Must this transcendental untruth not be positively grounded, with respect to its original unity with transcendental truth, out of the innermost essence of the finitude in Dasein? Does the non-essence of that illusion belong to this essence of finitude? The problem announced as unposed at the introduction to Section III B is here posed, and posed as a question directed at the part of the *Critique* the book has passed over. Transcendental illusion would be, on this reading, the Kantian name for the untruth that belongs to the essence of truth — which is the thesis of *Vom Wesen der Wahrheit* five years off, and the reason the Dialectic is the unwritten sequel to this book.

But does the problem of "transcendental illusion" then not need liberating from the architectonic into which Kant forces it, in accordance with his orientation to traditional logic — the more so if logic as such is shaken by Kant's laying of the ground as a possible ground and guiding thread for the problematic of metaphysics?

What is the transcendental essence of truth as such? How are this and the non-essence of untruth at once originally one, in the ground of the finitude of Dasein, with the fundamental neediness of the human being — as a being thrown into beings — of having to understand something like being?

Has it a sense, and is there a right, to grasp the human being on the ground of his innermost finitude — that he *needs* "ontology," the understanding of being — as "creative" and thereby as "infinite," when precisely the idea of the infinite being repels nothing so radically as an ontology? The formula is the book's last word on the creativity that has troubled it since §25: an infinite being would need no understanding of being, so the very capacity that looks like creation is the mark of destitution.

But can finitude in Dasein be developed even as a problem without a "presupposed" infinity? Of what kind is this "pre-supposing" in Dasein at all? What does the infinity so "posited" mean?

Will the question of being press itself out of all this questionability again in its elemental force and breadth? Or have we already become too much the fools of organization, of business and of speed to be able to be the friends of the essential, the simple and the steady — in which "friendship" (*philia*) alone the turning toward beings as such is carried out, out of which the question about the concept of being (*sophia*), the basic question of philosophy, grows?

Or do we need for that too first the recollection? —

So let Aristotle have the word: "and indeed what was sought of old and now and always, and always found perplexing, is this: what is being?" (*Metaphysics* Z 1, 1028 b 2 ff.). The book ends where it said metaphysics began, with a question rather than a doctrine, and with the word that the whole retrieval has been about: not what beings are, but what being means.

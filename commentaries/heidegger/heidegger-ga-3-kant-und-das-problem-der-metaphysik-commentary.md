# **Commentary on Heidegger, *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* (GA 3)**

A paragraph-by-paragraph commentary on the *Kantbuch* of 1929, covering the main text (GA 3, pp. 1–246) — the Introduction and all four Sections, through §45. The six appendices (pp. 249–311), including the *Davoser Disputation* with Cassirer, are not included here; they are a different genre and would need their own treatment.

Citations give the GA 3 page. Kant is cited as Heidegger cites him, by the A and B pagination of the *Critique of Pure Reason*. Translations are working renderings, not a published edition. Two recurring choices could mislead if left unmarked. *Grundlegung* is rendered "laying of the ground" rather than "foundation," because Heidegger's whole polemic turns on refusing the picture of a foundation slid under a finished building. *Enthüllung* and *Offenbarkeit* are kept apart as "unveiling" (of being) and "manifestness" (of beings): the pair carries in Kantian idiom exactly the distinction that *Sein und Zeit* §44 carries as disclosedness and uncoveredness, and collapsing them destroys the book's central claim about truth.

A small glossary of German terms is retained where an English word would blur them. **Dasein** — the being that we ourselves are, and here specifically "the Dasein in the human being" (*das Dasein im Menschen*), which is not a synonym for "human being" and is the pivot of the Fourth Section. **Einbildungskraft** appears throughout as "imagination," but never in the sense of fancy or image-making: it is a power of forming (*Bildung*), and Heidegger's argument depends on hearing *Bild* (image, look) inside it. **Gegenstehenlassen** — the letting-stand-over-against by which finite knowing first lets an object be an object; Michel Henry's *objectivation* translates this word, and readers coming to GA 3 from *L'essence de la manifestation* should note that it names an act of the subject, not a property of the thing. **Umschlag** and **Zurückweichen** — the reversal Kant performs between the two editions, and the shrinking back from the imagination that Heidegger reads in it.

The argument of the book, stated once so the reader can hold the arc. The *Critique of Pure Reason* is not epistemology and not a theory of natural-scientific knowledge; it is a laying of the ground of metaphysics, and what it grounds is ontology — the prior understanding of being that lets beings be encountered at all. That grounding runs back through five stages to a single source, and the source turns out to be the transcendental imagination, which Kant himself names the common root of sensibility and understanding and then abandons. Because the imagination is at bottom time, and time is pure self-affection, the ground of ontological knowledge is the temporality of a finite self. Kant shrank back from this because it dissolves the primacy of reason; the Fourth Section retrieves what he shrank from, and the retrieval is the metaphysics of Dasein as fundamental ontology. The book therefore reads Kant as an ally forced into service, and Heidegger's own later prefaces concede as much — the 1973 preface calls the *Kantbuch* a refuge (*Zuflucht*), a place to look for an advocate for the question of being that *Sein und Zeit* had raised and no one had heard.

Certain misreadings dog this text, and the commentary guards against them throughout. The book is not Kant philology and does not claim to be; Heidegger concedes the charge of violence in the preface to the second edition and grants that historical philology is right every time it presses the charge against a thinking dialogue. Finitude (*Endlichkeit*) is not a defect measured against an infinite intellect but the positive constitution of human knowing, and the whole reading collapses if it is heard as a lament. Ontological knowledge is not knowledge of some higher class of beings, and transcendence is not a passage across to a transcendent object; both name the prior projection of the being of beings within which any object can be met. The transcendental imagination is not a third faculty added to two others, and "root" is not a cause producing effects — Heidegger's claim is that intuition and thinking are *stems* of one root, so that neither is intelligible on its own. A schema is not an image and not a generic picture; the schema-image is introduced precisely to keep the two apart. Time as pure self-affection is not introspection and not the inner sense of empirical psychology, and the self is not first given and then found to be temporal. Finally, the metaphysics of Dasein is not a philosophical anthropology, and the Fourth Section is a sustained argument that the question "what is the human being?" cannot be posed anthropologically at all.

## **Einleitung: Das Thema der Untersuchung und seine Gliederung**

The investigation sets itself the task of interpreting the *Critique of Pure Reason* as a laying of the ground of metaphysics, in order to set the problem of metaphysics before us as the problem of a fundamental ontology. Two theses are packed into one sentence, and their order matters: the Kant interpretation is the means, the exhibition of fundamental ontology the end. Kant's text is not the subject matter but the medium in which a systematic claim is to be made visible.

Fundamental ontology names the ontological analytic of the finite essence of the human being that is to prepare the foundation for the metaphysics "belonging to the nature of the human being." It is the metaphysics of human Dasein required if metaphysics is to be possible at all, and it remains in principle distinct from every anthropology, including philosophical anthropology. That demarcation, stated here in a single clause, is what the entire Fourth Section will have to earn. Laying out the idea of a fundamental ontology means exhibiting this analytic as a necessary demand and thereby making clear in what intention, in what manner, within what limits and under what presuppositions it poses the concrete question: what is the human being? An idea first announces itself through its power to illuminate, so the idea of fundamental ontology is to prove and present itself in an interpretation of the *Critique*. The Kant reading is thus a test of illuminating power, not an appeal to authority — which is why the eventual verdict that Kant shrank back does not damage the demonstration.

What "laying of the ground" means must be clarified in advance. The expression takes its sense from building. Metaphysics is no building already standing, yet it is actual in all human beings "as a natural disposition" (Kant, B 21). Laying a ground could therefore mean sliding a foundation under this natural metaphysics, or replacing one already laid by pushing a new one under it.

Exactly this picture must be kept out of the idea. Laying the ground is rather the projecting of the building-plan itself, in such a way that the plan at the same time prescribes on what and how the building is to be grounded. Yet as the projecting of a plan it is again no empty production of a system with its compartments, but the architectonic delimitation and articulation of the inner possibility of metaphysics — the concrete determination of its essence. The metaphor is not decorative. A plan is drawn before the building exists and determines what would count as its ground; that is precisely the structure of the a priori, and Heidegger is smuggling the whole doctrine of projection into an image from the building trade.

All determination of essence is completed only in the laying bare of the ground of essence. This single sentence is the hinge on which the Third Section turns: an essence-determination that stops short of its own ground has not finished, which is why the five stages of the Second Section cannot be the end of the book.

Laying the ground, as the projection of the inner possibility of metaphysics, is necessarily a letting-become-effective of the bearing power of the ground that has been laid. Whether and how this happens is the criterion of the originality and breadth of a laying of the ground. A ground is not laid by being pointed at; it is laid by being made to bear. Heidegger thereby gives himself the standard by which he will convict Kant: the imagination is reached and named, and then not allowed to bear.

Should the interpretation succeed in bringing to light the originality of the origin of metaphysics, that originality is genuinely understood only when it is also brought into the concrete happening of letting the origin spring forth — that is, when the laying of the ground is repeated. *Wiederholung*, retrieval, is announced here as the title of the Fourth Section and as something other than restatement: to retrieve is to perform again, from the ground, what the first performance left unperformed.

Since metaphysics belongs to the "nature of the human being" and factically exists with him, it has always already formed itself in some shape. An explicit laying of its ground therefore never happens out of nothing, but in the strength and the weakness of a tradition that prescribes to it the possibilities of its starting point. With respect to the tradition enclosed in it, every laying of the ground is, in relation to earlier ones, a transformation of the same task. Tradition is not an obstacle to be cleared away but the very medium of the possibilities available — a claim that licenses Heidegger's method of destruction and simultaneously licenses his violence, since a transformation of a task is not a report on it.

The interpretation must accordingly bring four things to light: the laying of the ground of metaphysics in its starting point, in its execution, in its originality, and in a retrieval. The four Sections are named, and the sequence is one of increasing depth rather than of subject matter: the same single act is examined four times, each time from further back.

The body of the book carries a title of its own, standing over all four Sections: the laying out of the idea of a fundamental ontology by way of the interpretation of the *Critique of Pure Reason* as a laying of the ground of metaphysics. The Sections are subordinate to that one heading, and the reader who forgets it will take the four Sections for four topics rather than four passes at one.

## **Erster Abschnitt: Die Grundlegung der Metaphysik im Ansatz**

Exhibiting Kant's starting point amounts to answering one question: why does the laying of the ground of metaphysics become, for Kant, a critique of pure reason? The answer comes through three subsidiary questions — what concept of metaphysics did Kant find before him, what is the starting point of the laying of its ground, and why is this laying of the ground a critique of pure reason. The three §§ answer them in order.

### **§ 1. Der überlieferte Begriff der Metaphysik**

The horizon within which Kant saw metaphysics, and within which his own grounding had to begin, is roughly marked by Baumgarten's definition: metaphysics is the science containing the first principles of what human knowing grasps. In the concept of "first principles of human knowledge" lies a peculiar and initially necessary ambiguity — first principles may mean the highest beings from which everything else derives, or the fundamental determinations of anything that is. Baumgarten's division follows: ontology, cosmology, psychology and natural theology belong to metaphysics. Heidegger's marginal gloss sharpens the definition — metaphysics is the first science insofar as it contains the governing grounds of what human knowing *represents* — shifting the accent from what knowing grasps to how it represents, which is the direction the whole book will take.

The motives and history of the formation and consolidation of this school concept are not to be presented here; only a brief pointer at what is essential, to loosen up the problematic content of the concept and prepare an understanding of the fundamental significance of Kant's starting point. A footnote names the Kant research of Pichler, Heimsoeth, Wundt, Kroner and Nicolai Hartmann, and registers the objection that governs Heidegger's whole relation to it: they all hold fast from the outset to the conception of the *Critique* as epistemology, and now set out the metaphysics and the "metaphysical motives" alongside it. Setting metaphysics *alongside* epistemology is precisely what Heidegger refuses; the *Critique* is either a laying of the ground of metaphysics or it is misread.

It is known that the initially book-technical meaning of *meta ta physika* — a collective name for those treatises of Aristotle placed after the ones belonging to the "Physics" — later flipped over into a philosophically interpretive characterization of what those treatises contain. This flip does not have the harmlessness usually attributed to it. It pressed the interpretation of these treatises in a wholly determinate direction and thereby decided the conception of what Aristotle treats there as "metaphysics." Whether what is collected in the Aristotelian "Metaphysics" is metaphysics at all must be doubted. A cataloguing accident is thus made responsible for a doctrine; and the doubt Heidegger enters here is the seed of his later readings of Aristotle against the metaphysical tradition that claims him.

Kant himself still wants to assign the expression a directly substantive meaning, and Heidegger quotes the metaphysics lectures: since *physis* means nature, and we reach concepts of nature only through experience, the science that follows upon it is called metaphysics, from *meta*, trans, and *physica* — a science lying as it were outside the domain of physics, beyond it. Kant reads the accident as an insight, which is exactly the naturalization Heidegger has just called into doubt.

The book-technical expression that occasioned this substantive interpretation itself sprang from an embarrassment in the substantive understanding of the writings thus ordered in the Aristotelian corpus. For precisely what Aristotle aims at there as *prōtē philosophia*, philosophizing in the first place, later school philosophy — logic, physics, ethics — had no discipline and no frame into which it could be fitted. *Meta ta physika* is the title for a fundamental philosophical embarrassment. The point is not that the editors blundered but that they had nowhere to put first philosophy, because first philosophy is not a region alongside others.

The embarrassment in turn had its ground in the unclarity of the essence of the problems and cognitions discussed in those treatises. So far as Aristotle expresses himself about it, a remarkable doubling shows up precisely in the determination of the essence of "first philosophy." It is both knowledge of beings as beings and knowledge of the most eminent region of beings, from out of which beings as a whole are determined.

This double characterization contains neither two fundamentally different, mutually independent trains of thought, nor may one be weakened or eliminated in favour of the other, nor may the apparent duality be prematurely reconciled into a unity. What is needed is to illuminate the grounds of the apparent duality and the manner of the belonging-together of the two determinations, out of the guiding problem of a "first philosophy" of beings. The task becomes the more pressing in that the doubling does not first appear with Aristotle but has governed the problem of being since the beginnings of ancient philosophy. This is the *ontologische Differenz* being read out of a philological crux; and Heidegger's refusal to reconcile the two determinations too quickly is his standing methodological signature — the apparent contradiction is the phenomenon, not an obstacle to it.

To hold fast to this problem of the essential determination of "metaphysics," one may say by way of anticipation: metaphysics is the fundamental knowledge of beings as such and in the whole. This "definition" may count only as an indication of the problem — the questions where the essence of the knowledge of the being of beings lies, how far this necessarily unfolds into a knowledge of beings as a whole, and why this in turn sharpens into a knowledge of the knowledge of being. So "metaphysics" remains the title for the embarrassment of philosophy as such. The third question is the one that makes the book possible: a metaphysics of metaphysics is demanded by the structure of the problem itself, not imposed on it.

Post-Aristotelian Western metaphysics owes its formation not to the taking over and continuation of a supposedly existing Aristotelian system, but to the failure to understand the questionworthiness and openness in which Plato and Aristotle left the central problems. Two motives principally determined the formation of the school concept of metaphysics and increasingly prevented the original problematic from being taken up again. What made the tradition possible was a failure of hearing; the openness of the Greek beginning was closed by being systematized.

The first motive concerns the content-division of metaphysics and stems from the Christian faith-interpretation of the world. On that interpretation all non-divine beings are created — the universe; among creatures the human being has a distinguished position insofar as everything depends on the salvation of his soul and his eternal existence. Accordingly the whole of beings is divided into God, nature and the human being, to which regions theology, whose object is the *summum ens*, cosmology, and psychology are then promptly assigned. These make up the discipline of *metaphysica specialis*; in distinction from it, *metaphysica generalis* (ontology) has beings "in general" (*ens commune*) as its object. The tripartite architecture of school metaphysics is thus a theological inheritance, not a discovery about beings — which is why the human being's privileged place in it is a doctrinal residue rather than an argued result, a point the Fourth Section will exploit.

The second motive concerns metaphysics' manner of knowing and its method. Since it has beings in general and the highest being as its object, in which "everyone takes an interest" (Kant), it is the science of the highest dignity, the "queen of the sciences." Its manner of knowing must accordingly be the strictest and unconditionally binding, which requires that it conform itself to a corresponding ideal of knowledge. Mathematical knowledge counts as that ideal: rational and a priori in the highest sense, independent of contingent experience, hence pure science of reason. Knowledge of beings in general and according to their principal regions thus becomes a "science from mere reason." Metaphysics borrows its ideal of rigour from a domain foreign to it; the borrowing, not any particular doctrine, is what will fail.

Kant holds fast to the intention of this metaphysics, and even shifts it more strongly into *metaphysica specialis*, which he calls "metaphysics proper," "metaphysics in its final purpose." But in the face of the constant miscarriage of all attempts in this science, its inconsistency and ineffectiveness, all attempts to extend pure rational knowledge must first be interdicted until the question of the inner possibility of this science is clarified. So arises the task of a laying of the ground in the sense of an essential determination of metaphysics. Kant's critique is thus conservative in aim and revolutionary in effect: he wants what the tradition wanted, and the demand that it first show its possibility is what destroys it.

### **§ 2. Der Ansatz der Grundlegung der überlieferten Metaphysik**

In metaphysics as the pure rational knowledge of the "universal" in beings and of the respective totality of their principal regions, a "crossing-over" is performed beyond what experience can offer as particular and partial. Crossing over the sensible, this knowledge seeks to grasp the supersensible being. But its procedure has hitherto been "a mere groping, and, what is worst, among mere concepts" (B xv). Metaphysics lacks any binding demonstration of the insights it claims. What gives this metaphysics the inner possibility of being what it wants to be? The crossing-over is registered as a fact about metaphysics before it is analysed; that word, *Überschritt*, will shortly become *Transzendenz*, and the whole book is the analysis of it.

A laying of the ground in the sense of a delimitation of inner possibility must above all aim at the final purpose of metaphysics, an essential determination of *metaphysica specialis*, since this is knowledge of the supersensible being in an exceptional sense. But the question of the inner possibility of such knowledge finds itself thrown back upon the more general question of the inner possibility of a making-manifest of beings as such at all. Laying the ground is now the illumination of the essence of a comportment toward beings in which they show themselves at themselves, such that all assertion about them becomes demonstrable from there. The question about a special region of beings is deflected into the question about access to beings as such — and the criterion of demonstrability is set by the beings' showing themselves, not by the coherence of concepts.

What belongs to the possibility of such comportment? Is there an "indication" of what makes it possible? There is: the procedure of the natural scientists. A light dawned on them; they grasped that reason sees only what it itself produces according to its own projection, that it must go ahead with principles of its judgments according to constant laws and compel nature to answer its questions, rather than letting itself be led by nature as if on a leash (B xiii f.). The plan of a nature in general, projected beforehand, first gives in advance the constitution of being of the beings to which all investigating questioning is to be able to comport itself. This prior plan of the being of beings is inscribed in the fundamental concepts and principles of the science of nature in question. What makes comportment toward beings possible — ontic knowledge — is therefore the prior understanding of the constitution of being: ontological knowledge. The distinction that carries the entire book is introduced here, and it is introduced from the practice of physics rather than from a theory of consciousness.

Mathematical natural science gives an indication of this fundamental conditioning-relation between ontic experience and ontological knowledge. But its function for the laying of the ground of metaphysics is exhausted in that. Pointing to the relation is not yet the solution of the problem, only a statement of the direction in which the problem, understood in fundamental generality, must first be sought. Whether it can be found there alone, or at all — that is, whether the idea of a *metaphysica specialis* may be projected at all on the measure of the concept of positive scientific knowledge — is precisely what remains to be decided. The natural scientist's insight is a clue, not a model. Heidegger is guarding the reading against exactly the neo-Kantian conclusion he will name in §3.

The projection of the inner possibility of *metaphysica specialis* is led back, by way of the question of the possibility of ontic knowledge, to the question of the possibility of what makes ontic knowledge possible. But that is the problem of the essence of the prior understanding of being, of ontological knowledge in the widest sense. And the problem of the inner possibility of ontology contains the question of the possibility of *metaphysica generalis*. The attempt to lay the ground of *metaphysica specialis* is thus pressed back within itself onto the question of the essence of *metaphysica generalis*. The specialized disciplines of school metaphysics are not refuted but bypassed: asking after their possibility forces the enquiry into general ontology, and Heidegger has thereby made the *Critique* into a book about ontology without having to argue that God, world and soul are illegitimate topics.

With a laying of the ground that begins in this way, Kant brings himself immediately into dialogue with Aristotle and Plato. Ontology now becomes a problem at all for the first time. With that the first and innermost tremor enters the edifice of traditional metaphysics. The indeterminacy and obviousness vanish in which *metaphysica generalis* had hitherto treated of the "generality" of the *ens commune*. The question of the laying of the ground demands for the first time clarity about the kind of universalization, and thereby about the character of the crossing-over, that lies in the knowledge of the constitution of being. Whether Kant himself reaches full clarification of the problem is a subordinate question; enough that he recognized its necessity and above all presented it. It becomes clear too that ontology is not primarily related to the grounding of the positive sciences at all: its necessity and its role are grounded in a "higher interest" that human reason finds in itself. And because *metaphysica generalis* provides the necessary equipment for *metaphysica specialis*, the essential determination of the latter must also transform itself in the laying of the ground of the former. The concession about Kant's own clarity is not modesty but licence: Heidegger has secured the right to press the problem further than Kant took it, which is what the Third Section does.

Laying the ground of metaphysics as a whole means unveiling the inner possibility of ontology. That is the genuine — because metaphysical, that is, related to metaphysics as the sole theme — sense of what is constantly misinterpreted under the title of Kant's "Copernican turn." Kant's text is quoted: hitherto it was assumed that all our knowledge must conform to objects, but all attempts to make out something about them a priori through concepts came to nothing on that presupposition; let it therefore be tried whether we do not make better progress in the tasks of metaphysics by assuming that objects must conform to our knowledge, which already agrees better with the demanded possibility of an a priori knowledge of them that is to establish something about objects before they are given to us (B xvi).

Kant means: not "all knowledge" is ontic, and where ontic knowledge is present it becomes possible only through an ontological one. The Copernican turn so little shakes the "old" concept of truth as the conforming (*adaequatio*) of knowledge to beings that it presupposes it, indeed first grounds it. Ontic knowledge can conform to beings ("objects") only if these beings are already manifest beforehand as beings, that is, known in their constitution of being. It is to this last knowledge that objects must conform — that is, their ontic determinability. The manifestness of beings (ontic truth) revolves around the unveiledness of the constitution of being of beings (ontological truth); ontic knowledge can never conform "to" objects on its own, because without the ontological it does not even have a possible "to what." The Copernican turn is thus read as a thesis about the founding of truth rather than about the mind's contribution to the object. The pairing of manifestness and unveiledness restates *Sein und Zeit* §44 — uncoveredness of beings founded in disclosedness of being — in a vocabulary from which the word Dasein has been withdrawn, and this is the passage to cite for the continuity of the two books on truth.

So the laying of the ground of traditional metaphysics begins with the question of the inner possibility of ontology as such. Why does this laying of the ground become a "critique of pure reason"?

### **§ 3. Die Grundlegung der Metaphysik als „Kritik der reinen Vernunft"**

Kant brings the problem of the possibility of ontology to the question: how are synthetic judgments a priori possible? Interpreting this problem-formula explains why the laying of the ground of metaphysics is carried out as a critique of pure reason. The question of the possibility of ontological knowledge requires a preliminary characterization of that knowledge, and Kant, in accordance with the tradition, grasps knowing as judging. That inherited equation of knowing with judging is registered without protest here; the book will end by dismantling it, since the whole doctrine of the imagination shows that judgment is not the seat of ontological knowledge.

What kind of knowledge is present in ontological understanding? Beings are known in it. But what is known there belongs to beings, however they may be experienced and determined. This known what-being of beings is supplied in ontological knowledge before all ontic experience, and yet precisely for it, a priori. A knowledge that supplies the what-content of beings, or unveils beings themselves, Kant calls "synthetic." So the question of the possibility of ontological knowledge becomes the problem of the essence of synthetic judgments a priori. The point to hold is that the a priori is not prior in the sense of being independent of experience but prior *for* experience — a temporal-sounding "before" that the Fourth Stage will show to be temporal in the strict sense.

The instance grounding the legitimacy of these substantive judgments about the being of beings cannot lie in experience, since experience of beings is itself always already guided by the ontological understanding of those beings which experience is to make accessible in a determinate respect. Ontological knowledge is accordingly a judging according to grounds (principles) not suppliable in the manner of experience. The circularity is deliberate: experience cannot warrant what makes experience possible.

Kant calls our faculty of knowing from a priori principles "pure reason" — that which contains the principles of knowing something absolutely a priori (A 11, B 24). Insofar as the principles contained in reason make up the possibility of an a priori knowledge, the unveiling of the possibility of ontological knowledge must become an illumination of the essence of pure reason. But delimiting the essence of pure reason is at the same time the differentiating determination of its non-essence (*Unwesen*), and thereby the bounding and restriction — the critique — of pure reason to its essential possibilities. Laying the ground of metaphysics as the unveiling of the essence of ontology is "critique of pure reason." Critique is heard as limitation in the constructive sense: a boundary determines an essence rather than curtailing it, and *Unwesen* is not corruption but the range of what falls outside the essence.

Ontological knowledge, the a priori "synthesis," is what "the whole critique is really there for" (A 14, B 28). All the more urgently, then, does this synthesis require closer determination at the very moment the guiding problem is fixed. Kant uses the term in manifold meanings, and precisely in the problem-formula of the laying of the ground these meanings intertwine. Naming an ambiguity in Kant's central term at the point where the problem is being set is not pedantry: the three senses about to be distinguished are what let Heidegger locate a synthesis that is neither predication nor empirical combination, and that synthesis is the transcendental imagination.

The question asks after the possibility of judgments synthetic a priori. Every judgment as such is already an "I combine" — namely subject and predicate. As judgments, even "analytic" judgments are already synthetic, though the ground of the agreement of the subject-predicate connection lies solely in the representation of the subject. Synthetic judgments are then "synthetic" in a twofold sense: first as judgments at all, second insofar as the legitimacy of the connection of representations is supplied from the beings themselves about which the judgment is made.

But in the synthetic judgments a priori that have been made a problem, a still different manner of synthesis is at issue. This synthesis is to supply something about beings that is not drawn from them in the manner of experience. This supplying of the determination of being of beings is a prior relating-to beings, and this pure "relation to..." first forms the "to what" and the horizon within which beings become experienceable at themselves in the empirical synthesis. It is this a priori synthesis whose possibility must be illuminated. An investigation concerning the essence of this synthesis Kant calls a transcendental one: "I call all knowledge transcendental that is occupied not so much with objects as with our manner of knowing objects insofar as this is to be possible a priori" (B 25, A 11). Horizon is the decisive word. The third synthesis is not a joining of two given items but the opening of a field in which items can be given at all, and every later determination of the imagination will be a determination of this horizon-forming.

Transcendental knowledge therefore investigates not beings themselves but the possibility of the prior understanding of being — which at the same time means: the constitution of being of beings. It concerns the crossing-over (transcendence) of pure reason to beings, such that experience can now first conform to them as possible objects. Transcendence is here not a relation to something beyond the world but the passage from the understanding of being to the beings that can consequently be met; the "beyond" crossed is the boundary of what is merely given.

To make the possibility of ontology a problem means to ask about the possibility, that is, the essence, of this transcendence of the understanding of being — to philosophize transcendentally. That is why Kant, to make the problematic of traditional ontology recognizable, uses for *metaphysica generalis* the designation "transcendental philosophy," and why in mentioning that traditional ontology he speaks of the "transcendental philosophy of the ancients" (B 113). Kant's own name for his enterprise is thus read as a name for ontology, and the phrase about the ancients is Heidegger's warrant for taking transcendental philosophy to be the continuation of the Greek question of being rather than a modern departure from it.

The *Critique* nevertheless gives no "system" of transcendental philosophy but is "a treatise on method" (B xxii). That does not mean a doctrine of the technique of procedure, but the working out of a complete determination of the "whole outline" and the "whole inner articulation" of ontology. In this laying of the ground of metaphysics as the projection of the inner possibility of ontology, "the whole preliminary sketch of a system of metaphysics is delineated" (B xxiii).

The intention of the *Critique of Pure Reason* is accordingly misconstrued in principle if the work is interpreted as a "theory of experience" or even as a theory of the positive sciences. The *Critique* has nothing to do with "epistemology." If one could let the epistemological interpretation stand at all, one would have to say: the *Critique* is not a theory of ontic knowledge (experience) but a theory of ontological knowledge. But even with this conception — already remote from the prevailing interpretation of the Transcendental Aesthetic and Analytic — the essential is not hit, namely that ontology as *metaphysica generalis*, as the foundational part of metaphysics as a whole, is here grounded and for the first time brought to itself. With the problem of transcendence, "epistemology" is not put in the place of metaphysics; rather ontology is questioned as to its inner possibility. The concession is precisely calibrated: even the improved epistemological reading is refused, because any epistemology takes knowledge as its theme, whereas the *Critique* takes being as its theme and knowledge only as the place where being is understood.

If truth belongs to the essence of a knowledge, then the transcendental problem of the inner possibility of synthetic a priori knowledge is the question of the essence of the truth of ontological transcendence. What must be determined is the essence of "transcendental truth," which "precedes all empirical truth and makes it possible" (A 146, B 185) — for "no knowledge can contradict it without at the same time losing all content, that is, all relation to any object, hence all truth" (A 62 f., B 87). Ontic truth necessarily conforms to ontological truth. That is once again the legitimate interpretation of the sense of the "Copernican turn." With this turn Kant presses the problem of ontology into the centre. For the problematic of the possibility of original ontological truth nothing may be presupposed, least of all the "fact" of the truth of the positive sciences. Heidegger has now located within Kant's own text a truth that precedes empirical truth and grounds it, which is the Kantian name for what *Sein und Zeit* called the disclosedness in which alone assertions can be true. The refusal to presuppose the fact of scientific truth is aimed directly at the Marburg school, for whom that fact was the starting point.

The laying of the ground must rather trace the a priori synthesis back within itself alone to the germs underlying it, which let that synthesis grow into what it is — which make it possible in its essence. Kant himself says of the *Critique*, out of clear insight into the peculiarity of a laying of the ground of metaphysics, that the work is difficult and requires a resolute reader willing to think himself little by little into a system that lays nothing as given at the ground except reason itself, and thus, without supporting itself on any fact, seeks to develop knowledge out of its original germs (*Prolegomena* §4). The word "germs" (*Keime*) is Kant's own, and Heidegger takes it as authorization for the regress of the Third Section: a germ is what a thing grows from, so following the synthesis back to its germs is not construction imposed on Kant but the programme Kant announced.

So arises the task of showing how this development of the possibility of ontology out of its germs is carried out.

## **Zweiter Abschnitt: Die Grundlegung der Metaphysik in der Durchführung**

Before the inner possibility of ontological knowledge can be projected, a survey must already have opened onto the dimension of the regress to the bearing ground. It is the necessary fate of every genuine break-in to a hitherto concealed field that the field determines itself only gradually; the direction of access consolidates, and the way becomes passable, only in the course of the advance. So even where the first break-in remains guided by the security and unerring directive force of creative disclosing, an explicit systematic clearing and marking-out of the field is at first lacking. "Critiques require knowledge of the sources, and reason must know itself" — yet Kant works out this more original self-knowledge of reason only through the *Critique*. What Kant lacked is not insight but a map; and the excuse Heidegger offers him here is the same one he will withdraw in the Third Section, where the failure to follow the root is no longer a matter of missing cartography.

The interpretation that comes after, by contrast, is neither yet nor any longer in possession of the original directive force of the projecting, and must therefore secure the guiding survey explicitly in advance and anticipate the main stages of the inner movement of the whole. Before the execution can be re-enacted, the survey of the dimension of regress has to be supplied. Coming late has one advantage — the whole is visible — and one deficit, the loss of the discoverer's instinct; §§4–8 exist to compensate for the second by exploiting the first.

The Section accordingly falls into two parts: the characterization of the dimension of regress, and the stages of the execution of the projection of the inner possibility of ontology.

### **A. Die Kennzeichnung der Rückgangsdimension für die Durchführung der Grundlegung der Metaphysik**

The task is the essential determination of ontological knowledge by illuminating its origin out of the germs that make it possible. That requires clarity above all about the essence of knowing as such, and about the place and kind of the field of origin. Precisely this prior and sufficient characterization of the dimension of origin has been unduly neglected or misinterpreted in the interpretation of the *Critique* to date, so that, given the further wavering about the work's intention, no productive appropriation of its basic tendency could succeed. Along with the characterization of the field, the manner of the unveiling of the origin must also be marked out in its peculiarity.

#### **I. Die wesentlichen Charaktere des Ursprungsfeldes**

##### **§ 4. Das Wesen der Erkenntnis überhaupt**

Kant does not discuss the essential characters of the field of origin explicitly and thematically but takes them up more in the manner of "self-evident presuppositions." All the less may the interpretation overlook the function these positings exercise in advance. A marginal note asks for the point to be developed more sharply by starting from the difference between knowledge as representing and knowledge as knowing, with knowledge as knowing the guiding concept — a sharpening Heidegger carried out only in the 1935/36 lectures on the thing.

The presuppositions gather into a thesis. The source-ground for the laying of the ground of metaphysics is human pure reason, and in such a way that precisely the *humanity* of reason — its finitude — becomes essential for the core of the problem. The characterization of the field of origin must therefore concentrate on clarifying the essence of the finitude of human knowledge. This finitude by no means consists first or chiefly in the fact that human knowing exhibits all sorts of defects of inconstancy, inexactness and error; it lies in the essential structure of knowledge itself. The factical limitedness of knowing is only a consequence of that essence. Finitude is thus removed at the outset from the register of complaint. It is not that we know less than a god; it is that the structure by which we know anything at all is the structure of being dependent on what we did not make.

Exhibiting the essence of finitude requires a general characterization of the essence of knowing, and in this respect what Kant says in the first sentence of the thematic discussion of the *Critique* is usually valued far too low: whatever the way and the means by which a knowledge may relate to objects, intuition is that by which it relates to them immediately, and to which all thinking as a means is directed (A 19, B 33, italicized by Kant himself). For any understanding of the *Critique* one must hammer in: knowing is primarily intuiting. The marginalia press the point in both directions. Intuiting means here having the being itself manifest by letting it be given receptively; and "primarily" means at the bottom of knowing's essence *as finite*, so that thinking belongs necessarily to that same essence as the secondary — secondary in the order of the structure's build-up, not in the popular sense of "basically dispensable." Precisely because knowing is primarily intuition, an intuition on its own is never for us a knowledge.

Reinterpreting knowledge as judging (thinking) therefore violates the decisive sense of Kant's problem, since all thinking has merely a serving position with respect to intuition. Thinking is not "also" present alongside intuition; by its inner structure it serves what intuition primarily and constantly aims at. But if thinking is to be essentially related to intuition, both must have a certain inner kinship permitting their unification, and that kinship — descent from the same genus — is expressed in Kant's remark that for both "representation in general (repraesentatio)" is the genus (A 320, B 376 f.). A further marginal note refuses to let "serving" be heard as subordination: the understanding does indeed tower over intuition — in finitude and in neediness — and the greater that primacy, the more unconditional the dependence on intuition, and the less it can be dispensed with.

"Representation" here first has the wide formal sense in which something indicates, announces, presents another. Such representing can be performed "with consciousness"; there belongs to it a knowing of the announcing and of the being-announced (*perceptio*). And when in representing something through something not only the representing but what is represented in it is represented as such, that is, made conscious, then such representing is a relating-oneself to what presents itself in the representing. Knowledge is a representing in this sense of "objective perception."

Knowing representation is either intuition or concept (*intuitus vel conceptus*): the former relates immediately to the object and is singular, the latter mediately, by means of a mark that can be common to several things. On the first sentence of the *Critique*, then, knowing is a *thinking intuiting*. Thinking — representing "in general" — serves only to make the individual object, the concrete being itself in its immediacy, accessible, and accessible for everyone. "Each of these two is indeed representation, but not yet knowledge." A marginal note adds Kant's polemical placement against Locke and Leibniz: sensibility and understanding are "two entirely different sources of representations," which blocks in advance the reading of the difference as one of degrees of clarity.

One could infer from this a reciprocal and entirely equally weighted relation between intuiting and thinking, so that one might say with equal right: knowing is an intuiting thinking, hence at bottom judging.

Against that it must be held fast that intuition makes up the proper essence of knowledge and, for all the reciprocity of the relation, carries the proper weight. This emerges not only from Kant's declaration and from his own italicization of the word, but from the fact that only on this interpretation can the essential thing in the definition be grasped — the finitude of knowledge. The first sentence of the *Critique* is already no longer a definition of knowing as such but the essential determination of *human* knowledge: "as regards the human being" — in distinction from "God or another higher spirit" — "every knowledge of his consists of concept and intuition." Reading the sentence as a definition of knowledge as such is what produces the epistemological Kant; reading it as a determination of finite knowledge is what produces the metaphysical one.

The essence of finite human knowledge is elucidated by setting it off against the idea of infinite divine knowledge, the *intuitus originarius* (B 72). But divine knowledge is intuition not because it is divine; it is intuition because it is knowledge at all. The difference between infinite and finite intuition consists in this: infinite intuition, in immediately representing the individual — the one unique being as a whole — first brings this being into its being and helps it to its arising (*origo*). Absolute intuiting would not be absolute if it were dependent on an already present being, in conformity with which the intuitable first became accessible. Divine knowing is that representing which in intuiting first creates the intuitable being as such. And because it intuits beings immediately as a whole, seeing through them absolutely in advance, it needs no thinking. Thinking as such is therefore already the seal of finitude: divine knowledge is "intuition (for all his knowledge must be such and not thinking, which always proves limits)" (B 71). A marginal note supplies the correlate — such knowing is free of all sensibility and at the same time free of the need to know through concepts. The infinite intellect is not a superior version of ours but a structurally different one, and it is introduced solely as a contrast-foil: Heidegger is not asserting that it exists.

The decisive difference would be missed, however, if one said: divine knowing is only intuiting, human knowing is a thinking intuiting. The essential difference lies primarily in the intuiting itself, since knowing is properly intuition. The finitude of human knowledge must accordingly be sought first in the finitude of its own intuition. That a finite knowing being must "also" think is only an essential consequence of the finitude of its intuiting. Only so does the essential serving position of "all thinking" come into the right light. The whole architecture of the book depends on locating finitude in receptivity rather than in discursivity: if finitude lay in thinking, its overcoming would lie in a better intuition, and the transcendental imagination would have no work to do.

##### **§ 5. Das Wesen der Endlichkeit der Erkenntnis**

Negatively put: finite knowledge is non-creative intuition. What it has to present immediately in its singularity must already be present beforehand. Finite intuition finds itself dependent on the intuitable as something already a being of itself. What is intuited is derived from such a being, which is why this intuition is also called *intuitus derivativus*, derived intuition (B 72). Finite intuition of beings cannot give itself the object; it must let it be given to it. Not every intuition as such, but only finite intuition, is receptive. A marginal note blocks the obvious mistake: "derived" does not mean deduced from absolute intuition, and finite knowing is not a derivative of the absolute — the question of ontic origin is not at issue at all. "Derived" names a structural feature, that finite knowing comes to what it knows from elsewhere instead of creating it.

The character of the finitude of intuition therefore lies in receptivity. But finite intuition cannot take in unless what is to be taken in announces itself; by its essence it must be approached, affected, by what is intuitable in it. Affection is thus deduced from receptivity rather than presupposed as a physical fact — which is what allows the next move.

Because the essence of knowledge lies primarily in intuition, and because the finite essence of the human being stands in the theme, Kant continues immediately after the first sentence: intuition "takes place only insofar as the object is given to us; but this in turn is possible, at least for us humans, only in that it affects the mind in a certain way" (A 19, B 33). The qualification "at least for us humans" is inserted only in the second edition, and it only makes clearer that in the first edition finite knowledge was the theme from the outset. Heidegger needs this, since the second edition is elsewhere his evidence of Kant's retreat; here the addition is a clarification, not a change of mind, and he says so.

Because human intuition as finite takes in, and the possibility of a receptive "getting" demands affection, instruments of affection — the "senses" — are in fact necessary. Human intuition is not "sensible" because its affection happens by means of sense-organs; the reverse: because our Dasein is finite, existing in the midst of beings that already are and delivered over to them, it must take in what already is, that is, offer beings the possibility of announcing themselves. For the possible transmission of that announcement, instruments are necessary. The essence of sensibility consists in the finitude of intuition; the organs serving affection are *sense*-organs because they belong to finite intuition. With this Kant won for the first time an ontological, non-sensualist concept of sensibility. And since empirically affective intuition of beings therefore does not necessarily coincide with "sensibility," the possibility of a *non-empirical sensibility* remains essentially open — pure intuition, space and time, as Kant's own note at B 146 f. confirms. This inference is the linchpin of the whole reading. If sensibility means dependence rather than sensation, a pure sensibility is not a contradiction, and the way is clear for time to be the ground of ontological knowledge.

Knowledge is primarily intuition, a representing that immediately represents the being itself. But if finite intuition is to be knowledge, it must be able to make the being itself accessible as manifest, for everyone and at all times, in what and how it is. Finite intuiting beings must be able to share the intuition with one another. Yet finite intuition, as intuition, at first remains attached to the individual thing intuited on each occasion. What is intuited is a known being only if anyone can make it intelligible to himself and to others and thereby communicate it. This intuited individual, this piece of chalk here, must be determinable as chalk or as body, so that we can together know this being itself as the same for us. To be knowledge, finite intuition needs at all times such a determination of the intuited as this or that. Universality is thus derived from the demand that beings be the same for several knowers, not from a doctrine of concepts — the need for the understanding is a consequence of finitude's isolation in the singular.

In such determining, what is represented in intuition is further represented with a view to what it is "in general." The determining does not thematically represent the universal as such: it does not make the bodiliness of the thing into an object, but takes a look toward the universal only in order, with that in view, to direct itself to the individual and determine it from that look. This "universal" representing, which as such serves intuiting, makes what is represented in intuition more representable, in that it comprehends several under one and on the ground of this embracing "holds for many." Kant therefore calls this representing in general (*repraesentatio per notas communes*) "representing in concepts," and determining representing shows up as the "representation [concept] of a representation" (intuition). Heidegger's marginal note supplies the reason the text leaves implicit: the receptive representing becomes, through the concept, more *taking*, and only so can it have beings at all.

Determining representing is in itself an asserting of something about something, a predication. "The judgment is thus the mediate knowledge of an object, hence the representation of a representation of it" (A 68, B 93). The "faculty of judging" is the understanding, and the representing proper to it makes intuition intelligible.

Insofar as judging determination is essentially dependent on intuition, thinking always unites with intuition in serving it. Through such uniting (synthesis) thinking relates mediately to the object, and the object becomes manifest — true — in the unity of a thinking intuition. The synthesis of thinking and intuiting accordingly performs the making-manifest of the encountered being as object. Heidegger names it the truth-making, *veritative* synthesis, and identifies it with the "supplying" of the substantive determinateness of the being itself discussed in §3. Truth is thereby located in a synthesis that is not the joining of subject and predicate — a displacement that will be decisive when the veritative synthesis reappears in its pure form as the ontological synthesis.

The thinking that unites with intuition in the veritative synthesis is for its part, as judging, a uniting in another sense. Kant: a judgment is the representation of the unity of consciousness of various representations, or the representation of their relation insofar as they make up a concept (*Logik* §17). Judgments are "functions of unity," a representing of the unifying unity of the concept in its predicate-character. Heidegger names this the *predicative* synthesis.

It does not coincide with the uniting in which judging presents itself as the connecting of subject and predicate. That last synthesis Heidegger names the *apophantic*.

In the veritative synthesis, which makes up the essence of finite knowledge as such, the predicative and the apophantic syntheses are necessarily joined into one structural unity of syntheses. Three syntheses are thus nested rather than juxtaposed: the making-manifest of the being contains within itself the concept's unification of many under one, which in turn is articulated in the subject-predicate connection.

Anyone who asserts that for Kant the essence of knowledge is "synthesis" says nothing at all so long as the word is left in its ambiguous indeterminacy. The distinction just drawn is what makes the assertion sayable — and what will let Heidegger identify a fourth, pure synthesis that no analysis of judgment can reach.

Finite intuition, needing determination, is dependent on the understanding. The understanding not only belongs to the finitude of intuition but is itself still more finite, since it lacks even the immediacy of finite intuiting. Its representing needs a detour, a look toward a universal through which and from which the several individuals become conceptually representable. This detour-character, discursivity, belonging to the essence of the understanding, is the sharpest index of its finitude. The understanding is thus doubly demoted: it is dependent, and it is mediate. The tradition's ranking of intellect above sense is exactly inverted, and inverted on the tradition's own criterion of immediacy.

Yet just as the metaphysical essence of finite intuition as receptivity retains in itself the general essential character of intuition — that it is "giving" — so the finitude of the understanding still shows something of the essence of an absolute knowledge, an original, letting-spring-forth intuition. The understanding, related to finite intuition, is as little creative as intuition is; it never produces beings. But in distinction from the taking-in of intuiting it is a kind of bringing-forth. Judging about beings does not simply produce the universal in which the intuited is conceptually represented — the universal is drawn according to its content from what is intuitable. Only the manner in which this content holds as an embracing unity for many is the accomplishment of the understanding.

In producing the form of the concept the understanding helps to supply the content of the object. In this kind of "placing," the peculiar re-presenting of thinking announces itself — the word *Vor-stellen*, placing-before, is broken open to show a placing at work in it. The metaphysical essence of the understanding as thus "producing" is co-determined by this character of "from itself" (spontaneity), but not hit at its core. Spontaneity is conceded and then set aside; it is a true description of the understanding that fails to reach what the understanding is, and the Third Section will supply what does reach it.

The finitude of knowledge has so far been characterized as receptive and therefore thinking intuition, an illumination carried out with a view to the structure of knowing. Given the fundamental significance of finitude for the problem, the essence of finite knowledge is now to be illuminated from another side: with a view to what is knowable in such knowledge.

If finite knowledge is receptive intuition, then the knowable must show itself from itself. What finite knowledge can make manifest is essentially the self-showing being — the appearing, appearance. The title "appearance" means the being itself as object of finite knowledge. More precisely: only for finite knowledge is there anything like an object at all, since only finite knowledge is delivered over to what already is. Nothing already existing can stand over against infinite knowledge as something it would have to direct itself by; such directing-oneself-according-to would already be dependence, hence finitude. Infinite knowing is an intuiting that as such lets the being itself arise. Absolute knowing reveals the being to itself in letting it arise and has it manifest at all times "only" as arising — as *Ent-stand*. The coinage is built on *Gegen-stand*, what stands over against: where the finite knower has beings as standing over against it, the absolute knower has them as standing forth in their very arising. Insofar as the being is manifest for absolute intuition, it "is" precisely in its coming-to-be; it is the being as being in itself, not as object. So even the formula that this intuiting first produces the "object" in intuiting fails to hit the essence of infinite knowledge. A marginal note states the consequence baldly: objectivity is being in the empirical sense — that is, "object" is not a neutral term for what is, but the way beings are for a finite knower.

The being "in appearance" is the same being as the being in itself; indeed only this one being can become an object, though only for a finite knowledge. It reveals itself according to the manner and range of the ability to take in and determine that a finite knowledge has at its disposal. A marginal note fixes the identity precisely: what is the same is not the *what* but the *that* of the X. The thing in itself and the appearance are the same being, not the same content.

Kant uses "appearance" in a narrower and a wider meaning. Appearances in the wider sense (*phaenomena*) are a kind of "object," namely the being itself which finite knowing, as thinking receptive intuition, makes manifest. Appearance in the narrower sense means that in appearances in the wider sense which is exclusively the correlate of the affection belonging to finite intuition, stripped of all thinking: the contents of empirical intuition. "The undetermined object of an empirical intuition is called appearance" (A 20, B 34); appearing means "to be an object of empirical intuition" (A 89, B 121).

Appearances are not mere semblance but the being itself, and this being is not something other than the things in themselves but just this one being. The being itself can be manifest without the being being known "in itself," that is, as *Ent-stand*. The double characterization of the being as "thing in itself" and as "appearance" corresponds to the twofold way in which it can stand in relation to infinite and to finite knowing: the being in its arising, and the same being as object. The thing in itself is thus not a second object hidden behind the first but the same object correlated to a knowing that we do not have.

If the finitude of the human being is the problem-basis for the grounding of ontology in the *Critique*, then the work must lay particular weight on this difference between finite and infinite knowing. Hence Kant says of it that "it teaches us to take the object in two meanings, namely as appearance or as thing in itself" (B xxvii). Strictly one should not speak of "object" at all, since for absolute knowledge there can be no ob-jects — and a marginal note sharpens even that: for God there is no *being* at all, if being belongs to finitude. The *Opus postumum* confirms the reading: the thing in itself is not another being than the appearance, "the distinction of the concepts of a thing in itself and of the thing in appearance is not objective but merely subjective. The thing in itself is not another object but another relation (*respectus*) of the representation to the same object." A further marginal note glosses the noumenon accordingly, as a representation of reason that is not a particular object standing behind the phenomenon. Another note restrains the claim about finitude being the "problem-basis": finitude is not Kant's explicit theme — it is what his theme presupposes, and the difference between those two matters for how much Heidegger may claim to be reporting rather than interpreting.

From this reading, the expressions "behind the appearance" and "mere appearance" can now be clarified. The "behind" cannot mean that the thing in itself does after all stand over against finite knowledge, is merely not grasped "completely," presses its essence forward and occasionally becomes indirectly visible. It expresses rather that finite knowledge as finite necessarily at the same time conceals, and conceals in advance, so that the "thing in itself" is not merely imperfectly but essentially not at all accessible to it as such. What is "behind the appearance" is the same being as the appearance; but because finite knowledge gives the being only in the ob-ject, it does not let that same being be seen as *Ent-stand*. "According to the Critique everything in an appearance is itself again appearance." Concealing is thereby made a positive structural feature of finite knowing rather than a shortfall in it — the Kantian form of the thesis that untruth belongs to the essence of truth, and the passage to hold against §35's problem of transcendental untruth.

It is accordingly a misunderstanding of what "thing in itself" means to believe one must prove by positivistic critique the impossibility of knowing things in themselves. Such attempts still presuppose that the thing in itself is something intended as an object within finite knowledge, whose factical inaccessibility could and would have to be demonstrated. Correspondingly, in the phrase "mere appearance" the "mere" is not a restriction or diminution of the thing's reality but only the denial that the being is known infinitely in human knowledge: "even to the deepest investigation of its objects, in the world of sense, we have to do with nothing but appearances" (A 45, B 62 f.).

The essence of the difference shows itself especially clearly in the double meaning of "outside us" (A 373). In both meanings the being itself is always meant. As thing in itself it is outside us insofar as we, as finite beings, are excluded from the kind of infinite intuition that belongs to it. As appearance it is outside us insofar as we ourselves are not this being and yet do have access to it. Conversely, the discussion shows that these fundamental concepts of the *Critique* can be understood and made into a further problem only when the problematic of the finitude of the human essence is explicitly laid at the ground. They are not two layers of objects stacked behind one another within an indifferently posited "knowledge." Every reading that takes the distinction as one between two sorts of thing has failed at the first step, and Heidegger has now made finitude the sole key to it.

With this characterization of the finitude of human knowledge, the essential features of the dimension within which the laying of the ground moves have been indicated, and with them a clearer prefiguring of the direction the regress to the sources of the inner possibility of ontology must take.

##### **§ 6. Der Quellgrund der Grundlegung der Metaphysik**

The interpretation of the essence of knowledge and of its finitude yielded: finite intuition (sensibility) needs as such to be determined by the understanding, and conversely the already finite understanding is dependent on intuition, "for we can understand nothing except what carries with it in intuition something corresponding to our words" (A 277, B 333). When Kant says that "neither of these properties is to be preferred to the other" (A 51, B 75), this seems to contradict his placing the basic character of knowing in intuition. But the necessary belonging of sensibility and understanding to the essential unity of finite knowledge does not exclude but includes a rank-order in the structural grounding of thinking upon intuition as the leading representing. Precisely this rank-order must not be overlooked in view of their reciprocal belonging-together, nor levelled to an indifferent correlation of content and form, by anyone who wants to come nearer the innermost drift of Kant's problem. Kant's parity claim concerns indispensability, Heidegger's rank-claim concerns founding; they are compatible, and the form/content picture is what conflates them.

For the question of the regress to the source-ground of the possibility of finite knowledge it might now seem enough to stop at the simple reciprocal duality of its elements — the more so as Kant himself orients the "arising" of our knowledge on "two basic sources of the mind," the first being the receiving of representations (the receptivity of impressions), the second the faculty of knowing an object through these representations (the spontaneity of concepts) (A 50, B 74); and says still more sharply that besides these two sources of knowledge we have "no others" (A 294, B 350).

But this duality of sources is no mere juxtaposition. Only in the unification prefigured by their structure can a finite knowledge be what its essence demands: "only from their uniting can knowledge arise" (A 51, B 75 f.). And the unity of this uniting is no subsequent result of the elements' having come together; what unites them, this "synthesis," must let the elements themselves arise in their belonging-together and unity. If finite knowledge has its essence precisely in the original synthesis of the basic sources, and if the laying of the ground must press through to the essential ground of finite knowledge, then already at the prefiguring naming of the "two basic sources" a pointer to their source-ground — an original unity — suggests itself. The argument turns on a single claim, and it is the claim that carries the rest of the book: a unity that first lets its members be what they are cannot be assembled out of them. Hence the root cannot be a compound of the stems.

Kant does in fact give, both in the Introduction to the *Critique* and at its close, a remarkable characterization beyond the mere enumeration. In the Introduction: "only so much seems necessary by way of introduction or preliminary reminder, that there are two stems of human knowledge, which perhaps spring from a common but to us unknown root, namely sensibility and understanding, through the first of which objects are given to us, but through the second thought" (A 15, B 29). And in the Architectonic: "we content ourselves here with the completion of our business, namely merely to sketch the architectonic of all knowledge from pure reason, and begin only from the point where the general root of our cognitive power divides and throws out two stems, one of which is reason. Here, however, I understand by reason the whole higher faculty of knowledge, and thus oppose the rational to the empirical" (A 835, B 863). The "empirical" here means the experiencing receptive, receptivity, sensibility as such.

The "sources" are here grasped as "stems" springing from a common root. But at the first passage the "common root" is named with a "perhaps," while at the second "the general root" counts as existing. At both places, nevertheless, the root is only pointed at. Kant not only does not pursue it but even designates it as "unknown to us." From this follows the one essential thing about the general character of Kant's laying of the ground: it does not lead to the sun-clear absolute evidence of a first proposition and principle but knowingly goes, and points, into the unknown. It is a *philosophizing* laying of the ground of philosophy. The discrepancy between "perhaps" and "the general root" is the textual crack Heidegger works in the Third Section. And the closing formula is a thrust at Husserl as much as at the neo-Kantians: a grounding that terminates in absolute evidence would have made philosophy into a science, and Kant's refusal of that is here counted to his credit.

#### **II. Die Weise der Ursprungsenthüllung**

##### **§ 7. Die Vorzeichnung der Stadien der Grundlegung der Ontologie**

The grounding of metaphysics is the projection of the inner possibility of the a priori synthesis: its essence must be determined and its origin presented out of its source-ground. The elucidation of finite knowledge and the characterization of the basic sources have circumscribed the dimension in which the origin is to be unveiled. With that, the question of the inner possibility of synthetic a priori knowledge receives both a sharpening and a complication.

The preparatory laying-out of the problem yielded that knowledge of beings is possible only on the ground of a prior, experience-free knowledge of the constitution of being of beings. But finite knowledge, whose finitude is in question, is by its essence a receptive determining intuition of beings. If finite knowledge of beings is to be possible, it must ground itself on a knowing of the being of beings that lies before all receiving. Finite knowledge of beings therefore requires for its own possibility a non-receptive — apparently non-finite — knowing, something like a "creative" intuiting.

So the question of the possibility of the a priori synthesis sharpens: how can a finite being, which as such is delivered over to beings and dependent on taking them in, know — that is, intuit — beings before all taking-in, without being their "creator"? Put otherwise: how must this finite being be according to its own constitution of being for such an experience-free supplying of the constitution of being of beings, an ontological synthesis, to be possible? The paradox is stated as sharply as possible rather than softened, and the whole remainder of the book is its resolution: the answer will be that the finite knower produces not the being but the horizon within which beings can be met, and that it does so by being time.

If the question is posed this way, and every knowledge as finite is doubled into the two named elements and hence is itself synthesis, a peculiar complication enters the question of the possibility of the a priori synthesis. For this synthesis is not identical with the veritative synthesis, which concerns only ontic knowledge.

The ontological synthesis is, as knowledge at all, already synthetic, so the laying of the ground must begin by exhibiting the pure elements — pure intuition and pure thinking — of a pure knowledge. Then it must be illuminated what character the original essential unity of these pure elements, the pure veritative synthesis, has. This unity is to be such that it likewise determines pure intuition a priori: the concepts belonging to it must arise before all experience not only in their conceptual form but also in their content. Which means that the pure predicative synthesis necessarily belonging to the pure veritative synthesis is one of a distinguished kind, and that the question of the essence of the "ontological predicates" must move into the centre of the problem. Concepts whose *content* is a priori are not concepts in the ordinary sense at all, and naming this in advance is what makes the categories a problem rather than a list.

The question of the inner possibility of the essential unity of a pure veritative synthesis presses still further back, to the illumination of the original ground of that inner possibility. Only through unveiling the essence of pure synthesis from its ground does the insight arise how far ontological knowledge can be the condition of the possibility of ontic knowledge; and with that the full essence of ontological truth is delimited.

The laying of the ground of ontology accordingly runs through five stages: the essential elements of pure knowledge; the essential unity of pure knowledge; the inner possibility of the essential unity of the ontological synthesis; the ground of the inner possibility of the ontological synthesis; and the full essential determination of ontological knowledge. The five stages are not Kant's own division and do not answer to the Aesthetic, Analytic and Dialectic; they are the "inner drift" Heidegger claims to read behind the architectonic, and the claim will be defended in the introduction to part B.

##### **§ 8. Die Methode der Ursprungsenthüllung**

Already the preliminary characterization of the essential structure of finite knowledge showed a wealth of interconnected structures of synthesis. Insofar as the pure veritative synthesis contains the idea of a knowledge that in a certain sense apparently is not finite, the question of the possibility of ontology for a finite being became complicated. And the indication of the source-ground of the basic sources led into the unknown.

Given this character of the guiding problem and of the dimension in which it can be worked on, it is not surprising that the kind of unveiling of the origin and the way of the regress into the source-ground remain at first indeterminate. Their security and determinacy grow only in the course of the advance into the hitherto concealed region and in the confrontation with what shows itself there. Method cannot be settled in advance of a domain that is itself not yet secured; this is Heidegger's standing objection to method-first philosophy, and here it is Kant's own procedure that exemplifies it.

The region of the unveiling of the origin is, to be sure, nothing other than the human "mind" (*mens sive animus*), and its disclosure would be assigned to "psychology." Insofar as an interpretation of "knowledge" is at issue, whose essence commonly counts as judging (*logos*), "logic" must be involved in the disclosure of the mind as well. Taken externally, "psychology" and "logic" will divide the task, or quarrel over primacy, expanding and reshaping themselves as convenient.

But considering on the one hand the originality and incomparability of what Kant is seeking, and on the other the questionableness of the traditional "logic" and "psychology," which are not cut for such a problematic at all, it proves hopeless to grasp what is essential in Kant's laying of the ground by the guiding thread of a "logical" or "psychological" formulation of the question, or in an external linking of the two. That "transcendental psychology" is only the expression of an embarrassment becomes clear as soon as one has grasped the fundamental and methodological difficulties that face the determination of the finite essence of the human being. The label is dismissed not because it is unkind to Kant but because it names a discipline that does not exist: there is no psychology of what makes psychology's object possible.

So only one course remains: to leave the method of the unveiling of the origin open and not press it prematurely into a traditional or invented discipline. In leaving the character of the method open, one must keep in memory what Kant himself said about the work immediately after finishing the *Critique*: "this kind of investigation will always remain difficult" (letter to Marcus Herz, 1781).

A general pointer at the fundamental character of the procedure is nevertheless needed. The kind of investigation can be grasped as "analytic" in the widest sense: it concerns finite pure reason with a view to how, from the ground of its essence, it makes something like ontological synthesis possible. Kant therefore designates the *Critique* as a "study of our inner nature" (A 703, B 731), and this unveiling of the essence of human Dasein is for the philosopher "even a duty." A marginal note refers the procedure to transcendental reflection, which locates it within Kant's own vocabulary rather than Heidegger's.

"Analytic" here does not mean a dissolving and smashing of finite pure reason into elements, but on the contrary a "dissolving" in the sense of a loosening laying-free of the germs of ontology — a marginal note makes the sense unmistakable by glossing it as making fluid, bringing into flow. It unveils those conditions that let an ontology as a whole germinate according to its inner possibility. Such analytic is, in Kant's own words, a bringing "to light by reason itself" of "what reason wholly brings forth out of itself" (A xx). Analytic thus becomes a letting-be-seen of the genesis of the essence of finite pure reason out of its own ground. Analysis is inverted into genesis, which is why the Kantbuch can end in a doctrine of temporality rather than in a table of conditions.

Such analytic therefore contains a projecting anticipation of the whole inner essence of finite pure reason. Only by constructing through this essence does the essential structure of ontology become visible; and as so unveiled, that structure at the same time determines the construction of the foundations it requires. This projecting laying-free of everything that essentially makes an ontology possible brings metaphysics onto the ground and soil in which it is rooted as a "visitation" (*Heimsuchung*) of human nature. Kant's word at B xv for metaphysics as an affliction that keeps returning is taken over without irony: metaphysics is not a discipline one takes up but something that happens to a finite being, and the Fourth Section will make that the whole content of the question about the human being.

### **B. Die Stadien der Durchführung des Entwurfs der inneren Möglichkeit der Ontologie**

At this point the interpretation must assure itself anew, and with increasing sharpness, of the guiding problem. What is asked is the essential possibility of the ontological synthesis. Laid out, the question runs: how can finite human Dasein in advance cross over — transcend — beings that it has not only not created itself, but on which it is even dependent in order to be able to exist as Dasein at all? The problem of the possibility of ontology is thus the question of the essence and the essential ground of the transcendence of the prior understanding of being. The problem of the transcendental — that is, transcendence-forming — synthesis can therefore also be posed thus: how must the finite being we call the human being be according to its innermost essence, in order to be able to be open at all to beings that it is not itself and that must therefore be able to show themselves from themselves? Transcendence has now been defined entirely in terms of openness to what one did not make, which is why the answer cannot be a faculty of the subject in the ordinary sense; and the word Dasein enters the argument at exactly the point where the question turns from knowledge to the constitution of being of the knower.

The stages of an answer were prefigured above; now they are to be run through individually, though without an interpretation that exhausts everything equally. The interpretation follows the inner movement of Kant's laying of the ground without holding to his own disposition and its formulation. It must go back behind that disposition, in order to be able to estimate, from a more original understanding of the inner drift, the appropriateness, the right and the limits of the outer architectonic of the *Critique of Pure Reason*. The methodological licence for everything that follows is stated openly here: Heidegger is not claiming to expound Kant's order but to recover the movement that order imperfectly registers, and he undertakes in advance to justify the departure by the light it sheds on the architectonic itself.

# **Commentary on Heidegger, *Einführung in die phänomenologische Forschung* (GA 17\)**

A paragraph-by-paragraph commentary on the Marburg lecture course of Winter Semester 1923/24, covering the main lecture text (GA 17, pp. 2–290) — the Vorbemerkung and all three Parts, through §50. The Beilagen/Ergänzungen (pp. 293 ff.) are not included here.

Citations give the GA 17 page. Greek and Latin terms are kept and glossed on first use; a small glossary of German terms of art is retained untranslated where an English word would blur them: **Dasein** (the being that we ourselves are, understood as being-in-a-world, not "consciousness" or "subject"), **Sorge** (care/concern — here almost always the *Sorge um erkannte Erkenntnis*, the care for known knowledge that Heidegger makes the guiding structure), **Existenz**, and the paired **Verfallen** (falling) and **Verhängnis** (doom, the self-inflicted fate of a tradition run to seed). The word **Schein** (mere seeming, semblance) is left standing against **Erscheinung** (appearance-of-something-else), since the whole course turns on keeping them apart.

The argument of the course, stated once so the reader can hold the arc: "phenomenology" is built from φαινόμενον and λόγος, and recovering what those Greek words named exposes how far the *actual* phenomenology of Heidegger's day — Husserl's — has drifted from the things themselves. The drift has a single root, which the course names *Sorge um erkannte Erkenntnis*: the care to secure knowledge as absolutely certain. Part I recovers the Greek sense of the two words and then shows this care governing Husserl's *Logical Investigations* and the programmatic essays. Part II runs the *Destruktion* back through Descartes — where certainty (certum) is substituted for truth (verum) — and behind Descartes to the scholastic and ultimately Aristotelian ontology of the true. Part III returns to Husserl to show that he inherited Descartes' care for certainty undiscussed, and so missed the question of the *being* of the consciousness he made his theme; the course ends by pointing, past that omission, toward Dasein itself in its facticity, elusiveness, and uncanniness — the ground that *Sein und Zeit*, three years off, will occupy.

## **Vorbemerkung: The task of the lecture and the passion of genuine and rightful questioning**

The course has a double task: first, to fix and open up the horizon within which definite matters are to be expected — a preliminary orientation of the *Blickrichtung*, the direction of the gaze, and a stripping-away of mistaken expectations; second, the concrete working-through of those matters as they are brought near step by step. The order is not incidental. One cannot work on the matters before clearing the field of vision, and clearing it means removing what we falsely anticipate.

Two false expectations must go. Phenomenology is not journalistic information about a knack for "seeing essences." And — the more deeply seated error — no grounding, program, or system is on offer here; not even philosophy is to be expected. Heidegger states his conviction bluntly: philosophy is finished, and we stand before wholly new tasks that have nothing to do with the tradition. But this pronouncement is only a guide-thread; what alone counts are the matters (Tatbestände), and demarcation, classification, and polemic are secondary. The provocation is real but subordinated in the same breath: the death-of-philosophy claim earns its place only by clearing room for the matters, not as a thesis to be defended.

The task is threefold: clarify the expression "phenomenology"; bring into view the breakthrough of phenomenological research in Husserl's *Logical Investigations*; and bring into view how phenomenology develops from there — how far it is held fast, how far bent aside, and whether in the end its decisive meaning is given away. That last clause is the whole indictment of Part III announced in advance. The route is word-history: φαινόμενον and λόγος are two originary words of Greek philosophy, and from their change of meaning the emergence of the specific sense becomes intelligible. Insofar as these words *speak Dasein*, clarifying them moves us within the history of Western humanity's Dasein and its self-interpretation. From Husserl's self-interpretation of "phenomenology" just after the *Investigations*, one grasps how he takes and develops the research task. The main theme is fixed in advance: **Dasein** — world, dealings within it, temporality, language, Dasein's self-interpretation, and the possibilities of interpreting it.

No prior philosophical knowledge is presupposed. Three other things are: the passion of genuine and rightful questioning, which does not fall to one at will but has its time and tempo, and a readiness composed of three moments. First, a concern for an instinct-sure superiority *over* prejudice — not freedom *from* prejudices, which is utopian, since believing oneself to have none is the greatest prejudice of all. The scientific human is not free of prejudices but free *for* the possibility of giving one up at the decisive moment, out of the confrontation with the matter itself. Second, the care of becoming at home in a definite science. Science taken as a worked-up collection of material breeds surfeit with knowledge — the mark of present-day educated consciousness — and this very aspect is the *Verhängnis*, the doom, for one no longer understands what is actually going on. This cowardice before questioning often trims itself with religiosity, to which ultimate self-confronting questioning looks like presumption; one flees a fundamental possibility of Dasein. Third, the composure to face that life, given over to knowing inquiry, sooner leads anywhere else than to a comfortable "theoretical contemplation." Genuine readiness is a certain *maturity of existence*: not lapsing into surrogates, not aiming to finish quickly, but holding out for years in uncertainty, denying oneself every premature answer, and freeing oneself from the tradition — genuine in Greek philosophy — of scientific comportment *as theory*. The closing warning to his students turns the whole preface on the listener: you must not suppose you are *obliged to mean* what is meant here.

## **First Part: φαινόμενον and λόγος in Aristotle, and Husserl's self-interpretation of phenomenology**

### **Chapter One: Clarification of the expression "phenomenology" by way of a regress to Aristotle**

Chapter One reads *De anima* and *De interpretatione* to recover what the two component words of "phenomenology" once named, before their history buried it. Four traps govern the reading. φαινόμενον is not Erscheinung, the appearance of a hidden thing behind it; it means what shows itself from itself, and the drift toward "appearance-of-something" is exactly the degeneration Heidegger tracks. *De anima* is not psychology and αἴσθησις (perceiving) is not an Erlebnis (lived mental experience); perceiving is a living being's way of standing in its world. The realist/idealist opposition is absent from Greek thought, so Aristotle's "condition" of visibility must not be read as Kantian transcendental idealism — Heidegger says so outright. And the chapter's real target is language: the thesis it is driving toward is that speech, in its very facticity, is the source of deception, so that we mostly philosophize in words rather than in the things.

The word "phenomenology" first appears in the eighteenth century, in Lambert's *Neues Organon*, as the theory of appearance (Schein) — the doctrine of how to avoid being deceived by mere seeming. Kant uses a cognate; Hegel makes it the title of his chief work; nineteenth-century Protestant theology speaks of a phenomenology of the religions; Brentano used it in lectures Husserl heard. Two questions arise from this lineage. Why did Husserl choose the term? And how did φαινόμενον, which means what shows itself, ever come to mean Schein, mere seeming? The second question drives the whole chapter: a word for self-showing has turned into a word for deceptive semblance, and that reversal has a history worth uncovering.

One term must be kept out of play from the start: Erscheinung, "appearance." It corrupts the Greek by suggesting that a phenomenon is the appearing of something else standing behind it. Even the title *Περὶ ψυχῆς* (*On the Soul*) misleads if "soul" is heard modernly: for Aristotle, perceiving, thinking, and willing are not inner experiences, and *De anima* is not a psychology but an account of the being of a living thing in its world.

#### **§ 1\. Clarifying the φαινόμενον from Aristotle's analysis of perceiving the world by way of sight**

##### **a) The φαινόμενον as a distinctive mode of the presence of beings: presence in the day**

"Phenomenology" joins λόγος and φαινόμενον. φαινόμενον means: something that shows itself. The verb φαίνω means to bring to light; the stem φα- ties it to φῶς, light. Heidegger proposes to fix the matter behind the word by reading one concrete scientific text — *De anima* B7, on seeing — with all physics and physiology held off, since they would deflect the gaze from what Aristotle actually describes. No one, he notes, has since attempted so concrete an explication.

What is seeing, and how is the seen characterized? What sight takes in is the visible, and the visible shows up as color; color is spread over what is visible in itself, and it is taken in ἐν φωτί, in the light — more precisely, in brightness. So the prior question is what brightness is. Brightness is what lets one see *through* it (διαφανές, the transparent); it is not itself visible except through a foreign color. Brightness is the presence of fire, and Aristotle's discovery is that it is no body and does not move — Empedocles was wrong to make light travel. What moves is the sun, whose presence *is* the brightness. Brightness is thus a mode of presence (παρουσία), the way the daylit world lets things be seen: das Tagsein, being-in-the-day. This is the primary sense of φαινόμενον — a distinctive mode of the presence of beings.

Aristotle sorts the perceptible (αἰσθητά) three ways. An ἴδιον is what one and only one mode of perceiving reaches — color for sight, tone for hearing — and about it perception is always true (ἀεὶ ἀληθές). A κοινόν, like motion, is not cut to a single sense. A συμβεβηκός is what is perceived incidentally, and here deception is not only possible but the rule. The point that matters: in ordinary life I do not perceive bare color or bare tone but the singer's song — the incidental is what I actually encounter, and reaching the ἴδια takes an artificial, isolating stance. Perception, αἴσθησις, is a mode of a living thing's being in its world, not a mental event.

Now the guard against Kant. To say brightness is "the condition of the possible perceptibility of color" sounds Kantian, and Heidegger uses the phrase precisely to mark the difference. For Aristotle, "being a condition" concerns a way of being of the *world itself*: the presence of the sun belongs to the being of the daylit world, and the visibility of things stands under that worldly condition. It is not a condition supplied by a knowing subject. Heidegger states flatly that Aristotle and Kant are not to be opposed as realist and idealist — the opposition does not exist in Greek philosophy. The φαινόμενον is what shows itself as itself and is immediately there; whoever says brightness moves speaks παρὰ τὰ φαινόμενα, past what shows itself.

##### **b) The φαινόμενον as everything that shows itself in itself, in brightness or in darkness**

The concept reaches wider than daylit presence: a φαινόμενον is *anything* that shows itself from itself, whether in brightness or in darkness. This forces the question of darkness. If brightness is διαφανές, the letting-see, darkness looks like its mere privation, ἀδιαφανές. But darkness too lets something be seen — some things are visible only in the dark, like phosphorescent things. To hold this together Heidegger draws on Aristotle's cardinal distinction between ἐντελεχείᾳ ὄν (being-in-actuality) and δυνάμει ὄν (being-in-possibility). Darkness is a δυνάμει ὄν, something wholly positive: its being consists in *being possible brightness*. It is a στέρησις, an absence of what ought to be present — but a privation is not nothing, and our own category-scheme, Heidegger remarks, has not developed categories this originary. So the tidy formula "brightness lets see, darkness does not" misreads Aristotle: darkness lets see too.

He closes the section with a methodological warning that reaches past Aristotle. The basic concepts of philosophy, as they come down through history, are not a secure possession lying outside their genesis and immune to it. Inherited detached from their origin, they become our undoing (Verhängnis): the interpretation of the whole of Dasein gets shot through with concepts that are mere word-possession. This is the chapter's governing worry stated once, plainly — that we philosophize in words instead of in the things — and it is why the regress to the Greek sense of these words is not antiquarianism but a recovery of ground.

Both φαινόμενον and λόγος give expression to a matter, and it will become clear later from motives lying in Dasein itself how φαινόμενον can take on the sense "Schein," and how a philosophy gone external, running along the words, comes to grasp beings as "appearance of something." Aristotle had no such naïve metaphysics. To try today to criticize phenomenology by way of the word "appearance" (Heidegger names Rickert) is groundless. The φαινόμενον is what shows itself as a being; it is encountered by life insofar as life stands toward its world so as to see it, to take it in through αἴσθησις. The ἴδια αἰσθητά are what is perceived in the strict sense; but I ordinarily perceive κατὰ συμβεβηκός — houses, trees, people are there from the outset, and reaching the ἴδια needs an artificial stance. When the sun shows itself it is there a foot wide; it does not merely *appear* so.

Aristotle's originality in taking sight as his lead shows in his refusing to be thrown by the fact that there is no collective name for things that only the night lets be seen (fireflies and the like): such things are colored and speakable of, yet happen to be nameless. What matters is only that they *are*, are seen, and by their content have a claim to count as beings. That a name is lacking shows that our language — our doctrine of categories — is a language of the day. This holds especially of Greek, and one cannot fix it by building a category-scheme of the night; we must get back behind the day/night opposition to grasp why the day has its privilege. The recurring phrase παρὰ τὰ φαινόμενα brings out the special claim-character of the φαινόμενον: within science, what shows itself in itself is pressed into a foundational position, the ground for all further questioning. Science aims to grasp beings uncovered — σῴζειν τὰ φαινόμενα, to save the phenomena. The scientist's being is a definite comportment toward the being of the world, with two belonging-together determinations of this ἕξις: familiarity with the matters, and a παιδεία, a schooling that knows how to conduct itself in the field of inquiry and can tell whether an investigator draws what he says from the matter itself (καλῶς ἀποδίδωσιν) or merely chatters. From such παιδεία one decides which kind of inquiry a given object calls for — for instance, whether to treat a thing's being as secondary and speak first of its genesis. The answer: only once the ground for inquiry is laid can the question of origin be raised. In building a house what is first fixed is the εἶδος (the look, the way the house "takes itself out" in its surroundings as house), and only then the ὕλη. The φαινόμενον is the being itself.

#### **§ 2\. Aristotle's determination of λόγος**

##### **a) Speech (λόγος) as voice that means something (φωνὴ σημαντική); ὄνομα and ῥῆμα**

How does the φαινόμενον connect with what Aristotle explicates as λόγος? τὸ φαινόμενον is the being that a possible inquiry must so appropriate that it yields the ground for questioning. So φαινόμενον is not a category of apprehension but a way of being — a *how* of encountering, the first and, as first, the rightful one. The Greeks lacked the category "object" (Gegenstand); in its place stood πρᾶγμα, what one has to do with in dealings, what is present for concernful dealing. "Object," by contrast, is what stands over against a mere onlooker in the mode of bare looking-at. The accent of φαινόμενον falls entirely on the character of the Da, the there: what is always already there, met in the next glance, needing no disclosure yet often covered over.

λόγος is vocal being that means — voice: λόγος δέ ἐστι φωνὴ σημαντική. So the analysis descends. φωνή is a sound made by a living thing, a sound requiring πνεῦμα (breath), which like the tongue serves a double function — bodily warmth, and speech. Not every animal sound is voice; one can make bare noises, cough. What makes a sound voice is that φαντασία is present in it — here φαντασία in the plain sense of something's showing itself, self-display — so that with the sound something is to be taken in. On the basis of φαντασία the sound is called σημαντική, meaningful.

The name (ὄνομα) is a vocal thing intelligible as such. Its meaning is not given by the mere physiological capacity to speak: throat and tongue are φύσει, by nature, but a word is not. Words are κατὰ συνθήκην, by convention — each word has become, has its genesis. The word does not carry, once and for all, the fixed meaning that hits the matter; it is drawn not from primary experience of the thing but from prior opinions and nearest views of things. Its genesis is borne not by the physiological being of the human but by his existence proper: insofar as the human is in a world and wills something in it and with himself, he speaks. Yet the word is not present the way a tool is (οὐχ ὡς ὄργανον), like the hand. Language is the being and becoming of the human himself. What is named in a name is named so as to be lifted out of every time-determination (ἄνευ χρόνου): no detachable part of a name means anything on its own; the togetherness of the syllables is first founded by the unitary meaning, and only from it is the vocal articulation intelligible. A word is a word only when a vocal thing becomes σύμβολον — and Heidegger recovers the old sense: the broken halves of a ring that spouses or friends split at parting, so that at reunion each is known by fitting the other. The meaningful word points to its matter. There are also unarticulated sounds that make something known without meaning — a groan, ἀγράμματοι — which cannot be written or read because they run only on the sound, not on meaning.

λόγος is already used in ordinary language for a fundamental trait, and every interpretation of λόγος works from some prior opinion about its sense. We know vaguely what speaking is, but have no secure insight into what language meant for the Greeks in their natural Dasein — Hellenistic grammar, and the theory of knowledge after it, have covered over the question of how the Greek *lived* in his language. One thing is sure: the Greek lived in language in a distinctive way and was lived by it, and knew it. The being-able to address and discuss what is encountered — world and self — which need not be philosophy, Aristotle characterizes as the mark of being human: λόγον ἔχειν, to have speech. When someone speaks, he brings meaning to a stand: the hearer comes to rest in understanding the word (ὁ ἀκούσας ἠρέμησεν). To understand something is to *have* it, in the mode of understanding a naming of what is named. Against Plato, Aristotle insists that speaking, moving in language, arises from human free discretion and is not φύσει. And ὄνομα and ῥῆμα cannot really become a problem of how they "find their way together" into λόγος, because λόγος is the originary thing; name and verb are special *modifications* of it. In the ῥῆμα (verb), what shifts is the *how* of meaning: the κατάφασις drops to bare φάσις. The ῥῆμα co-signifies time (προσσημαίνει χρόνον) — "will die" — and always means with a view to another being; it has, at bottom, a relational character, marking out a circle of possible connections that is itself left indeterminate.

##### **b) The pointing-out speech (λόγος ἀποφαντικός), which uncovers (ἀληθεύειν) or covers (ψεύδεσθαι) the existing world in assertion (κατάφασις) and denial (ἀπόφασις); the ὁρισμός**

What is κατάφασις? Heidegger clarifies it by a detour. Since λόγος is not a tool but historical, growing from a given state of discovery of the matters, not every speaking accomplishes a pointing-out. Only that speaking is ἀποφαντικός in which ἀληθεύειν occurs: giving beings as unconcealed, or so giving them that in the pointing-out something is "put on," ψεύδεσθαι. ἀληθεύειν and ψεύδεσθαι — uncovering and covering — are the basic ways the ἀποφαντικός λόγος points out beings as beings. Not every λέγειν (asking, commanding, requesting) is "true or false"; but every λέγειν is δηλοῦν, disclosing — which must not be conflated with the theoretical uncovering that the modern habit of reducing all knowing to judgment presupposes.

Then a correction of the standard reading of "if something is added" (ἐάν τι προστεθῇ). This is read as though a sentence arises when further words are hung on a noun. But πρόσθεσις stands opposed to ἀφαίρεσις, abstraction — taking something away and standing it on its own, as geometry looks away from a thing's material to keep the bare spatial shape. πρόσθεσις means concretion: not adding the abstracted feature back, but positing what is posited *as a being*. So κατάφασις is alive beyond bare φάσις when what is meant in speaking is meant as a concrete being. There is λόγος when the speaking is a speaking *with the existing world*. Saying "stove" out of context, I do not speak from a Dasein; I step out of the concrete world and merely mean something, and whether stoves actually exist is then irrelevant to that meaning. Speaking is a being-with-the-world, something originary, lying *before* judgments — the judgment must be made intelligible from it, not the reverse. Logic's tradition of treating "Fire\!" as a judgment misses this: the point is not to establish fire's presence but to get people out of bed. λόγος needs no plurality of words. The originary word was a naming — not of a bare name, but of something encountered in the world, addressed as it is encountered.

So far λόγος has been characterized from three sides: φωνὴ μετὰ φαντασίας; meaningful sound; the bringing-to-a-stand. Speaking is no property like "having hair"; it co-constitutes the specific existence of the human — the human is in the world as the being that speaks *with* the world *about* it (where "about" does not mean judging). The λόγος ἀποφαντικός is that speaking-with-the-world through which the existing world is pointed out as existing (ἀποφαίνεσθαι: to let be seen from itself in its so-being) — but it is only one possibility among others. Aristotle even shifts, in *De anima*, from λόγος to διάλεκτος (speaking with others about something) and ἑρμηνεία (reaching understanding with others), and from this wins a definition of human being: a life standing in the possibility of dealing with the πράγματα, with the world as something to be concerned with, and able to speak. The being is, in its πρᾶξις, essentially characterized as speaking.

Aristotle weights heavily the question of what constitutes the *unity* of the λόγος, for the Greeks the determination of unity (ἕν) alternates with that of a being's specific being. Unity is uncovered in two respects: as to the *meant*, and as to the *factical* meaning. As to the meant: the λόγος differs from ὄνομα and ῥῆμα as πρόσθεσις — what is named is undifferentiated in its being, whereas what the λόγος means is characterized *as a being*. Meaning in the name is bare formal meaning; in the λόγος it is the pointing-out of the being as being. As to the factical meaning: the specific unity of the λόγος ἀποφαντικός is the κατάφασις with which the ἀπόφασις goes together, both as ἀπόφανσις (affirmation and denial). κατάφασις speaks something *of* another *onto* something; ἀπόφασις speaks something *away* — and in ἀπόφασις there is a double ἀπό, for the whole matter must first be held in its being before anything can be spoken away from it. Only so can the problem of negation be reached. These λόγοι are simple because no connection of λόγοι occurs in them, as it does in a hypothetical clause. Even a simple λόγος contains a ῥῆμα that says with respect to something else; the ῥῆμα does not touch the λόγος's unity, since no *naming* is contained in the λόγος. "The leaves are yellow" does not contain "are yellow"; in the λόγος what is spoken of is held as a being. So a λόγος can consist of a single word.

The distinguished λόγος is the ὁρισμός, the definition. Seen only from outside it presents a manifold (man, living thing); but against the ordinary λόγος ἀποφαντικός it is marked out in that what it says of the being is not ascribed to it as *something else* (as yellow is ascribed to the leaves) — what the λόγος says over the being *is the being itself*. The being is here addressed simply at itself, καθ' αὑτὸ λεγόμενον. In *Metaphysics* Z4, where the ὁρισμός is analyzed, Aristotle comes furthest in the analysis of the immediate — a forward position later never regained. Fundamental here is the correlation of λόγος and εἶδος: εἶδος is the look, the thing's "taking itself out" as "it is so"; and Aristotle interchanges εἶδος and λόγος. As we say "so you look" to mean "so you are," the being's look and the speech that addresses it belong together.

##### **c) The possibility of deception, the λόγος ἀποφαντικός, and αἴσθησις**

Where is the possibility of deception, of Schein, grounded — such that one could say there is only appearance in the world? Speaking gives the possibility of having the world in its Da-character, holding open access and preservation; but the λόγος ἀποφαντικός is not λόγος as such, nor even the decisive λόγος, even though in the history of thought's self-interpretation it has taken the leading role. For Aristotle unity has no merely formal sense; the question of unity is bound up with the question of being, being as Dasein. The λόγος ἀποφαντικός is one, ἓν δηλῶν, in that the disclosive function of λέγειν points out a being as a being — one would never reach the λόγος by starting from bare naming; the originary meaning-function is the pointing-out.

Aristotle faults his predecessors for too little heeding that the human spends most of his time in deception; it does not suffice to make deception a side-issue. Beyond αἴσθησις and νόησις one needs an account of φαντασία, of the having-something-there, for it is through the interplay of φαντασία with αἴσθησις and νόησις that deception arises. αἴσθησις is as such a lifting-off of something against another — a distinguishing (κρίνειν): in grasping something out, the grasped is set off against another, and this κρίνειν is not formal but is what makes the set-off thing accessible as there. This κρίνειν is constitutive of νόησις too. The human is the being that has its world *there* in the mode of a distinguishing self-making-accessible, and stands in the possibility of self-movement (κίνησις κατὰ τόπον) in this articulated way.

Under what conditions does αἴσθησις, as κρίνειν, stand? A living being is characterized by being-in-possibility — being able, having definite, marked-out possibilities of ability with respect to the world in which the able being has its being. (Here δύναμις and ἐνέργεια have their origin; already in Plato, but not yet fundamentally; in Aristotle they belong to the categorial determination of being.) Every perceiving goes upon the ὑποκείμενον, the underlying that is already there before all dealings; the perceptible is a ὑποκείμενον, and perceiving makes up the specific access to it. Each sense discriminates the differences of its own perceptible — white and black by sight, sweet and bitter by taste.

Then the decisive step: we also discriminate white from sweet — across senses — and just as immediately, not in bare opining but in *having* these matters there. One must not import here the opposition of thinking and sensibility. How are we able to grasp this difference? By perceiving, for both are αἰσθητά; and it will not do to call in a further sense like touch. The thing perceived by taste and the thing perceived by sight must both be manifest *for one*, must lie open in their what for one. And the same holds when I see "green" and another "red": how is this one matter accessible in its unity? Already in the originary enactment of perceiving, in its lifting-one-off-against-the-other, there lies a speaking (δεῖ δὲ τὸ ἓν λέγειν ὅτι ἕτερον). Speaking is one with the mode of perceiving; it is alive already in this distinguishing. Only on the ground of possible mutual understanding can a unitary matter be made accessible *for several* in its unity: here λόγος is alive as *communicating* λόγος. To communicate in the pregnant sense — to speak a definitely perceived matter out into the openness of Dasein — is to make the addressed accessible to the other so that I *share* it with him; we both now have the same. (Note the middle sense of ἀποφαίνεσθαι: for oneself, so that the matter becomes graspable and retainable for the one perceiving.) And this requires sameness of *time*: the temporal Dasein of the speaking with what is perceived. That I can perceive a perceptible of the world is grounded in my being simultaneous with what is to be perceived (ἐν ἀχωρίστῳ χρόνῳ). Temporality is not arbitrary here: it lies in the very sense of a perceiving and a perceived that the perceived is taken as *now* there. For Aristotle time is not deduced; it is drawn from the matter itself, as is its sameness.

The difference of white and black does not enter perceiving through an argument; it becomes alive *in the speaking*, and αἴσθησις is a κρίνειν. Some found it strange that a λόγος should be met in bare perceiving, and imputed to Aristotle the view that perceiving is already a judgment. But αἴσθησις is not called λόγος outright, only *something like* a λόγος. Aristotle characterizes it four ways. It is an ἀλλοίωσις, a becoming-other: in perceiving, the perceiver himself becomes another, standing now in a definite way toward his world. It is a πάσχειν, an undergoing: something happens *to* the perceiver in perceiving. It is a κρίνειν: in this lifting-off the look is explicitly appropriated, and Aristotle can call this same κρίνειν a certain speaking (λόγος τις), whose function is to point out the perceived as such. And it stands in the middle (μεσότης): sight is not fixed on one color but must see toward both sides, hold to a mean, and so take up all colors equally well. The mean concerns the *how* of possible taking-up. The being-character of perceiving is δύναμις: the ability-to-perceive, a specific being of the living thing from which all the foregoing determinations are to be understood.

Finally the turn to deception. αἴσθησις is in a being that *has language*, and with or without utterance it is always in some way a speaking; language does not merely speak *along* in perceiving — it *leads* it: we see *through* language. And precisely here lies the danger. Insofar as language is taken over traditionally rather than won originarily, it *covers* the very things it has as its ground-function to point out. So it becomes intelligible that in the Dasein of the human, just because he has language, the possibility of deception and Schein is there along with it.

##### **d) The three respects on the ψεῦδος. The factical Dasein of speaking as the proper source of deception. The world's circumstantiality and elusiveness**

*Metaphysics* Δ29 distinguishes ψεῦδος (the false) three ways: as πρᾶγμα ψευδές, a false being touching the proper being of things (we call gold false); as λόγος ψευδής, false speech; and as ἄνθρωπος ψευδής, the false human. The false thus concerns the world, the speaking, and the specific being-in-the-world — and the being itself. Before taking these up Heidegger sharpens the "as." The setting-off stance is already a pointing-out, not a judging: a being is to be shown as a being. Every lifting-off fixes and determines something *as* something, the one against another on the ground of a definite content — white is in itself other than black. In this lifting-off the "as," the being-other-than, is made explicit, though the being-other need not itself be thematic; the theme is the color itself. This is the "critical" as. But the full "as" also requires the ἀποφαντικός-as, the pointing-out as. Language covers this doubling of the as-character: the ἀποφαντικός-as shows itself in every speaking, and to each the critical "as" is joined.

How is the possibility of ψεῦδος grounded in the λόγος itself? ψεῦδος is the pointing-out *giving-out* of something as something — more than bare covering, since it gives a thing out as *other than it is*. A thing can be false in two ways. First, certain contents by their very content do not admit of being brought together — the diagonal called commensurable, or "you are sitting" when you are not. Second, there are beings whose specific being has the peculiarity of giving themselves out as what they are not — shadow-painting (σκιαγραφία), dreams — where the possibility of deception lies not primarily in a wrong apprehension but *in the being itself*.

The hard case is the first: how can one speak of ψεῦδος where the matter called false *is not at all*? How does language come to characterize a non-being as false being? The example is the diagonal presenting itself, through a definite ratio, as measurable. What we meet gives an initial prospect of having its being measurably in this or that respect; when the demonstration shows this impossible, that very false-prospect-character is taken to characterize its being. So the three senses interlock, each seeing the matter in one respect with the others thought along. πρᾶγμα ψευδές points to λόγος ψευδής. For each being there is one single λόγος in which I can address the thing itself in its being — the ὁρισμός, the addressing that presses the thing into its being-limits. Then there are λόγοι by which I address things in many respects, according to the circumstances (the "as-it-happens," αὐτὸ πεπονθός) in which I deal with them. This circumstantiality of the matter lets the addressing λόγοι be many. The false λόγος is, strictly, no λόγος of anything at all: it addresses a thing so that what I address it *as* is drawn not purely from the thing but with a view to something else I already know, and these respects lie *outside* the thing. Such a λόγος is never simple. The simple λόγος is the one by which I determine the thing itself without stepping away from it.

Aristotle uses the occasion to lift off a much-discussed traditional problem: if for each being there is only one wholly specific way of addressing it, there can be no *contradiction*. Insofar as there are only λόγοι that go each upon their own matter, there is no contradiction; contradiction moves in a dimension where it is not the matter itself that is at issue but the *talk about* it. It is possible to address a being not only by its own λόγος but through another's — falsely and utterly, yet sometimes truly, as "eight is double" by the λόγος of the two. The many λόγοι deceive throughout; but one may hit.

The third sense: the ἄνθρωπος ψευδής is the προαιρετικός, the one who *deliberately takes it upon himself* to cover the matter, to whose existence it belongs to bring such talk into others, whose way of dealing fundamentally misleads those he speaks with. How do the three depend on one another? Set the concrete λόγος as that of a human living in a world whose πράγματα can be addressed as ψεῦδος; transpose ψεῦδος into the sphere of factical Dasein — a human who, speaking about the world as it meets him, *can lie*. πρᾶγμα, human, and λόγος are three respects addressing one peculiar ground-phenomenon that Aristotle did not see. And here is the load-bearing thesis of the whole chapter: *the factical Dasein of speaking as such, insofar as it is there and merely insofar as it is there as speaking, is the proper source of deception*. The Dasein of speaking bears in itself the possibility of deception; in the facticity of language lies the lie.

What, then, is the facticity of speaking? It is spoken within a circle of humans in the shared world (Mitwelt); it is taken from the outset as speaking *about* something, as having the tendency to *say* something, and it stands in public regard. Merely through utterance something is made known, and with that the possibility is there of *hiding* something in Dasein — of saying something other than I mean, presenting what I say otherwise than it is. In this ground-possibility of language lies the facticity of lying. Here the remarkable thing shows: the liar must *know* the matter he speaks about — only if I know it am I in a position to lie properly. But the express tendency of a hiding life need not be present. There is a life not bent on covering the objects, that simply *talks along* in public — and this talking-along deceives merely through the fact of the talking itself. The proper opposite of speaking is not false speaking but *silence*. When the express intention to deceive recedes, speech still carries out, more or less consciously, in an estrangement from the matters. The possibility of deception lies here too in the fact of utterance, alive in a *mis-seeing* motivated not by carelessness of observation but by the way beings live and the world itself is encountered. Speech can have the positive tendency to give the matters truly and yet speak within a mis-seeing, the matters not properly appropriated. Through language itself we live in a quite definite apprehension of things — Nietzsche: "Every word is a prejudice."

A second motive for mis-seeing, beside circumstantiality, lies in the being of the world that Heidegger calls *elusiveness* (Entgänglichkeit): things are there in a peculiar character of not-being-there, of slipping away. This belongs to the being of the world itself — that there is fog is no trivial fact to be cited once as an epistemological example. Things can *elude* us — which does not mean they vanish — and their elusiveness becomes alive because they meet us in circumstantiality. We do not see things as matters prepared for scientific inquiry; that everyday Dasein of things is far richer and offers more changeable possibilities than the thematically prepared. Because the world in its richness is there only in the respective concretion of life, elusiveness is that much more encompassing, and with it the possibility of deception: the more concretely I am in the world, the more proper is the Dasein of deception.

How, finally, does one come to call *the things themselves* deception — to say of beings that they are false, rather than resting content to address non-being as non-being? Why the surplus of meaning in "false"? "The diagonal's commensurability is not, this being is false" must not be taken in isolation but from the context it is drawn from. Such sides are ordinarily known as measurable, have the standing and give the semblance of being so — and are not. This their non-being, which raises a definite semblance, is called false; the semblance is disappointed. So even this use of ψεῦδος about beings, insofar as they *are not*, is grounded in the one unitary context. Already in the speaking-out, since speech has the tendency of pointing-out, lies the semblance that there is such a thing within a field of the measurable; but within this speaking it lies that the speech is not a pointing-out *from a being*, but merely *means* what it speaks about — bearing the matters merely as such. This is Husserl's phenomenon of *nominalization and neutralization*: we can speak sentences as merely *meant*. Insofar as such speech directs itself upon the nearest semblance, what it says is false; it deceives in the proper sense. Hence the difference between deception and *dream*: the dream *has* the Dasein on the ground of which it deceives. The apophantic λόγος, taken in its factical Dasein, is something that harbors in itself the possibility of deception, and since λόγος can characterize the Dasein of the human, it rules through all his dealings with the world — all seeing, interpreting, speaking-out.

##### **e) Speaking and the world in their possibilities of deception. The shift in the meaning of φαινόμενον to Schein**

This λόγος that carries the ground-possibility of deceiving makes all perceiving liable to *mis-seeing itself* wherever it is governed by language. That is: the φαινόμενα, as beings in the factical world, are what the human mostly mis-sees — what first shows itself so plainly now becomes something that only *looks* so, that seems. It is no accident that in Aristotle, where φαινόμενα means the existing beings themselves as the ground for research, φαινόμενον can also mean the merely-so-appearing (φαινόμενον ἀγαθόν, the *apparent* good). This shift in the word — once the self-showing being itself, then the merely-apparent, the not-really-being — points, since it moves in the sphere of dealings with the world, to the ground-fact that in Dasein itself error and deception are interwoven in a fundamental way, not just as a bad property to be overcome that happens to turn up in the world.

Speaking as *utterance*, insofar as it is, is the possibility of deception — this speaking taken as being *in a world* that in its specific being-character *offers* possibilities of deception, so that within the whole of the world's Dasein there is an inner nexus that is the *being-possibility* of deception. Speaking, being there in the world, stands in a definite regard not further taken up by any reflection; the human's Dasein is taken in the respect that through speaking something is *said*, and speech carries in itself the claim to communication. The speaker has various possibilities of *hiding himself* in speaking: expressly, in the declared tendency of *lying*; and by wanting to feign, through speaking, that the speech is furnished with some knowledge of the matter — and even where the tendency is opposite to the lie, the possibility of deceiving through words persists. This speaking, with its deception-possibilities, itself stands in a world that offers deception-possibilities in turn: through the world's *circumstantiality*, so that the objects we deal with are there in their concretion according to the given situation, offering a manifold of ways-to-be-discussed; and through the world's *elusiveness*, blocked by fog, dark, and the like — such matters belonging to the being of the world itself. There are further beings that deceive by their specific being, like dreams. And there is the danger that speech is there *with other speech* — becomes a *re-speaking*, a speaking-along, a peculiar dominion language exerts insofar as it *displaces* dealing with the matters. So a manifold interweaving shows: deception-possibility as a being-possibility bound up with the Dasein of speaking and the Dasein of the world. Precisely speaking *hides* the matter. Insofar as access to what is first there is factically led through speaking and opinion about things, the existing world is hidden and encountered in a definite *giving-itself-out*. And insofar as the world is there in this possibility of showing itself, that very possibility reverses into its opposite — giving itself out *as something*. The appearing in the originary sense of self-showing becomes *Schein*. Grasping this possible shift of φαινόμενον into the meaning of the merely-so-appearing is what matters here.

##### **f) Σύνθεσις and διαίρεσις as the region for the possibilities of the true and the false**

It remains only to secure how far Aristotle had an express consciousness that deception has its *field* where things are given in a definite apprehension — that the possibility of deception is not grounded where a plain going-toward things occurs, but where the world is there in the character of the *so-and-so*. περὶ γὰρ σύνθεσιν καὶ διαίρεσίν ἐστι τὸ ψεῦδός τε καὶ τὸ ἀληθές: in the compass of putting-together and taking-apart lie both the true and the false. Perception of the ἴδια is always true and belongs to all animals; διανοεῖσθαι, thinking-through, can also be false, and ψεῦδος is present only where there are λόγοι. So there is a nexus between the necessity that the world be *constituted* as determinate in a manifold of possible respects and the possibility of deception: the speaker always speaks *in some respect upon* something. Pure seeing, like bare naming, is a plain going-toward — I let things stand as given, see the color as a plain taking. But ὅταν δὲ ἡδὺ ᾖ ἢ λυπηρόν — when we perceive something pleasing or distressing, the perceiving is a speaking that *apprehends things as something* (κατάφασις or ἀπόφασις). Meeting something pleasing, the perceiving is sudden, and the perceiving and the seeing-gladness are one; this unitary perceiving is there in the mean (τῇ μεσότητι). Insofar as this perceiving is the most originary — insofar as the things of the world are πρακτά, πράγματα, things to be dealt with — the natural access is a ἥδεσθαι or λυπεῖσθαι, an enjoying or being-pained. These matters go unseen as long as one holds to certain kinds of "experience," to psychology and the like — which is where Chapter One's polemic has stood from the first line: the recovery of what perceiving and speaking *are* has had, throughout, to be won back against the psychological misreading that φαινόμενον, αἴσθησις, and λόγος invite.

### **Chapter Two: Present-day phenomenology in Husserl's self-interpretation**

#### **§ 3\. Recalling the findings won in the Aristotle-interpretation. Anticipation of the predominance of the care for an idea of certainty and evidence over the release of the encounter-possibility of fundamental findings**

The Aristotle-reading yielded two things: findings that point toward the Dasein of the world and of human life (the world's character of showing-itself, and the λόγος-character of life as the possibility of pointing out what shows itself); and, in these, the possibility of *reversal* — the world's Dasein can turn into a giving-itself-out, and λόγος can cover Dasein over. Dasein bears within itself a *Verhängnis*, a doom: with its being, the possibility of deception and the lie is there.

The interpretation was never meant to make the development of a term intelligible anecdotally. It aimed to waken interest in the matters, and deliberately left opaque, at first, where things were heading — what must be learned is *reading and hearing in the manner of waiting*. Then Heidegger states the guiding thread of the entire course, and it is worth marking as the hinge on which everything after turns. The further development of philosophy, and the motivation of its various paths, is governed by *the predominance of an empty and yet fantastical idea of certainty and evidence* — a predominance *prior to* any genuine release of the encounter-possibility of philosophy's own matters. The care for a certain absolute knowledge, taken purely as *idea*, gets the upper hand over every question about the matters that are decisive; the whole development of philosophy *reverses*. The idea of a definite knowledge determines the theme, instead of a definite state of affairs assigning the ways it can be worked on. Heidegger's sharpest phrase: this idea springs not from attempts to win a science's proper access but "from a kind of intelligence gone mad, that invents a Dasein for itself."

This reverses the traditional picture of an "introduction" to a science. One does not first fix "object" and "method"; method must *become* out of the confrontation with the matters. And when we ask what the Aristotle-reading actually delivered, something surprising shows: not a *what*, not the content of an object, but a *how* — the world's how of showing-itself and the how of hiding grounded in it, and correspondingly the λόγος's how of pointing-out and covering. By stressing this *Wie-Charakter* (how-character) of Dasein, the interpretation has already gone beyond what the Greeks could grasp, for the Greek determinations of being are \*Was-\*determinations, content-characters: πράγματα (things one deals with), χρήματα (things in use), ποιούμενα (things produced), φυσικά (things that are of themselves), μαθήματα (the learnable), and, distinguished within all these, οὐσία — a word Heidegger de-abstracts by recovering its ordinary sense: one's holdings, the homestead, what is present around one, das Anwesen. οὐσία gives the ground-character of beings *as* being: presence (Anwesenheit). φαινόμενα names the nearest way beings are encountered as to their formal *how* — self-showing — which must therefore be phenomenologically decisive.

#### **§ 4\. Consciousness as the theme of present-day phenomenology**

The question can now be put: what is the theme of the research called phenomenology today? Heidegger takes Husserl's furthest-driven position in *Ideen I*, where phenomenology is defined as the *descriptive-eidetic science of transcendentally pure consciousness*. For his purposes only the pointer matters: the theme is *consciousness*. Whence its peculiar precedence — how does the field named "consciousness" come to furnish the theme of a science claiming to be fundamental? The task is to show this precedence is not arbitrary but grounded in possibilities Dasein bears within itself and already prefigured in Greek philosophy. If that can be shown, then the historical transformations prove to be motivated *in our own Dasein*, and history is not something cast off behind us and cited for illustration; in its transformations we meet nothing other than our own Dasein. So the inquiry is no historical narrative but concrete insight into definite possibilities. And the striking fact for the Greeks: consciousness was *never a theme*. The word enters Western life through Christian self-awareness and gets explicated in theology, but what it names is nowhere the object of inquiry. That consciousness should become the theme of a science is, for Greek and Christian awareness alike, excluded — which makes the modern precedence of consciousness the thing genuinely to be explained.

The four Greek "directions" of the question of being confirm the point: τὸ ὂν τῶν κατηγοριῶν (being as addressed in the categories, κατηγορεῖν being an intensified λέγειν, saying something to a being's face), τὸ ὂν δυνάμει–ἐνεργείᾳ (being in possibility and actuality, drawn from life as being-a-possibility), τὸ ὂν κατὰ συμβεβηκός (being incidentally, resting on circumstantiality), and τὸ ὂν ὡς ἀληθές (being as true). All four are drawn from the state of affairs of *being-in-the-world*; of consciousness nothing occurs, though there is a treatise Περὶ ψυχῆς. The radical reversal — that what "consciousness" names becomes the theme of *all* philosophy — is thus not self-evident but demands the genealogy the course will supply.

#### **§ 5\. The theme "consciousness" in the *Logical Investigations***

Phenomenology first steps forward under the express title *Logical Investigations*, within the traditional discipline of logic — the theme lay near for Husserl personally, driven as he was from mathematics into logic to understand mathematical thinking. The *Investigations* do not aim at a new logic textbook but at bringing logic's objects to a self-attesting *intuition* (Anschauung) — where "intuition" means only: making the object present *at itself, as it shows itself*, and making that presentation methodically secured. Something traditional and something originary are interwoven. Traditional: logic and epistemology were then to be grounded by underpinning them with a psychology, and Husserl's first work moves partly with, partly against this. Originary: the *way* a foundation is sought — one wanted a state of affairs in which all objects of logic become findable and interrogable, a "milieu" for logic; and the psychology of the day took as its field not the "soul" (ontologically referred to the being of the living thing) but *consciousness*.

Then the concrete route by which working on logic's objects forces one to secure "consciousness." Logic's theme is concepts, judgments, inferences — something *meaning-like* (bedeutungsmäßig), bound up with linguistic expression, since all thinking deposits itself in "assertions." Husserl then held a since-abandoned theory from Brentano (itself from Scholasticism): meaning is, against the many acts that grasp it, an *ideal unity*, a species, the genus for the individual meaning-acts. Seeking such ideal meanings required making present the *lived experiences* (Erlebnisse) in which meanings are there — Husserl's "subjective realization." Within meaning-acts a difference holds: acts of *empty meaning* and acts *fulfilled* by the meant matter itself (Leermeinen and Bedeutungserfüllung). Acts are identical with *intentional lived experience*, and "act" bounds a genus within the whole sphere of Erlebnisse called consciousness. So consciousness means nothing but a *region* of occurrences having the character of lived experiences — the regional sense Husserl holds to this day. This region has a characteristic access: *inner perception* (immanent perception of the immanent); the whole region is what can become conscious to me in immanent perception. And within it, a class of experiences — acts — directs itself *upon something*; after the *Investigations* Husserl narrowed "act" to those intentional experiences marked by the express *ego-cogito*, distinguishing them from non-act intentional experiences like background-experiences. Consciousness is thus the regional title for the whole stock of psychic experiences accessible through inner awareness, within which a distinguished class is consciousness-*of*\-something.

The measuring-rod, as always, is the Greek direction of regard. There the thematic field is beings-as-world and the being of life, and the interpretation *stays within Dasein* — what it interprets is that Dasein itself becoming express. Modern philosophy looks utterly different: its theme is consciousness *in a definite purification*, so that what first comes into view needs reworking to meet the demands of this conception of philosophy. It can look as though Husserl's distinctions are merely verbal — the primitive but ruling picture of what the *Investigations* do. But the region is characterized by its access (inner perception) and by harboring the act-class fundamental to its structure, and it is made the theme *with a view to epistemological-critical clarification*: logic's basic components are to be clarified so as to form a secured foundation for all further construction of knowledge.

#### **§ 6\. The care for known knowledge in which consciousness stands**

The two questions: what *being*, determined by which being-characters, is this region "consciousness," and how does it come to secure itself a precedence such that it furnishes the theme? Consciousness gets marked off from its access — inner perception — and the direction of that regard is *theoretical knowing*, taken as factical *science*, science as a possible achievement-nexus in culture and claimed as the foundation of a culture grounded on science. To characterize love as "consciousness-of-something" is, taken in itself, a monstrosity; it becomes intelligible only from the care that forms this field around theoretical knowing. And the reworking of consciousness *as epistemological-critical clarification* makes the care unmistakable: knowing is set into the *respect of knowing*, and the care goes upon *known knowledge* (erkannte Erkenntnis) because knowledge is to take over the securing of Dasein and culture. This care wants a factual ground from which the groundability of all knowing becomes genuine — and even its own self-expression, "it goes to the matters themselves," runs in a definite tendency: behind the maxim *"zu den Sachen selbst"* the most hidebound dogmatism can conceal itself. Right critique, standing in the matter, shows from *what it defends itself against* what it is really after — so Heidegger will read Husserl's polemics rather than his doctrines.

#### **§ 7\. Husserl's confrontation with contemporary philosophy in "Philosophy as Rigorous Science," and the care for known knowledge alive in it. The general aim of the essay**

The confrontation is available in *Philosophie als strenge Wissenschaft* (1910/11), ten years after the *Investigations*. First Heidegger fixes five *possibilities of caring* (Besorgen) that will serve as a lead-thread: a care discloses what it goes upon and brings it into Dasein; explicates the disclosed concretely; retains what is explicated; *pledges itself* (sich verschreiben) to the retained, making principles from it normative for other cares; and *loses itself* — positing what stands in the care so unconditionally that every care is motivated from it. Explicating a care along these lines opens access to its specific *unrest* (Unruhe). The result is announced in advance: the concrete care that leads to forming, retaining, and positing consciousness as the thematic field is *care for known knowledge*, such that the knowing of knowing becomes attestable from an ultimately secured ground.

The essay does not draft a program but sharpens consciousness *for the idea* of philosophy as rigorous science, on the conviction that even this idea has been lost — so the essay must both bring the idea to light and give it thrust by exhibiting concrete pieces of such research. It proceeds not by thematic presentation but by *critique*, oriented against a *falsification* of the idea of philosophy (naturalism) and a *weakening* of it (historicism). A historical distinction underlies this: from Descartes through Kant and partly Fichte a definite idea of scientific philosophy is alive; Romanticism weakened it — though Hegel, tellingly, shared exactly Husserl's hope of finally making philosophy scientific and, like Husserl, *teachable*. The formal measure of the idea: whether philosophy can lay down an objectively communicable doctrinal content binding for all time; so far as it cannot, it is no rigorous science. (Heidegger notes in passing that Husserl now stands far more positively to Fichte and Hegel and would no longer write his lines against speculative idealism.) The weight, though, falls on the being-character of the consciousness that is philosophy's theme, and on showing the care alive in the *critique* — where what one wards off betrays what one is after.

#### **§ 8\. Husserl's critique of naturalism**

Naturalism arises with the *discovery of nature* as the object of a distinguished science, mathematical natural science; historicism, analogously, from the discovery of history. Naturalism means: the being- and object-kind of the natural nexus becomes the *content-guiding thread* for grasping every kind of being and objectivity, so that the specific rigor of mathematical natural science becomes authoritative for every domain. Why does philosophy fall to this? Because philosophy always aimed at a lawfulness formulable as *norm*, so it is no accident that the science raised to such rigor seizes the task, and philosophy's specific objects fall prey to it. The ground-character of that science, besides its rigor, is that its results are formulable in *laws* that are "generally binding" — a bindingness so imposing in human Dasein that it is the real motive for *absolutizing* the idea of this science.

Naturalism works two ways. First, *naturalization of consciousness* — and Heidegger flags, parenthetically, that this coupling of *idea and consciousness* points back to Descartes, planting Part II's thesis here in advance. Seeing psychic being as nature, one determines it by natural-scientific categories; the organized form of this misunderstanding is experimental psychology insofar as it claims fundamental significance. (Husserl never objected to experimental psychology *as such*.) Ideal laws get reinterpreted into lawfulnesses of pure consciousness-processes, in the domain of willing as much as thinking; the exemplary index of all ideality, Husserl stresses, is the formal-logical laws. Second, *naturalization of the ideas*: the *Investigations* treated meanings as ideal unities of validity, grounding a lawfulness of their own kind — and a philosophy that sees everything as natural science reinterprets this *norm-and-idea lawfulness* into a lawfulness of the natural *course* of the thinking-process. The idea is not seen at all; naturalism is *idea-blind*. Natural-scientific critique is critique *within* natural-scientific knowing, directed at its matters; it is absurd, Husserl says, that the critical resources of a single science should critically investigate that science *qua* science — a wholly fundamental change of object has taken place, and misjudging this level let natural science claim epistemological problems for itself, blocking the way to bringing the specific objectivity "consciousness" into view.

Then the positive characterization by contrast. Nature is the unity of a definite thingly being: each thing is perceivable *as identically the same* through a manifold of direct perceptions, and this identifiable sameness is *intersubjectively* identifiable; each thing stands in causal series, each property a causal-lawful possibility of regulated changes within all-nature, and this thingly unity *presents itself in appearances* (Erscheinungen). This unspoken horizon is what the psychology in question takes its phenomena into — phantasy, perception, representation — taken over unclearly from tradition and never themselves made theme; only regularities *about* them are fixed, carrying the same undifferentiated ground-concepts. The basic defect of this psychology lies in the predominance of the natural-scientific regard, which aims at regularities of process and *leaps over the appearing thing itself*, overlooking that its field is not nature's kind. (Heidegger adds that Husserl's objections carry less weight now that phenomenological work has entered psychology — but only as a taking-over of results and termini, not a purification of the science from the ground up.)

The decisive contrast: consciousness, unlike nature, contains nothing held-identical through a manifold of direct experiences. Every perceivable Erlebnis is, the moment it is to be perceived again, no longer the same — and this non-identifiability holds even *for the same subject*, whereas natural things are intersubjectively identifiable. This is the concrete ground for calling psychic being *stream and flux*: no trivial popular label, but rooted in its very mode of being. Psychic being does not *present itself through* appearances; it *itself, as it appears, is the object* — the object of philosophy is never nature but always *phenomenon*. (Remarkably, in the *Investigations*, where he researches concretely, Husserl almost refuses the word "phenomenon.") This being is a *monadic unity*, constituted by lying in a temporality with a doubly infinite horizon — each being of this domain traceable into an endless past and an endless future. What method fits such being, if the study is to yield universally binding propositions and absolute objectivity? Not natural-scientific questioning, since this is not nature but has something like an *essence*; the only method reaching fixed results is *Wesenserschauung*, the intuiting of essences — the sole method adequate to a study of consciousness that reaches more than natural-scientific regulation, tasked with seeing ideal connections *as* ideal and delivering what it sees into binding propositions.

#### **§ 9\. Clarification of problems as purification and radicalization of their tendency. The care for securing and grounding an absolute scientificity**

What motivates this critique — its choice and treatment of object? Husserl calls his method a *clarification of problems*. But insofar as the clarification is *critique* against naturalization, it *absolutizes* the very idea of a scientific working of consciousness; the critical decision is at once a decision *for the matter at issue*. What is wanted is a strict, objectively binding, attestable lawfulness — and this pull toward purifying the field "consciousness" of every facticity, toward universal bindingness, *is* the already-named care for known knowledge. The load-bearing claim is stated plainly: the care from which the object-choice for the critique grows is the care for known knowledge, the care to secure knowledge by way of the *knowing of knowing*, the securing and grounding of an absolute scientificity. Naturalism is put under critique precisely because *its* problematic and method also aim to set norm-lawfulness on a scientifically secured ground — Husserl and his opponent share the care, and differ on execution.

Hence the peculiar form: "clarification of problems" *takes up* problems and thereby joins a definite decision for what is asked and the direction of asking, in the sense of a *radicalization* of the tendency alive in what is taken up. Husserl means to carry the scientific tendency toward natural science *radically through to its end*. Taking up the critique as a clarification of problems, the critique has *decided for* naturalism's scientific tendency; it proceeds as a *purification* that throws out every moment that could endanger the winning of absolute evidence and certainty — and this purification of the tendency *is its absolutizing*. The task splits fourfold, guiding the sections to come: to see what "clarification of problems" means and how in each step the care for absolute scientificity is alive; how the *arrangement* of problems is taken over positively from naturalism, betraying the leaning toward it; how at the center stands the problem of *knowing physical nature*, so that this nexus furnishes the horizon for the theme "consciousness"; and how certain historical tendencies are drawn on as positive.

#### **§ 10\. Clarification of problems**

"Problem" is best rendered *Vorwurf* — a projected task — not simply "question"; a problem is a question definitely formed and expressly *set as a task* for research. To clarify it, Heidegger turns to the structure of the *question* itself, and the analysis is among the most consequential in the course, since it works out, in nuce, what *Sein und Zeit* §2 will formalize. A question has moments: the *asked-about* (Befragtes), the *asked-for* (Gefragtes), the *respect* in which it is asked (Fragehinsicht), and the *how* of the asking — the manner in which the answer is claimed. In "Does an external world exist?" the asked-about is the external world; the asked-for is not the world but its *being-real*; the respect is reality. Each of these can be more or less expressly appropriated, and here recurs the very possibility of deception met earlier in language: a question can give itself out as a question while the respect ("reality") is so pre-fixed that the question is already a dogmatic opinion.

The decisive stroke is the analysis of the *answer-tendency* into two fundamentally different orientations. In the first, the answer is sought as a *valid proposition* that enriches the stock of valid truths and is built into a system of objective validities — science taken as a system of objective propositions, every result a transitional proposition founding further questioning, the last tendency being an ideally possible connection of all valid propositions. In the second, the answering aims not to multiply an identical stock of propositions but to bring *the being of the questioner* into a fundamental relation to the being asked about — possibly because there is an inner danger of being *driven away* from such a being. And here the being to be reached can show itself, in the questioning, as one that is *questionable in its very being-sense*, demanding to be questioned on the ground of its specific being, so that asking and answering do nothing but bring out the being-determinations of that being. This entails that *the questioning being — Dasein — fundamentally co-determines the being of the being asked about, and vice versa*. In this mode the answer *disappears*, does not come to itself: it is answer precisely when it knows how to disappear in the right way, freeing the path to the being, so that it stays with *questioning* — the answer strikes back into questioning, and in this striking-back into ever-new questioning is constituted what Heidegger calls *Fraglichkeit*, question-worthiness. Nothing yet decides whether this second mode is "science"; one would first have to agree on what alone scientific asking is. The two answer-tendencies are the whole difference between Husserl's care for known knowledge and the questioning Heidegger is preparing.

A problem, then, is a question held explicitly *worthy and needful* of answer, marked by its *task-character* for research; and just because the task is expressly grasped, the pressure is to *go further in answering* — leaving even less time to investigate the asked-about at itself.

#### **§ 11\. Arrangement of the questioning and the guiding thread**

Husserl takes over the *arrangement* of problems positively from naturalism: the so-called human sciences are determined only by their *being-other* than the natural sciences, and the idea of science is prescribed by the idea of mathematics as science — again a purely formal orientation to the moment of *validity*, with even norm-validity seen from the sheerly theoretical ("the exemplary index of all ideality are the formal-logical principles"). Across these moments the care for known knowledge shows itself to be what leads, gives, and preserves the problem.

#### **§ 12\. Characteristic moments of the care for known knowledge in Husserl's critique of naturalism: back-shine, falling, building-ahead, ensnarement, omission**

Here Heidegger draws out the *structure* of this care, in a series of terms that read as an early inventory of what *Sein und Zeit* will call the movement of falling. First, *Rückschein* (back-shine): what stands *in* the care — the objective bindingness to be won — is so alive in the care's own being that everything entering the care's field is determined *from* the thing cared-for. In the light of the care for known knowledge, everything it takes into its task-nexus is seen from that care — Husserl's own words: the idea of science is all-encompassing, thought in ideal completion it is reason itself. The back-shine is a moment of the care *insofar as the care knows nothing of itself*. Second, *Verfallen* (falling): the care has no time for reflection on whether what it deals with is in the end determined by itself; it falls to what it cares for, drawn by it inexpressly. Third, *Vorwegbauen* (building-ahead): the care, though inexplicit in absorbing itself, gives itself a peculiar self-interpretation in the form of a *programmatic systematics* — securing itself meaning and sense through a program it builds out in advance, thereby giving itself a specific *rest* and the security of an objectively binding obligation, and sharpening the back-shine (the whole of history gets violated in the program's sense). Fourth, and more originary, *Verfängnis* (ensnarement): the care, absorbing itself in the cared-for, *is what it is by ensnaring itself in itself* — a ground-phenomenon Heidegger says belongs more intimately to the being-character of Dasein. Through self-ensnarement, whatever comes in the care's way is cared-for, so that what it does *not* care for is not merely absent but cared-for *as something that ought not be there*. And from this, fifth, *Versäumnis* (omission): every care *qua* care omits something — not from outside, but precisely what it claims to care for. The omitted is not forgotten but *expelled*; the care defends itself against what it omits.

#### **§§ 13–14. Husserl's critique of historicism, and human Dasein as what is omitted**

The critique of historicism stands on weaker ground than the critique of naturalism, for Husserl's interest was anchored in the natural-scientific group, and the second lacks comparable working-through — the argument grows cautious and appeals more to feeling. Husserl's line: history can say neither for nor against the *validity* of ideas, so the fact that there has as yet been no philosophy as rigorous science is no proof against the *idea* of one; historicism carried through consistently leads to relativism and thence to skepticism. But in the decisive argument against skepticism the care for known knowledge *ensnares itself*: at the crucial moment it takes refuge in what it flees — it appeals to the very validity-of-propositions whose universal reach was in question.

What is the *omission*? Omitting is *deficient caring*: a being whose way of being does damage (Abbruch tut) to what it is with and related to. In the care for absolute securing of the norm and the forming of a genuine lawfulness, *the investigation of human Dasein itself never becomes a task*. What is to be secured — human Dasein, and with it culture — stands in the circle of the cared-for, yet the very being that is to *stand under* the norm never comes into the theme; even the "concrete I" and the "soul" are to be switched off. And this omission is itself something the care *cares for*: the care sees to it that the omitted remains omitted and does not come back across its path, blocking its own way to what it omits so as not to be disturbed in its omission. The named omission is thus not forgetfulness but an active warding-off — and what is warded off is precisely the being of the one for whose sake the whole securing is undertaken.

#### **§ 15\. Sharper specification of the care for known knowledge**

The care for known knowledge goes upon *justified*, last-valid knowledge, taken in advance as *scientific* knowledge, and scientificity is determined as "binding for all rational beings" — so the care is care for an *evident all-bindingness* (evidente Allverbindlichkeit). The tell is that *what* is to be known is, in advance, secondary: primarily the care aims to create *some* absolutely binding kind of knowledge at all. Hence science is grasped as the unity of a problematic and a method; the answer-tendency toward propositional validity in the sense of apodicticity *decides before* any interest in the matter asked about — the matter first gets its being through the forming and clarifying of the question. Philosophy becomes fundamental science in the sense that it is to make possible the setting-up of last-valid propositions; the matters themselves are met primarily *as problems*, as objective connections prescribed in definite problem-directions.

So *"zu den Sachen selbst"* — to the matters themselves — can here no longer mean making the matters present freely *from themselves*, prior to a definite way of asking; it means letting the asked-about be encountered *within this already-prescribed problematic*. The maxim keeps a certain originality against the constructions of contemporary philosophy, but in this philosophy's ownmost sense the call springs from a care that is *inadequate to the matter*: it is the demand to lose oneself decisively to the care for all-bindingness. The far more fundamental possibility lies outside its field of view — to free beings so that only the corresponding *worthiness* of a being to be questioned decides what is primarily philosophy's object, a decision that would have nothing to do with a mathematically borrowed idea of science and might first enact the proper sense of knowing. And *rigor* (Strenge) itself: since it is not the speakability of freed matters that primarily decides but bindingness and its attestation, the *seriousness* concentrates on shaping that bindingness — a *derived* seriousness, lacking the originality that would risk everything on letting what is known be the determining thing. Throughout our history the mathematical idea of rigor has been posited *uncritically as absolute norm*; but rigor is no empty idea, it is something concrete, formed out of a science's own discovery of beings, and cannot be ascribed from outside.

#### **§ 16\. The disclosing of the thematic field "consciousness" by the care for known knowledge. Regress to the historical concretion of the care**

So far it is clear only that the care *forms* the cared-for by securing a problematic and method, *pledges itself* to it, and *loses itself* in the field "consciousness" — losing itself most of all where the fundamental science of consciousness is characterized not merely as a possibility of culture's Dasein but as the ἡγεμονικόν, the ruling part, of human Dasein as such. But the decisive question — *how the care comes to this cared-for*, how it *discloses* the field — is unanswered. The answer runs through a moment not yet stressed: every care is *seeing*. A *Sichtigkeit*, a sightedness, belongs to being-within-a-world; it has nothing to do with theoretical knowledge but is an enactment-mode of the *ground-constitution* of Dasein, to be exhibited as *Entdecktheit*, disclosedness. (Heidegger cross-refers forward to the course's end and to Part II — these phenomena lie in a far more fundamental originality than the theories handed down.) Every care has its *Hinblick*, a directed regard alive in the enactment as *Umsicht*, circumspection, led by what Heidegger calls the care's *Absicht*, its intent. Dasein as being-in-a-world (In-Sein) is *disclosing* being: "in a world" names not two objects related but that the specific being of the living thing is grounded in *having* the world in the mode of caring for it.

The bridge to Part II: every care is in its being *factical* care — facticity belongs to the what-being of the care itself, which is to say the care *is a way of Dasein*. So the question of how the care for all-bindingness (which is not, at first, referred to any determinate field) comes to disclose and supply *the* field "consciousness" must be answered by regress to the care's *historical concretion*. And that concretion is *Descartes' research*: it is in Descartes that the care for certainty factically-historically discloses the field "consciousness." With this the course leaves Husserl and turns back, by way of the *Destruktion*, to the source.

## **Second Part: Regress to Descartes and the scholastic ontology that determines him**

### **Chapter One: Coming to terms with the regress to Descartes by way of a recollection of what has been carried out so far**

#### **§ 17\. The hermeneutic situation of the past and forthcoming investigations**

The regress to Descartes must first be made transparent in its own character, because Husserl's regress to Descartes was riddled with definite failures. Heidegger does this through a *Rückerinnerung*, a recollection — not a retelling but a becoming-clearer in re-traversing the path, which sets what has been traversed in a sharpened horizon. And he uses the occasion to lay out the *hermeneutic situation* of the whole course, in the triad that *Sein und Zeit* will make canonical. Every interpretation is governed by: the *Vorhabe* (fore-having) — what is had in advance, on which the regard rests; the *Vorsicht* (fore-sight) — the way what is fore-had is seen; and the *Vorgriff* (fore-grasp) — how what is seen is, from a definite motivation, conceptually explicated. ἑρμηνεύειν means to interpret in a way that keeps itself transparent about itself.

The fore-having of *this* investigation is *Dasein*; the fore-sight is Dasein *as to its being*, its possibilities and ways of being; the fore-grasp explicates its being-characters — Dasein seen toward a proper possibility of its being, *Existenz*. Hence the decisive terminological stroke: the being-characters of Dasein, taken as being, are *Existenzialien*, and they have *nothing to do* with the ordinary categories, which always concern content-laden worldly domains. The Existenzialien exclude any systematics; their connection is grounded wholly otherwise than a system of categories. And the recollection shows the unity of the whole course: both investigations — clarifying the term "phenomenology," and characterizing what the term names today — proceed as *interpretation of the pre-given upon Dasein*. φαινόμενον and λόγος were interpreted upon Dasein; and the field "consciousness" was interpreted upon a definite *care*, its formation interpreted as a way of Dasein. So the last result of the Husserl-interpretation is that *the care for known knowledge is the seized being-possibility of what the Aristotle-interpretation indicated* — and, in its moments (back-shine, building-ahead, ensnarement, omission), the care for known knowledge is *flight before Dasein as such*. That consciousness becomes the thematic field is thus no harmless event: Dasein flees itself and gives up the possibility of radically sighting and grasping itself.

#### **§ 18\. Becoming-free from the discipline and the handed-down possibilities as becoming-free for Dasein. Ontological investigation of Dasein as Destruktion**

Neither investigation was oriented to a *pre-given discipline*; both led *out* of the discipline to a peculiar phenomenal nexus, Dasein — a *becoming-free from the discipline for Dasein itself*, seizing the possibility of making Dasein the theme of a research determined by Dasein itself. This freeing is at once a freeing *from handed-down possibilities* of determining this being. All prior research bearing on Dasein — under the titles stream-of-experience, reason, life, I, person — shows a ground-omission: never first interrogating the being it treats *as to its proper constitution*. Even "philosophy of life" (Lebensphilosophie), which tends toward living Dasein, shows how little it grasped its own task; and Dilthey, who developed the greatest possibilities for grasping life, worked *reactively* and so built up his own way to life — for all his fight against naturalistic psychology, he still labored at *psychology*, moving in the very way of seeing he warded off. The greater difficulty than sureness of instinct is the *transparency* of instinct. And the interpretation upon Dasein faces a peculiar, unremovable difficulty: every attempt to experience Dasein originarily is undertaken *from today's situation of interpretation* — what is presented as "past" is no past but a *bad present*, and the task is first to *disclose* the past at all. Every historical orientation is not a contingency but a task prescribed from Dasein itself.

#### **§ 19\. Regress to the proper being of the care for known knowledge in its originary past as regress to Descartes**

How does interpreting the care for known knowledge lead to Descartes? In Husserl the field "consciousness" is set up by going back to concrete Dasein as a *mere exemplification of a universal*: consciousness names the circle of phenomena studied for structures belonging to any consciousness *qua* consciousness, and being-human is only a *case* of this general possibility — a connection governed by the logical distinction of genus and specimen. Thereby the proper, *historical* connection of Dasein with its possibilities is widened into a *formal-logical* order that being does not interest at all. The determinations of the being of this consciousness will prove to lead back into old Greek ontology and logic; what looks new in Descartes is new only externally, and shows on inspection *no break* but the seizing of a possibility already prefigured — the care for known knowledge is a being-possibility that Greek philosophy determined ontologically in the *absolute precedence of θεωρεῖν* among all being-possibilities of Dasein. The care for known knowledge rules today in an *uprooted* way, no longer knowing its own descent; and its proper being cannot be seen in its averageness — one must go back to its proper being *in its originary past*, a research-nexus bearing the name "Descartes."

#### **§ 20\. Destruktion as the way of interpreting Dasein. Three tasks; the question of the sense of the truth of knowledge in Descartes**

The regress looks like bending a factual investigation into a historical one — but the being of the care *is a being-historical*: it can be uprooted, as today, and yet still be historical, its history *underground* rather than lost. So Dasein shows itself as something *covered over by its own history*, the history of its way of interpreting itself; the Greek-formed nexus of category-research and logic still rules the direction of regard on what one calls Dasein. Bringing Dasein into view therefore requires freeing it from these conceptual overgrowths that Dasein itself built up for the purpose of its own explication — in which a peculiar tendency shows itself: *Sich-selbst-verbauen*, Dasein's building-itself-shut. The freeing by way of dismantling, *Destruktion*, leads the concepts back to their proper origin and thereby exposes their inadequacy to Dasein.

Four points fix the method, and they are worth stating precisely because *Sein und Zeit* §6 will compress them. First, Destruktion is *no universal historical method* but a concrete way arising from the needs of categorial Dasein-research and limited to it; it *seems* purely negative, but it seeks in what it takes up not the weaknesses but *the positive, the productive* — it makes visible, on the strong possibilities, where they reach their limits. (Theological and philosophical work both fall in its scope; Christian theology has always lived hand-to-mouth, borrowing its conceptuality from whatever philosophy was at hand — even Luther's originary beginning was buried within a decade by Melanchthon and the retaken Aristotelian tradition.) Second, Destruktion is *critical*, but what it criticizes is not the past it discloses; the critique falls on *the present*, our Dasein insofar as it is covered by a past gone inauthentic — not Aristotle or Augustine but the *today* is criticized, and the past is thereby made visible as our proper *having-been* and *being-able-to-be-again*. Third, Destruktion is *historical knowledge in the proper sense* and needs no annex of systematic questioning. Fourth, it can never be *refuting*, protecting one's own position by refutation; it has sense only as matter-disclosing research into Dasein's being-characters. Its objectivity brings the past to its proper being, its *aptitude for striking back upon the present* — and only when the past is brought into this striking-back-possibility does its objectivity, and the present's bond with history, arise. Philosophical research thus cannot be resolved into a "systematic" and a "historical" consideration; it lies *before* that distinction, more originary than the basis on which it is drawn.

Applied to Descartes, three tasks follow: to show that this care is in fact alive in Descartes' research (externally easy — his work stands under the criterion of the *clara et distincta perceptio*); that this care *discloses consciousness*, and how; and that by the definite being of this care, definite being-characters of *what can be disclosed at all* are already prescribed. The care of knowing is oriented to *truth*, and in that orientation a definite *idea of truth* becomes visible — which Heidegger will orient upon Dasein itself, asking in what sense truth belongs to Dasein at all (the Augustinian question of *veritas* and *vita*). The idea of truth that meets us with the care for known knowledge will prove, properly grasped, to be *not a character of knowing* but a *ground-constitution of life itself*, torn from its possibility by a way of being of the care; the Greek term ἀλήθεια already shows the beginnings of truth's embedding in Dasein. Provisionally, though, Dasein does *not* appear in the traditional handling: truth is seen as a character of *judgments*, of theoretical knowing, so that truth means as much as *validity* — the whole complex being what is called, in the widest sense, the epistemological problematic.

### **Chapter Two: Descartes. The how and the what of the disclosing-being of the care for known knowledge**

#### **§ 21\. Determinacies of "truth"**

Before Descartes, Heidegger lays out how, from positing an *idea* of truth, definite possibilities of the *being of truth in life* arise — a four-stage layering that is a genealogy of truth's decay into validity. Truth is taken, first, as *the true*: the being that is disclosedly *there* in a definite kind of knowing, the being in its proper disclosed self-Dasein — scholastically, *verum id quod enuntiando ostenditur*, and already in the *enuntiando* a further possibility is prefigured. Second, the experiencing of a known being goes together with its being addressed and spoken about; the disclosed being is the *what-about* of a speaking, and the spoken-out disclosed being is the *Satz*, the proposition — which holds the being in itself *as disclosed insofar as it is spoken*, and is as such *communicable*, able to be accepted, contradicted, rejected. Third, such a truth can be there *free-floating*, no longer regarded as speaking *about a definite being*: the proposition can be there in the mode of *being-repeated-after* (Nachgesprochenwerden), with the claim to join in the truth it expresses — and so truth has, in publicness, the being-mode of a *Gültigkeit*, a validity whose attestation-mode can vary and can even be *missing*. Fourth, this validity, passed around in various modes of being-spoken, has in itself, according to its origin, the possibility of a peculiar *attestability*: where a validity is accepted such that the attestability is itself valid, the proposition is *correct*, a *Richtigkeit* — and this moment of correctness can be covered and missing, yet the proposition can still raise the *claim to bindingness* on the ground of its origin. The layering shows how what began as *the true* — the disclosed being itself — slides, stage by stage, into a free-floating validity that no longer looks back to any being at all, which is exactly the condition of the "truth \= validity" equation the care for known knowledge takes for granted.

A fifth stage completes the decay: taking a proposition's *bindingness* as what *ought* to be acknowledged, the proposition-as-valid becomes an *ought* (Gesolltes), and construing the ought as *value* yields *truth as value*. Starting from that last stage, *nothing* of truth's originary sense remains: value-philosophy so far removes itself from truth that the way to exhibiting truth's proper sense is finally blocked. This path of removal is not contrived for Destruktion's sake — it is the path *history actually made*, the movement in which the interpretation of the being-as-true runs, and the tendency to it lies already in Greek philosophy, which never took the originary sense of being-true seriously and oriented the true in advance as a determinacy of the *proposition*, reinforced by the special weight theoretical knowing carried from the start. Only once more does a new attempt arise to give truth a new sense — in the New Testament and, following it, Augustine; but the Gospels do not explicate it, and it would have been theology's task to explicate such an originary sense of truth in explicating faith. Theology omitted this. (Even Kierkegaard's "paradox" is nothing but the result of this fundamental omission in the interpretation of truth.)

#### **§ 22\. Three possibilities of the care of knowing: curiosity, security, bindingness**

The care for known knowledge is only *one* determinate possibility, to be set among others. The care of knowing has three: *curiosity* (Neugier), *security* (Sicherheit), and *bindingness* (Verbindlichkeit). Curiosity does not let itself be satisfied with having known a section of beings; it wants only the possibility of pressing ever further, no longer caring *what* it knows, still less the *being* of what it knows — it wants only that ever-new possibilities of knowing be there. This is *Sich-Versorgen* in a double sense: the care goes wholly up in itself, so that only *being-able-to-see* matters, and thereby grows unreceptive to the proper appropriation of beings (the interestedness in everything, even religion, that rules today is a weak offspring of this); and it *provides for itself* such that it needs nothing else, is *needless* (unbedürftig), and so fails whenever the claim is raised to *make serious* with what it has known. This "curiosity/needlessness" is an unmistakable early sketch of the *Neugier* of *Sein und Zeit*.

Security is care that knowing be so enacted that knowing and the known are *known in their being-known*; knowing is brought into a definite being-known — *care for known knowledge*. Because it wants the knowing itself, wants it given with insight, and cares primarily and solely about *insightfulness*, it develops a special interest in *methodological* considerations, whose peculiar precedence characterizes the present way of knowing. Here too it ensnares itself: caring first for the *security* of possible knowledge, it takes into the bargain a *mis-seeing* of the being to be known, and a specific *blindness* to what is really to be known grows up — method taken as the way to the greatest possible evidence, where method properly means only that the way *to the matter itself* is opened and secured.

Bindingness usually goes together with one of the first two, aiming not merely at more knowing or securer knowing but at a *dominion* of knowing as such, so that knowing sets itself through as what precedes and determines all possibilities of Dasein. It *over-heightens* knowing — when it speaks of itself it says "the propositions of knowledge bear the stamp of eternity" — a presumption in which knowing systematically covers over its own being-possibilities, exemplified in the way this care confronts historicism. With these possibilities goes the respectively ruling concept of truth; and taken together as historically-having-been and still-ruling, they make evident that the present is, in the widest sense, *unprepared* for the question: what does truth of life and of Dasein mean? Both pieces — truth and Dasein — are wholly unclarified, yet one supposes one has the means in hand to settle it. Descartes is a decisive turning-point *only* in the way the today, under the dominion of theoretical knowing, interprets itself and its history — for Descartes is really thoroughly *medieval*. (Heidegger contrasts his own account of the true with Husserl's in the *Investigations*, where truth is oriented as *propositional* truth and determined as the *fulfillment of an intention*, truth consisting in the fulfillment of the initially empty meaning.) The concluding question sets the direction: what *way of being* in Dasein itself is the being-caring in the sense of knowing, such that these determinate unfolding-possibilities are there ontologically in it?

### **Chapter Three: Descartes' determination of the *falsum* and *verum***

#### **§ 23\. Preview of the nexus of questions**

The *falsum* is determined first as *error*, error as *malum*, and this, on the traditional view, as *non esse qua privatio* — a definite mode of *non-being*. Light must fall from this onto the *verum* too. The *falsum* is the *cavendum*, what the care of knowing has to avoid; and in that concept a definite view of the *verum* is already alive. It has been denied that Descartes determines the *verum* in the same orientation — the usual reading assigns the *falsum* to the *voluntas* and the *verum* to *perceptio* as the theoretical. Heidegger's counter-thesis: the characterization of the *verum* stands in the *same enactment* as that of the *falsum*, and it is precisely this peculiar interpretation of the two that *makes it necessary* to assign the *falsum* to the will and the *veritas* to the ideas or their correlative grasping. And because being-true also decides about *being*, the whole rests on an ontology — Descartes' *Meditationes de prima philosophia* stands, as its title says, knowingly within *ontologia generalis*, no modern "first philosophy" but the consciousness of connection with the old ontology's questioning. The origin-sources to be touched: Thomas, Anselm, Augustine, with a glance to Aristotle.

#### **§ 24\. The *cogito sum*, the *clara et distincta perceptio*, and the task of an ontological securing of the truth-criterion**

Descartes has found something true, and this something appearing with the insightfulness of being-true is the *cogito sum* — better, *the res cogitans qua ens*. *Cogitatio* equals *intentio*: everything that happens in us of which we are conscious, so far as there is consciousness of it in us — nearly the phenomenological concept of intentional lived experience. With grasping the being of the *res cogitans* the *criterion* for the evidence of that grasping is co-grasped, because *cogito* means *cogito me cogitare*: I am certain I am a thinking thing, and asking what is required to be certain of anything, I find nothing but a *clara et distincta perceptio* of what I affirm — whence the *regula generalis*, that all is true which I perceive very clearly and distinctly. Descartes means by *cogitatio* not experiences merely directed at something but experiences that have in themselves a *consciousness of themselves*.

The decisive point, and the one that overturns the standard reading of Descartes: the criterion *as such does not suffice for him*. In speaking the proposition *cogito sum* I experience that my assent is *drawn* to assent — but I cannot found knowing on an obscure connection of being-drawn; I must have the *clara et distincta perceptio* itself *ontologically secured*, the *regula generalis* justified as a possible absolute criterion. Modern philosophy branded Descartes' proof a relapse into old metaphysics, but one must ask whether Descartes did not see *more radically* in seeking a further grounding of the criterion — and Heidegger notes that Husserl concentrated his last years' work on exactly this, justifying the idea of absolute evidence *out of itself*. This task drives Descartes to the *falsum*: I have the criterion only *obscurely*; probing into myself I find I am a *res finita, imperfecta*, so I cannot have the ground of my existence in myself, and am led to a *ground* of my Dasein — the idea of God as the infinite, an idea whose *realitas objectiva* I as imperfect cannot have caused. If God is and the positive in me stems from him, God-created and so *true*, then everything in me *qua* being is a *verum*, and the *clara et distincta perceptio* that co-constitutes my proper created being is true; God, giving me my Dasein, cannot be the cause of my deceiving myself. Whence, then, error? Only once error is shown *not* to stem from God, according to his being, is the criterion of all knowing ontologically grounded, and with it the possibility of the *falsum as malum in the ens creatum* laid open. Read in the light of the Destruktion: we seek what *positing of Dasein* lies in what one today calls consciousness.

#### **§§ 25–26. The classification of the *cogitationes*; *realitas objectiva* and *realitas formalis***

The *judicium* is the place of the *verum* and *falsum*: to judge is to assent, to say Yes — and here, Heidegger notes, is the root of Brentano's classification (idea/judicium/affectus) that so influenced phenomenology, with *voluntas* entering the judgment. So long as I regard *ideae* merely in themselves — the *fundamentum* for the judgment — I cannot fall into error; error arises only where something can *turn over*, where *verum* as *esse* (a *cogitatio* present as *res*) admits its counter, *non esse*. On the *realitas objectiva* / *realitas formalis* distinction Descartes builds, with old ontological axioms, an evident assertion of the *Dasein* (Vorhandensein, presence-at-hand) of the represented: every *realitas objectiva* demands *of itself* a *causa* whose reality answers to the being given in the *realitas objectiva*; every cause *qua* cause is real, being-as-cause is *realitas formalis*. The idea of God, as *realitas objectiva*, I as *imperfectum* cannot have caused, so I must be caused by *esse perfectum* — Deus est entitas essendi sui.

What matters for the Destruktion is the *levelling* this performs. Descartes sees in the being of the *res cogitans* a *double* being — *esse repraesentatum* and *esse repraesentans*, the *esse* of the *cogitare* and the *esse* of the *cogitatum* — and *nivellates* them into one, one *esse animi*, because *cogitare qua cogitare* and *cogitatum qua cogitatum* are given equally evidently, both attestable as something (*aliquid*) present-at-hand, and in view of the *esse verum* finally identical: both are, i.e. created, God-created, i.e. *verum*. The criterion of the *clara et distincta perceptio* thus takes on the *function of levelling being-characters* — a telling enactment-mode of the care for known knowledge, which meets us just where two different ways of being *within consciousness* are levelled so that both fall, unchecked, under the scholastic causal consideration. Descartes transfers the causal axioms of *esse naturale*, formalizing, onto the being of the represented-as-such — which he can do only because "objectivity" itself belongs to the *ens formale* of the idea: the idea *is* as a letting-see, and with its being goes the being-sighted of something, the presence of a *whence* "in" the *perceptio*.

#### **§§ 27–28. The being of the *falsum* and *error*: the *concursus* of *intellectus* and *voluntas*; *error* as *privatio*, and *creatum esse***

Error is traced to *two causes concurring*: the *facultas cognoscendi* (intellectus) and the *facultas eligendi sive arbitrii libertas* (voluntas) — *ab intellectu, et simul a voluntate*. Neither faculty *qua* faculty can be the sufficient ground of error, for the faculty-character is a way of being that is positive, and as *esse positivum* it is *esse creatum*, hence a *bonum*; *qua* faculty, the being of the *falsum* cannot be a *malum*. The intellect grasps only *ideas* about which judgment can be passed, and no error proper is found in it precisely so regarded. That my intellect is *limited* — grasping neither all objects nor all it grasps with *clara et distincta perceptio* — is no *privatio* but a mere *carentia* relative to the ideal of God's constitution; since I cannot claim that infinity, my finitude is no defect. And the intellect is, for Descartes, where the *perfectio* of the human lies — though he says the perfection lies in the *voluntas*, his explication of the will (following Thomas) runs *toward* the intellect as *facultas eligendi*.

The will is that faculty circumscribed *by no limits*: *sola est voluntas sive arbitrii libertas quam tantam in me experior ut nullius majoris ideam apprehendam*. I can imagine a greater power of imagination but not a greater will, which is in a certain way *absolute* — and precisely in respect of the will I experience my *likeness to God*, the *imago et similitudo Dei* of Genesis. God's will surpasses mine in the *ratio cognitionis* (transparency), the *potestas* (executability), and the *ratio objecti* (range of willed objects); but *in itself, formally and precisely regarded*, the will is *not greater* than mine, consisting only in this, that we can do or not do — affirm or deny, pursue or flee — what the intellect proposes, so borne that we feel constrained by *no external force*. Error, then, is the *concursus* of a finite intellect (which does not grasp everything clearly and distinctly) with an *infinite* will (which extends beyond what the intellect clearly presents): error occurs when the will *affirms* beyond the reach of clear-and-distinct perception. Its being-sense is *privatio* — a *doing-damage* (Abbruchtun) to the proper being of the created human, whose ground-determinations of the *esse* of the *res cogitans* are *perceptum esse* and *creatum esse*. What Heidegger extracts is that the whole analysis of consciousness — its double being levelled, its truth secured through a divine guarantor, its error located in a will made in God's image — is carried through in a *scholastic-theological ontology of createdness*: the "modern" cogito is determined throughout by *creatum esse*, and this is what the standard reading of Descartes as epistemological pioneer conceals.

### **Chapter Four: Regress to the scholastic ontology. The *verum esse* in Thomas Aquinas**

#### **§§ 29–30. The connection of *verum* and *ens*: being-true as a mode of being; the proper being of the *verum* as *convenientia* in the *intellectus***

Behind Descartes stands the scholastic ontology of the true, and Heidegger reads Aquinas' *De veritate* q.1 to find where *verum esse* "springs out." A being can be regarded two ways: in itself, and *secundum convenientiam unius entis ad aliud* — according to the fittingness of one being to another; and it is at *this* second point, in the formal dimension of *esse in ordine ad aliud*, that the being-possibility of the *verum* first appears. The *verum* is not a free-standing thing but a *relational* mode of being. *Convenientia* is the being-referred, the being-mutually-assigned of being to being — and Heidegger flags its *doubleness* as what makes the whole approach possible. In one direction it has its determination *toward the intellectus*: as a character of the *verum* bearing on knowing, it requires the *assimilatio intellectus ad rem* (to the intellect's nature belongs the *proportio*, the being-borne toward the matter). In the other, *ut res intellectui correspondeat* — the thing corresponds to the intellect. Both directions of this mutual being-directed are summed as *conformitas*, which the Jewish-Arabic mediation renders *adaequatio* — whence the formula *adaequatio rei et intellectus*, the correspondence-definition of truth that the entire tradition, Descartes included, inherits.

The *verum* as *convenientia* has three relational moments: the *ratio formalis* (the *convenire* itself), the *fundamentum* (the *res*, whence a *convenire* is possible at all), and the *effectus* (the *cognitio* as effect of truth, the being-true of a judging). Which of the three is the *proper* being of the *verum* — where is truth *at home*? Aquinas argues, and the answer is the pivot: the *res* is *not* what is primarily true; what bears the *ratio communis* completely need not be the *cause* of the others but that in which the sense of *verum as convenientia completa* is fulfilled (as "healthy" is said primarily of the living thing, only derivatively of the medicine). The connection of *res* and *intellectus* is actual in the *cognoscere*, a *motus cognitivae virtutis* that goes from intellect to known via the thing and *terminates in the known*; so the *convenire* comes to its being *in the anima itself, in the intellectus* — *verum est in intellectu*. Modern ears hear truth relocated into the "subject"; but the proper being of the *verum* is in the intellect *of God*, the *ens perfectissimum*, and the whole connection is seen purely *objectively and ontologically*. The being-true is thus a *mode of being*, not a property of a mental act — but a mode grounded in a divine intellect.

#### **§§ 31–33. In what sense the *verum* is in the *intellectus*; its grounding in the originary truth of God; the determinability of God's being**

A *privatio* (blindness) is no *entitas*, yet a *vera caecitas* can be truly asserted — so truth in created things comprehends only the *entitas rei*, the *adaequatio* of thing to intellect, and the *adaequatio* of intellect to things or to *privations* of things. Where truth concerns a *privatio*, its *fundamentum* is not the privation (which has no positive content to motivate grasping) but lies in the being of the *intellectus*: the intellect, as grasping what is, is itself true, directed to its proper *bonum*, and *the bonum of the intellect is the verum*. The knot loosens, Heidegger says, the moment one *drops every modern way of seeing entirely*: intellect and thing are set into the *one region of the ens creatum*, and knowing is taken as an *objective relation of being* within that createdness. This positive orientation gives the interpretation of the truth-"relation" a security no other interpretation of knowing reaches — at the cost that *being-knowing loses its originary sense*, modified into the neutral being-sense of a *movement from one being to another*, a levelling onto the *ens creatum*.

And the whole region of *ens creatum* is itself determined by reference to an *ens increatum*, the *esse Dei*. Here Heidegger turns the theology inside out: God's Dasein is *not* the source from which the being of the created is determined; rather, *from a definite pre-conception of the being of the created, God's being is itself determined*. The seemingly originary being is *derived from the esse creatum* by a definite method — the ground of what is later called negative theology, a *remotio* of the being-characters incompatible with an *ens absolutum*. Even that remotio needs a positive idea of an *ens simplex* as its criterion; God is taken in advance as *actus purus*, *essentia Dei* without *compositio ex materia et forma* — which shows that the accessible being of the world is taken in the sense of *producing, forming*. The five ways of the *Summa* (q.2 a.3) each regard the world's being in a definite respect — the *primum movens immobile* (Aristotle's πρῶτον κινοῦν ἀκίνητον), the *causa efficiens prima*, and so on — and derive God's being by remotio, wholly formally, with *no religious relation* of the human's being to God's in play. Heidegger's extracted result: in determining *verum esse* as a *modus essendi*, the *esse* is grasped as *esse creatum*, and the *modus essendi* of the *verum* is prescribed by the created being's *being the object of the intellectus divinus*; since every being *qua* being measures itself against that intellect, every *esse* is a *verum*. The correspondence-truth Descartes will presuppose, and that Husserl will inherit as "truth \= validity/fulfillment," rests on this formalized ontology of createdness — and this is the ground the *Destruktion* was after.

### **Chapter Five: The care of knowledge in Descartes**

#### **§§ 34–36. The being of knowing as judging within the horizon of *creatum esse*; the taking-of-a-rule (*clara et distincta perceptio* as general rule); the origin of clarity and distinctness**

The being of knowing is a *cogitatio*, and the *cogitationes* fall within the *res cogitans*, which is *esse perceptum* and, as *creatum esse*, makes up the being of the human. Knowing is *judicare*, and judging carries with it a *Regelnahme*, a taking-of-a-rule — and it is here, in knowing as judging under a self-imposed rule, that the *care-character* of knowing first comes out clearly. Into this belongs the ground-phenomenon Heidegger names *Beruhigung*, tranquilizing: knowing, as care, seeks to set itself at rest by securing a rule that guarantees its results. The rule is the *clara et distincta perceptio*; and Descartes derives clarity and distinctness not from interrogating the various *domains of being* but from the pre-given *mathematical* disciplines — the "simple natures" (the *pure intellectuales* known by an inborn light, the *pure materiales* like figure and extension, the *communes* like existence and duration). (Heidegger notes that Scheler simply took over this position, extending essence-grasping from consciousness to the universal being-nexus — the old ontology reaching the present through many channels.)

#### **§ 37\. The care of knowing as care of certainty, as *Sichvergreifen***

The rule's content shows its origin twice over: in *what* it regulates — *scientia*, read off from the pre-given mathematical disciplines — and in *how* it regulates — by the method read off from mathematics. And these sciences are taken in advance not on the *content* of their objects but on the objects' *mode of being-encountered for assenting knowing*. So the care that gives itself this rule reveals itself as the *care of certainty*: the care of knowing does not tarry with a being *qua* being, does not regard objects as to their content, but goes upon them *in the how of their graspability*, and that graspability seen upon *assent*. Its tarrying is peculiar — and the decisive formula falls here: *esse as verum and verum as certum are primarily set into the care, so that the ens reaches its task only by the detour through the certum*. Truth grasped primarily as *certainty*: this is the substitution the whole *Destruktion* was driving at, and it is Descartes who performs it.

This care does not limit its rule to its origin-domain but gives it out as *regula generalis*: only knowledge satisfying the rule is science at all — and *the originary ground is given up in the very forming*. Descartes accordingly excludes possibilities of knowledge from science (historical knowledge can have only propaedeutic value) or reshapes disciplines to fit — theology, metaphysics, mathematics, and, in physics, admitting of objects only their *extensio*. The fate of the doctrine of *innate ideas* is the clearest case: Descartes takes it up from the Oratorian movement and builds it into his doctrine of the *clara et distincta perceptio*, because the older Thomistic account of knowing — the *species intentionales* mediating sensible and intellectual knowledge, resting on the Aristotelian οὐδέποτε νοεῖ ἄνευ φαντάσματος ἡ ψυχή (the soul never thinks without a phantasm) — could not be accepted: guided by the rule, he must relocate the interpretation of knowing into the field the rule itself makes accessible, since the physiological "in-between region" yields only an *obscurum*. Exceeding its origin-domain, the care reveals itself as a *Sichvergreifen* — a *mis-grasping*, an over-reaching of its ownmost possibilities: it no longer sees its proper possibilities but over-reaches itself in construing the rule as *generalis*, and this is a *self-heightening* mis-grasping, *covered* precisely by the fundamental significance it ascribes to itself, since the rule presents itself as the only possible way of rigorous knowledge.

#### **§ 38\. The care of tranquilizing. Descartes' interpretation of the *verum* as *certum* while retaining the scholastic ontology**

Knowing is characterized by Descartes as *judicium*, as *modus volendi*, as *propensio in bonum* — and the *bonum* for the judgment's inclination is the *verum*. The taking-of-a-rule shows that the care of knowing grasps, in advance, the *verum in the sense of the certum*: truth primarily grasped as certainty. This is the *care of tranquilizing* (Beruhigung) — the care sets itself at rest by fixing the *certum* as the measure and reducing truth to it. Heidegger's summation of Part II: Descartes performs no break with the tradition but seizes a prefigured possibility. He keeps the scholastic ontology of the *verum esse* — truth as *convenientia/adaequatio* grounded in the *intellectus divinus*, being as *creatum esse* — and *within* it substitutes *certum* for *verum*, so that the being of the *res cogitans* is disclosed and secured under the care of certainty. What the modern reading celebrates as the founding of subjectivity is, ontologically, the seizing of an inherited possibility: the care for known knowledge, tracing back through Descartes to the scholastic-Aristotelian ontology of the true, disclosing "consciousness" as its field while never once asking after the *being* of that field. With this the ground is laid for Part III's return to Husserl.

### **Chapter Six: The being-character of the *res cogitans*, of consciousness**

#### **§§ 39–41. The *certum aliquid* as what the care of knowing seeks; the seeking as *dubitare*, *remotio*, *suppositio falsi*; the being of the seeking-being (*ego sum*) as the first thing found**

The *cogito sum* springs out in the task of grounding a fundamental science on a *fundamentum indubitabile* satisfying the *regula generalis*. What is *sought* is the *certum* — and Descartes wants only *a certum aliquid*: what it is is secondary, its *what* finally indifferent so long as it satisfies the rule (*pergam donec aliquid certi... cognoscam* — I will go on until I find something certain, or at least the certainty that there is nothing certain). Sharpened, the sought must be a *certum qua absolutum et simplex*, prescribed by the rule's principle of *series*. The *how* of the seeking is *dubitare*: knowing-as-judging withholds *assensus* from whatever is present as *obscurum* or *incertum* — *judicare* becomes *abstinere*, and in its extension *continere*. And this doubting completes itself as *remotio* (letting-not-come-into-question), which ends in a peculiar *suppositio*: the *dubitatio incerti* becomes a *suppositio falsi*, the doubting of the uncertain reshaped into the assumption of the uncertain *as false*. Here the ground-property of knowing-as-*judicare* shows itself — its *libertas*: the more the seeking knowing holds to its *determinatio in bonum qua certum*, the freer the doubting is; freedom consists in *making serious with the suppositio falsi*.

The inference that carries the whole Cartesian move sits in one schematic chain: the *incertum*, the object of *dubitatio*, is nothing but the *non verum* (since *certum* \= *verum*); as *non verum* it is already *falsum*; as *falsum* it is *non ens*. So Descartes can say the pre-given *does not exist*. This is why the doubt can proceed by *remotio* to a total supposition of falsity — the equation of certainty with truth, inherited through the *Destruktion*, licenses treating the uncertain as *nonexistent*. In this posture the seeking, pressing on, strikes something it must address as *found*, as what corresponds to the seeking: the *being of the seeking-being itself*, the *ego sum*. The determination *toward the certum as fundamentum absolutum* is zeitigt — temporalized — in the *remotio* itself; one must picture this *determinatio* to see the *snapping-shut of the seeking upon the seeking-being*.

#### **§§ 42–43. The seeking after the what-character of the *ego sum*; the *ego cogito*; the valid proposition in the character of all-bindingness**

Descartes admits, for determining the *ego*, only what satisfies the rule. He refuses a *definitio* (*animal rationale* would only breed further questions) and attends instead to what *spontaneously, nature leading*, occurred to him — and reaches the *ego cogito*. The decisive equation comes at the end of Meditation II: *fieri non potest cum videam, sive cum cogitem me videre, ut ego ipse cogitans non aliquid sim* — it cannot be that, while I see, or (what I *no longer distinguish*) while I think that I see, I who think am not something. *cum videam* is set equal to *cum me videre cogitem*: *sentire* \= *cogitare me sentire*, *cogitare* \= *cogitare me cogitare*. So Descartes grasps *cogitare* in advance as a being that *is in the mode of having-itself-along* (Sich-mit-haben) — a being that, in its own way, at once *has itself*. *Cogito* does not mean simply "I establish that something thinks" but a *cogitare* such that I *have myself along* with this being. Later this was called self-consciousness, an inner consciousness *accompanying* every act — but, Heidegger insists, the talk of *accompanying* already misses it: the being of *cogitare* *is* a having-oneself-along, and just because it belongs to *cogitare* to be had-along by itself, Descartes can say *is qui cogitat non potest non existere dum cogitat*.

## **Third Part: Demonstration of the omission of the question of being as exhibition of Dasein**

### **Chapter One: The covering-over of the question of the specific being of the *res cogitans* by the care of certainty**

#### **§§ 44–45. Descartes' perversion of the having-oneself-along into a formal-ontological proposition; the covering of access to the genuine being of the *res cogitans***

Here is the hinge of the entire course, and its bearing on *Sein und Zeit* is direct. Descartes has *seen* a genuine phenomenon — the *Sich-mit-haben*, that the being of *cogitare* has itself along — but he *perverts* it. Because he wants a *proposition*, a formal-logical *cogito* holding of a "thinking-thing," the structure-character of the *cogitatio* becomes irrelevant; it suffices that the thinking-thing *yield the proposition*. In the moment its content is taken *formal-ontologically*, the proposition is *no longer object-apt* (gegenstandstriftig): taking the state of affairs as occasion for a formal proposition, Descartes *perverts the specific being he had previously seen*. The *sum* is *emptied* to the sense of formal-ontological *being-something* — whence the fitting formula *sum res cogitans*: Descartes himself says he can and will assert nothing about the *I* and the *am*, because the question of the *specific being* of the *res cogitans* concerns him only secondarily. The having-oneself-along is levelled into an "accompanying inner consciousness," and access to the genuine being of the *res cogitans* is thereby *covered over*.

But Heidegger draws the positive method out of the same passage: taken *not* directly (where it says nothing) but as a *formal indication* (formale Anzeige) — referred to the respective *concretion* of what it each time means — the proposition *has its right*. To the being of *cogitatio* belongs *Jeweiligkeit* (in-each-case-mineness), and every being in the sense of Dasein is characterized by its Jeweiligkeit, further by its *temporality*, and by the specific way this *ego sum* is in *what it has*. This is the course's methodological deposit: the *cogito sum* is not a false proposition but a *formally indicative* one that must be led back to the concrete Dasein it indicates rather than absolutized into a formal-ontological principle.

The summary characterization of what Descartes found: the being-predication of the *res cogitans* is *esse certum* and only this; it was never in the tendency of the seeking to interrogate *cogitare* as to its *specific being* — that question lies outside Descartes' task, though he later claims this formal being as *absolute* being; the seeking *itself* blocks in advance the way to determining this being, since the being of the *res cogitans* must *set back* its specific being to become formal and enter the proposition as a mere something; the being-determinations taken into account stem from a *traditional ontology* not oriented on the *res cogitans* at all; and that ontology takes its *fundamentum* already reshaped, most clearly in that *esse* means nothing but *esse creatum* — God and the created grasped in the *same* category, *esse creatum*, God only the *causa prima et absoluta* of it. The whole "modern" turn to consciousness rests on an ontology of createdness that never asks after the being of consciousness — the *omission* Part III names in its title: *the demonstration of the omission of the question of being as the exhibition of Dasein*.

### **Chapter Two: Descartes' question of the certainty-being of the *res cogitans* and the indeterminacy of the being-character of consciousness as the thematic field of Husserl's phenomenology**

#### **§§ 46–47. Descartes and Husserl: fundamental differences; connection and unitary ground-tendency in the care of certainty**

One must not read Husserl through external analogies with Descartes, nor identify them: there is a *fundamental difference* in *how Husserl grasps consciousness in content*. But precisely as to the *disposal of the question of being* of consciousness, and as to the sense of the *esse certum*, Husserl moves *wholly in Descartes' sense* — indeed today more than ever wants to go *beyond* Descartes in this, to grasp consciousness *absolutely*. Two negative points fix the connection: the being-region of consciousness is posited as *absolute* without its being being interrogated in any sense other than that of absolute certainty-being; and the sense of certainty-being, the phenomenon of *evidence*, Husserl grasps far more sharply than anyone before him. The Cartesian *cogito sum* is the finding in the character of *fundamentum absolutum simplex* at which Descartes *tranquilizes himself*; from that being-character *as a contentful proposition* the being-sense of the *res cogitans* is determined, and the question of its being is *once and for all disposed of* — Descartes needs it no longer, since it remains only to reach further propositions by *deductio*. And the concept of being that results, decisive for Husserl too, is: *being \= possible domain-being for working-up by a science*. Where the *Sache* is primary the science is secondary; here the science and its possible erection are primary, the being of what is treated secondary — a "grotesque" heightening, Heidegger says, of a tendency toward founding ever-new disciplines that became a ground-tendency of intellectual history since Descartes.

### **Chapter Three: Husserl's more originary omission of the question of being with respect to the thematic field of phenomenology, and the task of seeing and explicating Dasein in its being**

#### **§§ 48–49. Husserl's *disfiguring* (Verunstaltung) of the phenomenological findings through the care of certainty inherited from Descartes; the research into the origin-history of the categories**

The same care of certainty leads Husserl to *disfigure* the positive achievements of phenomenology in three respects. First, *intentionality* is grasped — less expressly than inexpressly — as a *specific theoretical comporting*: intentionality gets rendered "meaning" (Meinen), and it is claimed that for every complex intentional nexus *theoretical meaning* forms the foundation, that every willing, loving, hating is founded on a *representing* that first gives the will-able, the hate- and love-able. This is a methodological mistake — analogizing the study of emotional experiences to knowing — and it carries over structures won in a wholly different direction of regard; even beings get taken in the specific theoretical apprehension, *exemplified in the real being qua Ding*, so that nature becomes the foundation onto which culture and history are then built as *values* laid over natural being. Second, *evidence* — which Husserl set on suitable ground for the first time, superior to all prior accounts — is interpreted in its enactment as the *covering* (Deckung) of the meant with what is grasped at itself, normed by concord and conflict as the *cogito sum* is by the principle of contradiction. But this is a *grasping- and determining-evidence* of theoretical knowing, transferred by analogy; the proper evidence-question begins only with the evidence of the *access* to a being and the *disclosing* of it, within which theoretical evidence has only its place. Third, the *eidetic reduction* of pure consciousness proceeds under the leadership of *ontological determinations foreign to consciousness* — the determinations of traditional ontology and formal logic — so that pure consciousness, once won through the transcendental reduction, is made a *possible domain of a science* by importing categories that owe their origin *not* to any analysis of *this* being's specific being-characters. Hence the task, if making consciousness a theme is legitimate at all: *what is the specific being here made a domain?* — which requires the *research into the origin-history of the categories* (§49) as the presupposition of seeing and determining Dasein.

#### **§ 50\. Repetition of the traversed characters of the care of knowing, and the exhibition of Dasein itself**

The course closes by drawing its whole indictment into a *positive exhibition of Dasein* — and this final section is, more than any other, the seedbed of *Sein und Zeit*. The characters of the care of certainty gather into three groups: the *self-heightening mis-grasping* and *tranquilizing* (the care rests in certainty itself and lets its knowing never reach the being of itself); the *covering* and forming of *needlessness*, whose telling moment is the care's relation to *tradition* — the care that wants a foundation takes over, uncritically, the whole stock of ontological determinations that give its own being its ground, so that tradition *befalls* it without becoming express, which means the care is *not transparent to its own being-possibilities* (for only where a being sees itself in its situation can the tradition that befalls it become express); and the *levelling of being* (Einebnung), all beings seen on one plane dictated by the idea of scientific systematics, so that every being-determination, and above all that of Dasein, moves on a level no longer apt for a genuine question of being.

These characterize the being of knowing *as a way of Dasein itself* — and since this knowing is thereby *removed* from beings and above all from the being of Dasein, i.e. from *itself*, the movedness is the *flight of Dasein before itself* (Flucht des Daseins vor ihm selbst). This flight does not simply move *away* into science; in one with the flight, Dasein *cares for the displacing of itself* — the ontology built from the care for the idea of science is held fast as the *only* possible way of interrogating Dasein, and Dasein is thereby buried. Here Heidegger names the ground-phenomenon: *Verdrehung*, distortion (long misnamed "reflection"). The being one calls "consciousness" is not "something relating to something," a subject before an object; it is a being that, *in its being-placed-toward the world, co-cares for its own Dasein* — no express reflection on the I need appear; in the *matter* one pursues lies a caring for Dasein itself, and *precisely where reflection is absent* the phenomenon shows in its ownmost sense, as the *drawing of the proper being of care into the cared-for*. The linguistic difficulty is that Dasein, adequately determined, is not a being one *has* but the being one *is* — the being that carries in itself the possibility of becoming "I am," a possibility Dasein tends to *displace*.

This *Verdrehung* is a *methodical guiding thread*: from it, intentionality — consciousness's ground-character — is *cut back to its limits*, the limits of its interpretive function; and it is the structural ground on which *joy, terror, grief, anxiety* become explicable, phenomena missed when construed as intentionality. *Anxiety* cannot be grasped as a being-related-to-something; it is a phenomenon of *Dasein itself*. And the course ends on the constellation *Sein und Zeit* will make famous. Dasein is *Faktisch* — here and now, in its Jeweiligkeit; facticity is not the concretion of a universal but the *originary determination* of Dasein's specific being. In fleeing itself by way of covering, Dasein shows itself as *warding itself off* (sich seiner selbst erwehren) — and this self-warding is not contingent but *makes up its being*. What it wards off, the *Bedrohung* (threat), lies *in Dasein itself*: the threat is *that it is* — *daß es ist ist die Bedrohung des Daseins selbst*. Reading the threat off the *whither* of the flight — the care of certainty wants to go up in a *tranquilizing*, and curiosity (the care that especially led Greek knowing) wants to become *at home* in beings, to be at home in the mode of secured Dasein — Heidegger names what Dasein flees: *Unheimlichkeit*, uncanniness, the not-being-at-home. Uncanniness is the proper threat under which Dasein stands, the threat that *is in Dasein at itself*; it shows itself in the *everydayness* of Dasein, has nothing to do with loneliness, is not seen when Dasein loses itself in reflection, and is covered when Dasein is grasped as *personality*. Asked *what* it is, uncanniness is *nothing*; asked *where*, it is *nowhere* — it expresses itself in the flight of Dasein before itself, the *flight into familiarity and tranquilizing*. With this the "introduction to phenomenological research" arrives at its true beginning: not consciousness, but the Dasein that flees its own uncanniness — the being whose being is at issue for it, which *Sein und Zeit* will take up three years later.

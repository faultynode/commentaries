# Commentary on Heidegger, *Prolegomena zur Geschichte des Zeitbegriffs* (GA 20)

*Marburg lecture course, Summer Semester 1925. Cited by the printed pagination of the Klostermann edition (ed. Petra Jaeger), which is the standard scholarly reference for this volume. Section numbers (§) are Heidegger's own.*

## Orientation and standing misreading-guards

GA 20 is the immediate precursor to *Sein und Zeit*, drafted the year before the treatise. Roughly two-thirds of it reappears, often verbatim, in Division One of *Sein und Zeit*; the last quarter diverges sharply, presenting an analysis of death, conscience, and time that Heidegger reworked before publication. The course therefore rewards reading as both a first draft of the published existential analytic and an independent argument with its own emphases — above all its far more extended engagement with Husserl, whom *Sein und Zeit* mentions only glancingly.

The traps this text sets, and which the commentary guards against throughout:

*Phenomenology is not psychology, and intentionality is not a relation between two things.* The whole point of the immanent critique of Husserl (§§5–13) is that intentionality is the structure of comportment, not a bridge thrown across a gap between an inner mental item and an outer object. Reading intentionality as "the mind's directedness toward a representation" collapses exactly what Heidegger is trying to open up.

*The critique of Husserl is immanent, not external.* Heidegger does not reject phenomenology; he claims phenomenology is unfaithful to its own maxim. The three "neglects" of §13 (the question of the being of the intentional, the question of the being of the region "consciousness," the question of the sense of being as such) are charges of infidelity, not refutations from outside.

*Categorial intuition is the hinge, not a curiosity.* The doctrine that we intuit categorial form — being, unity, plurality, relation — and not just sensory content (§6) is what licenses Heidegger's later claim that "being" is given and can be interrogated phenomenologically. Skip it and the leap from Husserl to the question of being looks arbitrary.

*"Being" (Sein) is never a being (Seiendes).* The ontological difference is not yet named as such here, but it governs everything. When Heidegger says the tradition "passed over" the world or forgot the question of being, the point is always that it treated being as if it were some highest or most general entity.

*The environing world (Umwelt) is primary, the present-at-hand is derivative.* Heidegger inverts the traditional order. What is first given is not a stock of neutral things (present-at-hand, *vorhanden*) to which value and use are then added, but a meaningful workshop of handy equipment (ready-to-hand, *zuhanden*). Knowing is a deficient, founded mode of a prior absorbed dealing, not the basic relation to the world.

*Das Man is not a sociological observation.* The "anyone" (das Man) is an ontological structure of Dasein's being-with, the answer to the question "who?" in everydayness — not a lament about conformism or mass society, though it grounds the possibility of such laments.

*Care (Sorge) is not worry.* Sorge is the structural being of Dasein, articulated as ahead-of-itself / already-in / alongside; anxiety, fear, and everyday "cares" are modes founded on it, not its meaning.

A fixed glossary of retained terms: *Dasein* (the entity that we ourselves are, whose being is at issue for it); *Sorge* (care, the being of Dasein); *das Man* (the anyone); *In-der-Welt-sein* (being-in-the-world); *Zuhandenheit* / *Vorhandenheit* (readiness-to-hand / presence-at-hand); *Bedeutsamkeit* (significance, the worldhood of the world); *Befindlichkeit* (disposedness, being-found-in-a-mood); *Verfallen* (falling); *Entdecktheit* (uncoveredness); *Rede* (discourse). Everything else is rendered in ordinary English.

## Introduction: The Theme of the Lecture Course and Its Manner of Treatment

### § 1. Nature and history as object-domains for the sciences

Heidegger opens by unpacking the subtitle, "Prolegomena to a Phenomenology of History and Nature." *Prolegomena* names what must be settled in advance before such a phenomenology can even begin; what that advance work is becomes clear only once we see what a phenomenology of history and nature is supposed to do. The two guiding words, "history" and "nature," first present themselves as the object-domains of the two great families of empirical science — natural science on one side, the human, cultural, and historical sciences on the other. We habitually understand history and nature *through* the sciences that study them. But this makes them accessible only so far as they are already thematic objects of those sciences, and it is not settled that an object-domain coincides with the primary subject-matter from which a science first carves its themes.

The load-bearing suspicion follows: the very split into two domains may be the symptom that a single, more original nexus has been covered over — the unity of nature and spirit in human existence — which no later stitching-together of the two halves can recover. The division arises only from the side of the sciences, which level history and nature down to object-domains. A phenomenology of history and nature must instead disclose reality as it shows itself *before* scientific questioning, as pre-given to that questioning: not a phenomenology of the sciences of history and nature, nor of history and nature as their objects, but a disclosure of the original manner of being and constitution of both. Only this lays the ground for a theory of the sciences — a grounding of their genesis out of pre-theoretical experience, of the manner of their access to pre-given reality, and of the concept-formation proper to them.

Heidegger names the discipline that does this "productive logic": an anticipatory disclosure and conceptual penetration of possible object-domains that, unlike traditional theory of science, does not trail behind some contingent, historically given science and dissect its structure, but leaps ahead into the primary subject-field of a possible science and, by disclosing the constitution of being of that field, first makes available the basic structure of that science's possible object. This, he claims, is the original sense of logic in Plato and Aristotle — realized there only within narrow limits, and buried since. The forward-connection is explicit: phenomenology's task is to make subject-domains intelligible prior to scientific treatment, and on that ground the sciences themselves.

The diagnosis of the "crisis of the sciences" supports the claim that one cannot reach the subject-fields by way of a theory of the sciences that happen to exist. The crisis is spoken of in two senses. There is the loss, felt especially by the young, of a living relation to science — the mood around Max Weber's lecture, which Heidegger reads as despair papered over by building worldviews and mythologies on top of science. But the *real* crisis is internal: the basic relation of individual sciences to the matters they interrogate has itself become questionable, and with it a live tendency toward reflection on the basic structure of those matters, toward securing shaky foundational concepts out of original acquaintance with the things. Genuine progress happens only in this field. The historical human sciences, Heidegger adds pointedly, lack such crises not because they are secure but because they have not yet even reached the maturity that makes revolutions possible.

He then runs quickly through the disciplines to show the tendency at work. In mathematics, the foundational crisis of formalism (Hilbert) versus intuitionism (Brouwer, Weyl), where the latter — influenced by phenomenology — asks whether what is primarily given is the determinate structure of the objects themselves, e.g. the continuum before its scientific interrogation. In physics, relativity theory, which Heidegger insists is no relativism but the opposite: a theory of the conditions of access designed so that the invariance of physical law is preserved, aimed at finding the in-itself of nature. In biology, the effort to think the basic sense of the living against a mechanism into which even vitalism falls by trying to specify a "life-force" in mechanical terms. In the historical sciences, Unger's literary history as a history of problems, pressing past mere artistic presentation toward the history of the matter presented. In theology, the drive — through a renewal of faith — to win an original explication of the human being's being toward God, prying the fundamental question of the human loose from the traditional dogmatic system, which rests on a philosophy and conceptuality that have stood the questions of the human, of God, and of their relation on their heads.

Everywhere the same movement: a pressing forward to an original understanding of the matters themselves. The step-laws of such revolutions differ across the sciences because the manner of being of what is experienced differs, because the sciences are nothing but concrete possibilities of human existence expressing itself about its world and about itself. If the sciences are not to be a contingent enterprise justified only by tradition, the decisive question — the point at which the crisis is answered — is to bring the matters interrogated to an original experience *before* their covering-over by a determinate scientific questioning. Heidegger restricts himself to two domains, history and nature, whose original manner of being is to be exhibited.

### § 2. Prolegomena to a phenomenology of history and nature guided by the history of the concept of time

The orientation gained from the sciences is external and not the genuine lead-in to the matter itself. To see history and nature in their reality *before* scientific treatment means winning a horizon out of which they can first be set off — and this horizon must itself be a field of subject-matters from which history and nature stand out. The prolegomena concern the laying-free of this field, the states of affairs that lie before history and nature and out of which they draw their being. And this laying-free is to be undertaken by way of a *history of the concept of time*.

Heidegger anticipates that this looks like a strange detour, and dissolves the strangeness in two moves. First, externally: both historical and natural reality are, traditionally, courses of process in time; time-measurement is basic to physics, chronology indispensable to historical research. History and nature are, crudely put, temporal. Against this temporal whole one sets the extra-temporal (the mathematical) and the supra-temporal (the eternal, of metaphysics or theology). So — schematically — time is an *index* for the division and demarcation of the domains of being as such. The concept of time yields information about the manner and reality of such demarcation of the universal realm of beings; at each stage of its formation it becomes a guiding thread for the question of the being of beings and their possible regions — though without explicit awareness of this role, and therefore handled crudely, never bringing the possibilities latent in it to fruition. The concept of time is thus no arbitrary concept: it stands in connection with the ground-question of philosophy, the question of the being of beings, of the reality of the real.

The history of the concept of time is accordingly the history of the *discovery* of time and of its conceptual interpretation — which is to say, the history of the question of the being of beings, carried each time by the prevailing understanding of time and the prevailing stage of the concept's elaboration. Read to its end, this history is the history of a *decline*, a mutilation of the ground-question of research into the being of beings: a history of the incapacity to pose the question of being radically anew — an incapacity grounded in the being of Dasein itself. This is the first appearance of the guiding thesis that the forgetting of the question of being is itself an ontological fact about the questioner. Against this external characterization, the course will eventually face the question: what is it about time, about the concept of time, about the understanding regard upon time, that fits it for this peculiar, hitherto self-evident function of characterizing and dividing the regions of reality into temporal, extra-temporal, and supra-temporal?

The manner of the treatment follows from the fundamental import of the concept. A history of the concept of time could be carried out as a doxographical collection of opinions about time and formulations of its concept, in the hope of thereby winning an understanding of time itself. But the most careful collections of opinions remain blind so long as what is constantly being asked for in such historical survey has not first been clarified. One will never win the understanding of time itself out of the history of the concept of time; on the contrary, only a prior worked-out understanding of the phenomenon of time lets the earlier concepts of time be understood at all. Heidegger presses the objection further against himself: if a mere systematic discussion of the concept of time suffices to determine history and nature as temporal realities, why the history at all? The answer overturns the traditional partition of philosophical labor. The objection holds only so long as one believes a systematic philosophical treatment is possible without being, at its innermost ground, historical. If it turns out that the ground-question — the question of the being of beings — forces us into an original research-sphere lying *before* the traditional division of philosophical work into historical and systematic knowledge, then the prolegomena to the investigation of beings in their being are to be won only by way of history: the manner of research is neither historical nor systematic but *phenomenological*. Making the necessity and sense of such fundamental research evident — and evident out of the content of the matters themselves, not out of some arbitrarily invented idea of philosophy or a so-called standpoint — is itself among the aims of the course. The historical clarification of the history of the concept of time is only didactically separate from the analysis of the phenomenon of time itself, which is in turn the preparation for the possibility of historical understanding.

### § 3. Outline of the lecture course

The ground-question about the reality of history and nature is the ground-question about the being of a determinate subject-domain, and for the question of being the concept of time is the guiding thread; so the question of the being of a being, if it is to understand itself radically, is bound to a discussion of the phenomenon of time. From this the course's articulation into three parts follows: a first part, the analysis of the phenomenon of time and the winning of the concept of time; a second part, the disclosure of the history of the concept of time; a third part, on the ground of the first two, the working-out of the horizon for the question of being as such and of the being of history and nature in particular.

A short preparatory part precedes the three, orienting the reader to the general methodological character of the inquiry — a determination of the sense and tasks of phenomenological research — and is itself in three chapters: the origination and first breakthrough of phenomenological research; the fundamental discoveries of phenomenology, its principle, and the clarification of its name; and the first formation of phenomenological research together with the necessity of a radical reflection within it and out of it. The first part divides into three sections: the preparatory description of the field in which the phenomenon of time becomes visible; the laying-free of time itself; and the conceptual interpretation. The second part, the history of the concept of time, is pursued backward from the present: Bergson's theory of time; the concept of time in Kant and Newton; and the first conceptual discovery of time in Aristotle. These three main stages are the stations at which a *relative* transformation of the concept of time took shape — relative, because at bottom the Aristotelian concept is retained throughout. Bergson does attempt to press past it to something more original, which justifies treating him separately; but seen down to the categorial foundations he presupposes — quality, succession — he remains traditional and does not move the matters from the spot. The third part treats the exposition of the question of being as such and of the being of history and nature in particular, and at the same time puts us in a position to determine the sense and task of phenomenological research more transparently, from the clarified subject-content of the theme.

*Note on what survives.* Heidegger executed only the preparatory part and the first section of the first part (the existential analytic of Dasein, §§9–36) before the semester ran out. The promised history of the concept of time in Bergson, Kant, Newton, and Aristotle, and the third part on the horizon for the question of being, were never delivered. What the course actually produces is thus the "preparatory description of the field in which the phenomenon of time becomes visible" — that is, the analysis of Dasein — pursued far enough to reach time as the being in which Dasein can be its own wholeness (§36). This is why GA 20 reads, in the event, as an early *Sein und Zeit* rather than as the history of the concept of time its title promises.

## Preparatory Part: The Sense and Task of Phenomenological Research

### Chapter One: The origination and first breakthrough of phenomenological research

#### § 4. The situation of philosophy in the second half of the nineteenth century. Philosophy and the sciences

The prehistory of phenomenology must be understood from the historical situation of philosophy in the last decades of the nineteenth century, which is itself determined by a transformation of scientific consciousness after the collapse of the idealist systems — a transformation touching not only philosophy but every science. Out of it comes the late-century attempt to secure a right of its own for scientific philosophy: to leave the individual sciences their autonomy while carving out a distinct field for philosophy. This yields a philosophy with the essential character of theory of science, a logic of the sciences. That is one mark of the renewal. The second: it proceeds not from an original return to the interrogated matters but from a return to a historically given philosophy, that of Kant. Philosophy thereby becomes traditionalist, taking over a determinate complex of a determinate set of questions, and taking up a determinate position toward the concrete sciences. This is the frame within which phenomenology will define itself — and the frame it will have to break.

The scientific situation around mid-century is set by the watchword in every science: facts of experience, not speculation and empty concepts. Its dominance has many grounds, above all the collapse of idealism. The sciences concentrated their force in the fields of experience, in the two already-separating domains of the historical world and nature. What survived of philosophical reflection did so as a crude materialism, a so-called natural-scientific worldview. The historical sciences renounced philosophical reflection altogether; oriented in the world of Goethe and Lessing, they counted only concrete work — the tendency toward "facts." This required a first task: opening and securing the sources, and with it philological criticism, the technique of interpretation. But the substantive interpretation, the "conception" of the source-material, was left to the historian's spiritual existence and shifted with the impulses alive in him — from the 1870s onward, essentially political ones — producing the 1880s dispute over whether history is cultural or political history. No one pressed into the sphere of principle, because every means for doing so was lacking; the historian's basic relation to his object was uncertain, left to popular cultivation. This condition, Heidegger says, still holds even where both sides are now gathered under "history of spirit."

The natural sciences of the day stand in the great tradition of Galileo and Newton, but the field expanded into the physiological and biological, and with the physiological, psychic life entered the horizon of natural-scientific questioning — first the regions bound most tightly to physiology, life as it expresses itself in the sense-organs. Investigated by natural-scientific method, this became sense-psychology, the psychology of perception and sensation, closely tied to physiology; psychology became physiological psychology, as Wundt's chief work shows. Here was found the domain in which even psychic life, spirit, could be disclosed with natural-scientific means. Heidegger fixes the decisive point: under the influence of English empiricism reaching back to Descartes, psychology's task was conceived as *science of consciousness*. In medieval and Greek philosophy the whole human being was still in view, and inner psychic life was grasped in a natural experience not yet marked off as inner perception against outer. Since Descartes the concept of psychology, of the science of the psychic, is characteristically altered: the science of the spiritual, of reason, becomes science of consciousness, a science that wins its object in so-called inner experience. Even physiological psychology takes this framing for granted, formulating it externally as an opposition — not science of the soul as a substance, but of psychic appearances, of what gives itself in inner experience. The telling development is that natural science thereby pushes into a territory traditionally reserved for philosophy, with the tendency of a natural-scientific psychology to install itself in the field of philosophy and even to become philosophy's basic science. This last clause names the target of the whole chapter: psychologism, the elevation of a natural-scientific psychology of consciousness into first philosophy.

**a) The position of positivism.** Positivism reigns in every scientific discipline: the tendency toward the "positive," understood as the factual, facts in a determinate interpretation of reality — countable, weighable, measurable facts, the experimentally determinable, or in history the occurrences first accessible in the sources. It is to be understood not only as a maxim of concrete research but as a theory of knowledge and of culture as such. As theory it was developed simultaneously in France and England by Comte and Mill. Comte distinguishes three stages of human development — religiosity, metaphysics, science — the scientific stage now at its beginning, its goal a sociology built with natural-scientific method, a general doctrine of the human and human relations. Mill grasps positivism philosophically as a universal theory of science; the sixth book of his *System of Logic* treats the logic of the "moral sciences," his name for what we call history or the human sciences. This Anglo-French positivism soon entered Germany and around the 1850s awakened theory-of-science reflection. Within this movement Lotze stands isolated, keeping the tradition of German idealism awake while trying to do justice to the positivism of the sciences — a peculiar transitional position not without significance for what followed.

**b) Neo-Kantianism — the theory-of-science rediscovery of Kant.** In the 1860s Mill's *Logic* became more widely known, and the possibility of a question about the structure of the concrete sciences offered philosophy an autonomous task while preserving the sciences' own right. This theory-of-science task awakened the memory of Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason*, now read in that key. The return to Kant, the founding of neo-Kantianism, proceeds under a quite determinate set of questions, the theory-of-science one — a narrowed conception of Kant that only now, Heidegger says, one is trying to overcome. This reflection also revealed a fundamental omission in the previous theory of science: faced with the second great group of empirical sciences, the historical disciplines, it saw itself required to supplement Kant's work with a theory of *historical* reason, as Dilthey had already formulated the task in the 1870s. The determinately directed rediscovery of Kant first concentrated on a positivistic interpretation of his philosophy, carried out by Cohen, founder of the Marburg School, in *Kants Theorie der Erfahrung*. The title shows how Kant is fundamentally seen: theory of experience, experience understood as *scientific* experience as realized in mathematical physics — a Kantian-oriented theory of the positivism of the sciences, executed as investigation of the structure of knowledge wholly within the Kantian horizon, a working-out of the constitutive moments of knowledge in the sense of the science of consciousness. Here too, in theory-of-science work, a return to consciousness, matching the direction of psychology. However differently consciousness became the theme in natural-scientific psychology and in epistemology, it remained — and remains to this day — the unspoken thematic field: the sphere that Descartes made the ground-sphere of philosophical reflection. The convergence Heidegger is tracing is the point: psychology and epistemology, apparent opponents, share the same Cartesian ground of consciousness, and phenomenology will have to be measured against whether it too remains on that ground.

**c) Critique of positivism — Dilthey's demand for an autonomous method for the human sciences.** Mill tried, in the sixth book of his *Logic*, to transfer natural-scientific method to the historical sciences. It was Dilthey who, in his early years, recognized both the impossibility of this transfer and the need for a positive theory of the sciences drawn from themselves. He saw that a philosophical understanding of the historical disciplines can succeed only by reflecting on their object, the reality actually thematic in them — by laying free the basic structure of that reality, which he called *life*. From this newly and independently posed task he presses to the need for a psychology, a science of consciousness, but not in the natural-scientific or epistemological sense: the task of seeing "life" itself, the ground-reality of history, in its structures. The decisive thing in Dilthey's questioning is not the theory of the sciences of history but the tendency to bring the reality of the historical into view and from there to clarify the manner and possibility of interpretation. He did not, Heidegger grants, pose this radically enough; he moved at the same time within contemporary questioning, asking alongside it after the structure of knowledge itself, so that the *Introduction to the Human Sciences* (1883) is in the main theory-of-science in orientation. Dilthey is the positive hero of this narrative: the one who pressed toward the reality of the historical (life) itself, and whose failure to reach a method of his own is a failure of radicality, not of instinct.

**d) The trivialization of Dilthey's questioning by Windelband and Rickert.** The impulses of the Marburg School and of Dilthey were then taken up by Windelband and Rickert, flattened and trivialized, the problems bent past recognition, so that theory-of-science clarification became an empty methodology. It no longer asks after the structure of knowledge, of research, of access to the several realities, still less after the structure of those realities; its sole theme is the logical structure of *scientific presentation*. This goes so far that in Rickert's theory of science the sciences under discussion are no longer recognizable — mere schemata of sciences are laid at the base. This disfigurement had the dubious success of covering over the real sense of Dilthey's questioning and blocking its positive effect down to the present. Against it, the positive thing in Dilthey is the tendency toward the reality itself that is thematic in the historical sciences; on this ground he keeps a distinguished position in late-nineteenth-century philosophy, precisely against the Marburg School, because he stayed free of a dogmatic Kantianism and, with a drive toward the more radical, tried to philosophize purely from the matters themselves. But tradition and contemporary philosophy were too powerful to hold his peculiar nature on a sure path; he often wavered, sometimes seeing his own work in the terms of the very traditional philosophy it opposed, yet again and again the elementary instinct of his own questioning broke through. This uncertainty shows that he reached no method and no genuine set of questions of his own — though his thrust into the proper domain remains the decisive thing. Heidegger uses the point to state his own criterion: what is decisive in philosophy is not the battle of schools and standpoints, nor treating things again with traditional concepts under an adopted standpoint, but disclosing new subject-domains and, in productive concept-formation, making them the possession of science. The revived contemporary tendency toward system carries no sense dictated by the matters; it is purely traditionalist, like the renewal of Kant that runs on past Kant to Fichte and Hegel.

**e) Philosophy as "scientific philosophy" — psychology as the basic science of philosophy (the doctrine of consciousness).** Summarizing: by mid-century a determinate scientific philosophy became dominant, and the expression "scientific philosophy" has a threefold sense. First, it is philosophy *of* the sciences — theory of scientific knowledge, having the fact of science for its proper object. Second, through this very questioning after the structure of given sciences it wins a theme of its own, researched by its own method, since it no longer falls within individual-scientific consideration; it is "scientific" because it gains its own field and method, a method that keeps its security through constant orientation to the factual conduct of the sciences, thereby avoiding worldview-speculation. Third, it seeks to ground the various disciplines that all direct themselves at consciousness through an original science of consciousness itself, a psychology. Neo-Kantianism directed itself sharply against natural-scientific psychology, but this did not prevent psychology — from the side of natural science (Helmholtz) and from philosophy alike — from being raised to the basic science of philosophy: if knowledge is an act of consciousness, there is a theory of knowing only once psychic life, consciousness, has been given in advance and investigated "scientifically," i.e. by natural-scientific method. Heidegger notes in passing that present-day psychology stands, under phenomenology's influence, at a wholly different level. At the century's end, "scientific" philosophy across all directions has consciousness for its theme, with explicit knowledge of its connection to Descartes, who first determined consciousness, the *res cogitans*, as the basic theme of philosophy. Heidegger declines to trace the tangled details as unproductive, but flags one thread: since 1840 an Aristotle-tradition is alive within the movement, founded by Trendelenburg out of opposition to Hegel and continuous with the historical research of Schleiermacher and Böckh in Greek philosophy. Trendelenburg's students are Dilthey and Brentano — the hinge that carries the narrative directly into the origins of phenomenology.

**α) Franz Brentano.** Brentano studied in Berlin in the late 1860s, originally in Catholic theology; his first work took Aristotle as its theme, interpreting him from the horizon of medieval philosophy, above all Aquinas. Heidegger grants this recasts Aristotle substantially, even distorts him, but insists the decisive gain lies elsewhere: through this engagement with Greek philosophy Brentano won more original horizons for philosophical questioning. Inner difficulties of the Catholic system — the Trinity, and in the 1870s papal infallibility — drove him out of that world, but he carried off determinate horizons and a reverence for Aristotle into a free, unbound philosophical science. His path out of tradition was marked by Descartes, so that his work is a peculiar mixture of Aristotelian-scholastic philosophizing with the modern Cartesian problematic; here too the aim is a science of consciousness. The decisive step in the *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874) is that Brentano first freed himself from transferring natural-scientific and physiological method to the study of psychic life. His habilitation thesis of 1866 — the true method of philosophy is none other than that of natural science — must not be read, Heidegger warns, as demanding that natural-scientific method be applied to philosophy; it demands the reverse. Philosophy is to proceed *in its own field* as the natural sciences do in theirs, i.e. to draw its concepts from its own matters, with fundamental regard for the kind of thing at issue. The thesis switches off natural-scientific method rather than importing it. This misreading-guard is the whole point: "empirical" in Brentano means factual and non-constructive, oriented to the matter, not inductive in the natural-scientific sense.

For psychology this means: before all theories about the connection of the psychic with the bodily, of sensory life with the sense-organs, the first task is simply to take up the facts of psychic life as they are immediately accessible. The very first thing is a *classification of psychic phenomena* — a division that follows not an arbitrary principle brought from outside but the nature of the psychic itself, an ordering (and with it a formation of basic concepts) drawn from the essence of the matters at issue. Ordering always proceeds under a point of view, and this can be of three kinds: one can order a given manifold under an *invented schema* (imagined inner-to-outer versus outer-to-inner processes); one can take the point of view from a neighboring object-domain (ordering psychic processes by reference to physiological relations, as when thought and will are fixed by motor-nerve phenomena); or one can *draw the point of view from the very facts to be ordered*, winning the principle from the matters rather than bringing it to them. This third is Brentano's maxim: the ordering of experiences must be *natural*, taking up into the classification what by its nature belongs there — where "nature" means what a thing is as seen from itself. A genuine classification proceeds only from prior acquaintance with the objects, from the study of the objects; one must already have knowledge of their basic structures to order them in a way faithful to the object.

So the question arises: what is the nature of psychic as against physical phenomena? Brentano answers that psychic phenomena are distinguished from all physical ones by nothing so much as that something objective dwells within them. Any differences *within* the field of the psychic must therefore be differences in this basic structure of in-dwelling, differences in the way something is objective to an experience — in representing the represented, in judging the judged, in willing the willed. This basic structure, that something objective dwells in every experience, Brentano calls *intentional in-existence*. "Intentio" is a scholastic expression meaning directedness-toward; Brentano speaks of the intentional in-existence of the object, and stresses that Aristotle already laid this point of view at the basis of his treatment of the psychic and that scholasticism took up the phenomenon. On this guiding structure Brentano divides psychic comportment into three basic classes: presentation in the sense of presenting (wherever something appears, is simply given and taken in — a simple *having-there* of something); judgment (a taking-as-true or rejecting-as-false, a determinate position toward the presented as presented); and interest (love, emotion — the class comprising all psychic phenomena not in the first two, for which, Brentano notes, a proper term is lacking; later one said valuing, or better, value-taking). A basic thesis governs their relation: every psychic phenomenon is either itself a presentation or has presentations at its base — nothing can be judged, desired, hoped, or feared unless it is presented. Simple having-there of something thereby gets the function of a founding comportment. Heidegger notes the lines of influence: this descriptive psychology worked on James, and through him back on Bergson's *Essai sur les données immédiates de la conscience*, and strongly on Dilthey's 1894 program for a descriptive and analytic psychology as the basic science of the human sciences. But the truly decisive thing in the development of Brentano's questioning is that he became Husserl's teacher.

**β) Edmund Husserl.** Husserl was originally a mathematician, a student of Weierstrass, and took his doctorate with a mathematical work; what he heard of philosophy did not exceed the ordinary. Only after his promotion did he attend Brentano's lectures, and the personal impression — the passion of questioning and observing — held him for two years (1884–1886) and decided the scientific direction of his work, settling his wavering between mathematics and philosophy. Within an unproductive contemporary philosophy Brentano opened for him the possibility of a *scientific* philosophy. Characteristically, Husserl did not begin with some fetched-in problem but philosophized on the ground he had: his Brentanian-methodical reflection turned on mathematics. He worked first, traditionally speaking, on the logic of mathematics, but above all on the analysis of the structure of mathematical objects — number — habilitating in Halle under Stumpf, Brentano's oldest student, with a work on the concept of number, made possible as a real investigation of the matters on the basis of Brentano's descriptive psychology. Soon the questions widened to the principled, pressing to the fundamental concepts of thought as such and of objects as such; the task of a scientific logic grew, and with it reflection on the right method for investigating logic's objects — a more radical grasp of what Brentano's descriptive psychology offered, together with a fundamental critique of the contemporary confusion of psychological-genetic with logical questioning.

This work occupied Husserl more than twelve years and issued in the *Logical Investigations* (1900/01), with which phenomenological research first broke through; it became the founding book of phenomenology. Dilthey was the first to see its central significance, calling it the first great scientific advance in philosophy since Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason*; seventy years old, he ran seminars on it and, in a letter to Husserl, compared their work to boring a mountain from opposite sides and meeting in the middle — Dilthey finding here a first fulfillment of the fundamental science of life itself he had sought for decades. The book's reception, Heidegger stresses, fell far short of what it demands. Lipps and his Munich students took the *Investigations* merely as improved descriptive psychology; Natorp, for the Marburg School, credited only the first volume's critique of logic and its demonstration that logic cannot be grounded on psychology, dismissing the decisive second volume as a relapse into the very psychologism the first had refuted. Heidegger lays part of the blame on Husserl's own self-interpretation in the introduction to the second volume — "phenomenology is descriptive psychology" — a formula wholly inadequate to what the volume actually accomplishes; Husserl, writing it, was not yet able to survey what he had in fact laid down, and corrected the misinterpretation himself only two years later. The *Investigations*, for all their foundational character, bring no deep wisdom for soothing needs of the heart; they move in dry, special problems — object, concept, truth, proposition, fact, law — under the second volume's subtitle "Investigations in Phenomenology and the Theory of Knowledge," in six extensive studies whose connection is not immediately transparent (expression and meaning; the ideal unity of the species and abstraction-theories; the doctrine of wholes and parts; independent and dependent meanings and the idea of pure grammar; intentional experiences and their contents; elements of a phenomenological clarification of knowledge). The subtitle "theory of knowledge" was chosen only in deference to tradition; Husserl explicitly rejects epistemology in the usual sense, since strictly speaking epistemology is no theory at all but reflection and evident self-understanding of what thinking and knowing are according to their pure generic essence — and every "theory," as a deductive system for explaining given facts, still harbors a hidden naturalism. The manner of appropriation the work demands is likewise unusual: a continuous investigating course requiring, at each step, explicit intuitive presentification and controlling exhibition of what is treated. One cannot lift out results and build them into a system without perverting the whole sense of the investigations; phenomenological investigations cannot be reported in abridged form but must each time be repeated and run through anew. Heidegger therefore declines any summary of contents — which would itself be a misunderstanding — and turns instead to work out the fundamental discoveries directly, thereby preparing the very attitude in which the whole course will proceed.

### Chapter Two: The fundamental discoveries of phenomenology, its principle, and the clarification of its name

Heidegger names three discoveries he means to work through — intentionality, categorial intuition, and the genuine sense of the *apriori* — and insists these are not doctrines to report but structures to see; only by seeing them can time itself later come phenomenologically into view. He treats the three in an order that is itself argued for: the second rests on the first, the third on the second, so that intentionality is progressively shown in its fundamental import.

#### § 5. Intentionality

Heidegger takes intentionality first because it is the very phenomenon at which contemporary philosophy stumbles, the thing that blocks an unprejudiced reception of what phenomenology intends. He grants the historical facts the critics cite: Brentano recognized intentionality as the structure constituting the nature of a psychic phenomenon and made it the criterion dividing the psychic from the physical, taking himself merely to renew what Aristotle and scholasticism knew; through Brentano, Husserl learned to see it. So why speak of a *discovery* by phenomenology? Because there is a gulf between the crude acquaintance with a structure used for classification and the understanding of its ownmost sense and constitution — and the horizons of secure research that understanding opens. Heidegger flatly rejects the day-literature caricature (Rickert, Kraus) that Husserl simply took over a dark, metaphysical, scholastic concept, so that phenomenology is burdened with dogma and not grounded in immediate givenness. He rejects it not to save Husserl's originality against Brentano but to keep the most elementary steps toward understanding phenomenology from being foreclosed in advance. For the Marburg School too intentionality was the stone of stumbling that barred access.

**a) Intentionality as a structure of lived experience: exhibition and first clarification.** The thesis to be shown: intentionality is a structure of lived experiences *as such*, not a relation subsequently attached to experiences understood as psychic states. Heidegger warns that grasping it cannot succeed at a stroke; the maxim "to the things themselves" does not mean the things are seized without labor, but that reaching them is a laborious dismantling of the prejudices that bar them. *Intentio* means, literally, directing-oneself-toward: every experience, every psychic comportment directs itself at something — representing is representing *of* something, memory memory of something, judging judging *about* something, and likewise expecting, hoping, loving, hating. This looks trivial, unworthy of the name discovery; the task is to see what it means phenomenologically. What is required is not special acumen but the setting-aside of prejudice: plain seeing and holding-fast of the seen, without the curious question what to do with it. Heidegger stresses that objectivity toward the most self-evident is the hardest thing, because the human being has the element of its existence in the artificial, the already-talked-over.

He takes an everyday case: perceiving a chair he finds on entering the room and, because it is in his way, shoves aside — deliberately *not* a case of staring contemplation. Natural perception, as one lives in it moving about one's world, is mostly not a self-standing observing of things but is absorbed in concrete practical dealing with them; I do not perceive in order to perceive, but to orient myself, clear a path, work at something. A crude interpretation would run: inside me a psychic process runs off; to this psychic event "in consciousness" there corresponds "outside" a physical real thing, so that a relation arises between the reality of consciousness (the subject) and a reality outside it (the object). Since the perception can be a deception or hallucination — Heidegger's example, an automobile driving over the audience's heads — the real object can be *absent*; so, the objection runs, not every perception is a perception of something, and intentionality is no necessary mark of perception. And even where a physical object does correspond, that it does would be a dogmatic claim, since (the Cartesian premise) it is not settled that I ever get out beyond my consciousness to a reality at all. In using the concept of intentionality one thus supposedly smuggles in two presuppositions: the metaphysical one that the psychic reaches out of itself to a physical (forbidden since Descartes), and the assumption that a real object always corresponds to a psychic process (refuted by illusion and hallucination). This, Heidegger says, is what Rickert and others mean when they find metaphysical dogmas lodged in the concept.

But — the decisive turn — has this interpretation even brought intentionality into view? No; it hopelessly bars access to what is phenomenologically meant. Take the hallucination again. One says: the automobile is not really present, so there is no coordination of physical and psychic, only the psychic is given. But is the hallucination not, *by its very sense*, hallucination — a supposed perceiving *of an automobile*? Is this supposed perceiving, precisely as lacking any real relation to a real object, not as such a directing-toward a supposedly-perceived? The deception itself, as such, is a directing-toward, even when the real object is factually absent. So it is not that a perception first *becomes* intentional when a physical thing enters into relation with the psychic and ceases to be intentional when the real thing does not exist; rather the perception in itself, whether veridical or deceptive, *is* intentional. Intentionality is not a property added to perception in certain cases; perception is from the outset, as perception, intentional, entirely apart from whether the perceived is really present. The critics' whole apparatus mislocates the phenomenon.

**b) The basic constitution of intentionality.** To clarify it Heidegger returns to natural thing-perception and now looks not at the directing-itself but at the *toward-which* — not at the perceiving but at the perceived, and specifically the perceived *of this perception*. What is it? Without prejudice: the chair itself. I do not see "representations" of the chair, grasp no image, feel no sensations of it; I see it — itself. And describing it naturally, I recount the wholly determinate if trivial history in which the chair is daily present: the lecture-hall chair beside the lectern, somewhat damaged by use, badly painted at the factory. This perceived of natural perception Heidegger calls the *environmental thing* (Umweltding). I can dwell in the perception and describe the same chair otherwise — so heavy, this color, movable, it falls if dropped, splits under an axe, burns when lit — and here too I speak of the perceived itself, not of representations, yet of other determinacies: what is now said holds of any piece of wood, determines the chair not *as* chair but as *natural thing* (Naturding). Language marks the difference finely: I give roses, or flowers, but not "plants"; botany dissects not flowers but plants. Rose as flower is environmental thing, rose as plant natural thing; the perceived in itself is both. Heidegger presses a further point against the layering: when in natural speech I say the chair is hard, I do not register a degree of density in a material thing but say it is *uncomfortable* — hardness, material resistance, is *there* in the character of uncomfortableness, and only so, not first inferred from it. Certain structures belonging to the natural thing present themselves *first* in determinate environmental characters. Pressing further into the found natural thing, I can raise from it that materiality and extension belong to it as such a thing, that every extended thing is colored, every color spread out, the material displaceable — and now I speak neither of the chair as environmental thing nor as natural thing but of *thinghood as such* (Dinglichkeit), of structural moments read off the given itself.

In all three cases Heidegger dealt with the perceived being *in itself*. The standard objection — that this is naïve, since strictly I first see only colored patches, have only yellow-sensations, and add the rest — he meets by embracing naïveté: we want the pure naïveté that first and properly sees the lecture-hall chair. "Seeing" here is not optical sensing but simple taking-cognizance of what is found. So understood, one *sees* that the chair comes from a factory without drawing inferences or having a sensation of a factory. The field of what is simply findable is in principle far wider than any perception-theory would fix, and even the structural connections among general characters (materiality, extension, coloring, spread) can be seen — not by a mystical act but by simple presentification of structures read off the given.

**c) The perceived in its how: the perceivedness of the being, the character of bodily-there.** Still we are not at the perceived in the strict phenomenological sense. That is not the perceived being in itself but the perceived being *insofar as it is perceived* — the *how* in which it shows itself in concrete perception, the *perceivedness* (Wahrgenommenheit) of this chair, the manner and structure in which it is perceived, which differs from the manner in which it is merely represented. Of this new structure none of the earlier determinations of the chair can be predicated: being-perceived belongs not to the chair as chair (a stone or house can be perceived too) but to perceiving as such, to intentionality. So one distinguishes: the being itself (environmental thing, natural thing, thinghood) and the being in the manner of its being-intended — its being-perceived, -represented, -judged, -loved, -hated, -thought. What shows itself when we look to the perceivedness is *bodily presence* (Leibhaftigkeit): the being that presents itself as perceived has the character of being bodily-there, given not merely as itself but as itself *in its bodily presence*. Heidegger sharpens this by two contrasts. First, self-givenness versus bodily-givenness: presentifying the Weidenhausen bridge, I mean the bridge itself, not an image of it, yet it is not given to me *bodily* — that would require my going down and standing before it. What is self-given need not be bodily given, but every bodily-given is self-given; bodily presence is a distinguished mode of self-givenness. Second, self-givenness versus *empty meaning* (Leermeinen): in merely thinking of or recalling the bridge in conversation, I mean the bridge itself, without seeing it in its look — meant directly, but empty, without intuitive fulfillment. Much of our natural speech moves in this empty meaning: we mean the things themselves, not images, yet do not have them intuitively. This holds not only of sensory perception but across all acts: "1 + 2 = 2 + 1" can be said blindly yet understood, or carried out with insight, each step accomplished under presentification of the meant — the difference between blind and seeing accomplishment.

A further mode is *image-perception* (Bildwahrnehmung). Looking at a postcard of the bridge, what is bodily given is the postcard, itself a thing; but it is an *image-thing*, and perceiving it I see *through* it the depicted, the bridge — not thematically grasping the image-thing but, naturally set, seeing the depicted in it. Heidegger insists on the sharp structural difference from simple perception, because a persistent theory tries to explain object-perception *by* image-consciousness: as though in seeing this house I first perceive an inner image and then take it as depicting an outer house — inside a subjective image, outside the transcendent depicted. Nothing of the sort is findable; in simple perception I see the house itself. The theory not only explains nothing and courts an infinite regress (the inner image would need its own image-thing, and so on); the decisive objection is that it contradicts the plain phenomenological finding, being theory without phenomenology. Image-consciousness is itself possible only as a perceiving in which first the image-thing is grasped and only in it the depicted. Two further moments belong to perceivedness: the perceived thing is always meant in its *thing-wholeness*, though I see only one side or aspect at a time (I do not take the chair to have sawn-off legs because I see only the front); as I go round it the aspects change while the bodily selfsameness of the perceived holds through — the thing *adumbrates* itself (schattet sich ab) in its aspects, yet what is meant is never an adumbration but, in each adumbration, the perceived thing itself.

**d) First indication of the basic kind of intentionality as the mutual belonging of *intentio* and *intentum*.** These modes of representing are not simply side by side but have a structural connection: an empty meaning can fulfill itself intuitively in presentification, and presentification in turn can be filled by bodily givenness — as when a dispute over the number of arches sends us to the bridge itself. Perception is a distinguished case of intentional *fulfillment*: every intention bears in itself a tendency to fulfillment, and each has its own determinate mode of possible fulfillment (perception only through perception, memory never through expectation but through remembering presentification). The upshot Heidegger wants: the perceived shows itself in its perceivedness, the image-conscious in its imagedness, the presentified in the manner of presentifying, the empty-meant in the manner of empty-meaning — all these are different modes of the *being-intended* of their objects. Hence a peculiar belonging-together of the *how* of being-intended, the *intentio*, and the *intentum* — where *intentum* means, in the sense worked out, not the perceived *as a being* but the being in the *how* of its being-perceived. Only with the *how* of being-intended belonging to every *intentio* does the basic constitution of intentionality come, provisionally, into view. Heidegger notes Husserl's terms *noesis* and *noema*, from *noein* (to perceive, simply grasp), flagging that this terminology already carries an interpretation of the directing-toward drawn from *theoretical* knowing and read across onto the practical sphere — harmless for present purposes, useful for fixing that intentionality is fully determined only when seen as this belonging of *intentio* and *intentum*.

Against Brentano: he saw the *intentio*, the noesis, and the variety of its modes, but not the noema, the *intentum*; he stayed uncertain about what he called the "intentional object," wavering between the being itself and the how of its being-grasped, undivided from the being. The pure setting-off of the how of being-intended never occurs in him; intentionality as such, as a structural whole, is not lifted out — and so it gets identified with the psychic, taken as a character of the being (the psychic) whose structure it is. Worse, Brentano left undetermined *what* intentionality is supposed to be the structure *of*, because he took the psychic in the traditional Cartesian sense of the immanently perceivable, the immanently conscious. This, Heidegger says, is a defect not yet overcome even in present phenomenology: intentionality is still grasped as a structure of *consciousness* or of *acts*, of the *person*, with those realities themselves taken over traditionally. Husserl tries to get past the psychic conditioning by taking intentionality as a general structure of *reason* (reason not as psychic, marked off against the physical); Scheler by taking it as structure of *spirit* or *person*, again marked off against the physical. But with reason, spirit, *anima*, the very starting-point these theories work with is not overcome. Heidegger's methodological verdict: one may not refute phenomenological intentionality simply by criticizing Brentano — that surrenders the theme in advance. Not intentionality as such is metaphysically dogmatic, but what is built under the structure, or left unquestioned beneath it, through the traditional failure to ask what it is the structure *of*. The methodical rule for first grasping intentionality is precisely *not* to labor at interpretations but to hold fast only to what shows itself, however meager, so that through intentionality one may see, equally factually, what it is the structure of. Intentionality is not the final explanation of the psychic but the first move toward overcoming the uncritical positing of traditionally determined realities — psychic, consciousness, experience-nexus, reason. Because that task lies in this basic concept, the word must least of all become a phenomenological slogan; and one must say plainly that the mutual belonging of *intentum* to *intentio* remains dark, that how the being-intended of a being relates to that being itself stays enigmatic — and that these enigmas cannot even be interrogated so long as one covers over their enigmatic character with theory for or against intentionality. Understanding is advanced not by speculating about intentionality but by following it in its concretion — which is the occasion for the second discovery.

#### § 6. Categorial intuition

What is grasped here could be discovered only after intentionality had been laid free as a structure. "Intuition" means what was earlier called "seeing" in the full sense: simple grasping of the bodily-found itself, as it shows itself. Heidegger fixes two guards. It is not prejudged that sensory perception is the sole or most original intuiting — there may be further possibilities of intuition for other subject-fields. And nothing may be read into the term beyond the phenomenological usage: simple grasping of the bodily-given as it shows itself, no special faculty, no exceptional way of transposing oneself into otherwise closed depths of the world, nothing like Bergson's intuition. It is therefore cheap to characterize phenomenology as bound up with modern intuitionism; with that it has precisely nothing to do. The discovery of categorial intuition is the demonstration, first, that there is a *simple grasping of the categorial* — of those holdings in beings traditionally called categories — and second, above all, that this grasping is *invested in the most everyday perception and in every experience*.

**a) Intentional meaning and intentional fulfillment; identification, evidence, truth.** Heidegger prepares the demonstration through two general considerations, on intentional meaning and fulfillment, and on intentional comportments *as expressed* — since our comportments are throughout expressed experiences, if not in spoken words then in a determinate articulation carried by an understanding I have of them while simply living in them. To give a perception expression can mean either *making-known* (Kundgabe) the act — reporting that I am now perceiving, a possibility holding of any act — or communicating what is perceived *in* the act, the being itself. The perceptual assertion "this chair is yellow and upholstered" is of the second kind: it lifts determinate holdings from the at-first simply and unarticulately grasped thing, drawing them from the originarily given intuitive content; it has its exhibition as *coverage* (Deckung), it is true, and in its sense lies a tendency toward its own truth. The question: does the perceptual assertion find full fulfillment in the perceived? Is the whole "this S is p and q" perceptually exhibitable at the thing? The chair, the being-upholstered, the being-yellow I can see — but the "this," the "is," the "and" I shall never in all eternity see as I see the chair. There is a *surplus* of intentions in the full assertion whose fulfillment cannot be provided by simple thing-perception. Even in a mere nominal positing — "the yellow, upholstered chair" — a surplus remains: yellow (the color) I can see, but not the *being-yellow*, the being-colored; the attribute "yellow" fully spelled out means *the yellow-being chair*, and this "being" is not perceivable. Being is no real moment in the chair, as the wood or hardness are; Kant already said being (he meant real being) is no real predicate — and this holds of the "is" of the copula too. So there is no adequation between assertion and perceived: the assertion expresses what is perceptually not findable at all. Must the idea of an adequate fulfillment of assertions, and with it the idea of truth, be given up?

Not before we see what these unfulfilled elements — the "this," the "is," the "and" — actually are. The traditional move: color is sensory, real; being is nothing of the sort, hence non-sensory, non-real; and since the real counts as the objective, the non-sensory gets equated with the mental in the subject, the immanent — the real given from the object, the rest thought-in from the subject, which is given in *inner* perception. So the origin of these non-sensory moments (being, unity, plurality, "and," "or") is placed in immanent perception, in reflection on consciousness — the argument of English empiricism since Locke, rooted in Descartes, still in force in Kant and German idealism. That one can today attack idealism from just this side is possible only through phenomenology's demonstration that the non-sensory, the ideal, *cannot* be identified with the immanent, the conscious, the subjective. Heidegger states the positive core, quoting Husserl: the origin of the concepts *state-of-affairs* and *being* (in the sense of the copula) lies not in reflection on judgments or on judgment-fulfillments but *in the judgment-fulfillments themselves* — not in these acts as objects but in the *objects of these acts*. Being (category), "and," "or," "this," "one," "some," "then" are nothing conscious, nothing psychic, but *correlates of certain acts*. If I want the concept of an aggregate, I find the phenomenon not by reflecting on the psychic process of taking-together but by looking to what is meant *in* that comprehending; likewise the categorial of identity is found not in reflection on consciousness but in what is meant in that comportment. And — Heidegger's meta-point — the correction of the old prejudice (that "non-sensory, non-real" = immanent, subjective) hangs together with the discovery of intentionality itself: one does not know what one does if one decides for the correct grasp of the categorial while thinking one can dismiss intentionality as a mythical concept; the two are one and the same.

**Simple and founded acts.** "Totality," "and," "but" are not conscious, not psychic, but objectivity of a proper kind: they are given by acts that present something *at* the being itself, though not with the character of the real-sensory thing-object; not exhibitable by sensory perception, they are exhibited nonetheless in an essentially like manner — originary self-giving in the corresponding giving acts. As "totality," "number," "subject," "predicate," "state-of-affairs," "something" are objects, so the acts originarily exhibiting them must be grasped as *intuitions*, on the earlier sense of intuition. Heidegger then sharpens what "simple" means. Simple (sensory) perception does not deliver the fulfillment of all assertion-intentions, but the character of the "simple" was itself left undetermined. Its clarification opens the sense of founding: what makes perception simple is that each phase in a continuous perceptual series is *itself* a full perception of the thing — the whole thing is in each moment bodily itself, and itself as the same. The perceptual continuum is not stitched by a supervening synthesis; it is a single, as it were stretched-out perception, giving its object simply and immediately, one-leveled. *Simplicity* (Schlichtheit) means the absence of leveled, unity-founding acts — a character of the intentionality of grasping, which does *not* exclude that this perception is in the highest degree complicated in its act-structure. Simplicity of perception is not simplicity of act-structure; conversely the leveledness of categorial acts does not exclude their being simple. This one-leveledness lets Husserl define the *real* object as the possible object of a simple perception — for Husserl the most original sense of reality — and the real part as any part of a real object. This is a determinate concept of "real," correlative to simple perception, and it governs Husserl's whole analysis of the reality of the world. In simple grasping the thing-whole is given explicitly, its parts and moments implicitly — *there*, meaning liftable-out. And this simple perception, or what it gives, can become the *foundation* for acts that build on it, taking it as ground for new objectivities.

**c) Acts of synthesis.** In simply perceiving a being, that being is at first simply *there*, its real parts unarticulated but liftable. The lifting-out is accomplished in new acts of *explication*. Lifting the "yellow" as being *in* the chair first makes the yellow present as a moment not present in the simple perception; and lifting the yellow *as in S* co-lifts S as a whole containing it, and at bottom lifts the *relation* of yellow and S — so that the previously unarticulated thing becomes visible *through* an articulation Heidegger calls a *state-of-affairs* (Sachverhalt). Though founded on the perceived thing, the state-of-affairs is not a real part of it: the being-yellow of the chair is no real moment like the arms or the upholstery, but is *ideal in nature*. Lifting the state-of-affairs alters nothing in the given thing; nothing happens to the chair — yet it becomes, precisely in what it is, *explicitly* visible, more properly present, through this new objectivity. Two refinements. The order of steps (from part q to whole S to relation) does *not* represent the genuine accomplishment: the primary thing is not the grasping of q, then of S, then their assembly, so that the relation is built up from pre-given elements — rather *the relating itself is primary*, and only through it do the related terms become explicit. And the relation is graspable in two directions — "q is in S" and "S has q in itself" — a double directedness belonging to the sense of a state-of-affairs as such. The acts of lifting and giving the state-of-affairs are one original act-unity that brings the being in this new objectivity to givenness. Following Aristotle, the relating act can be understood as *synthesis* or *diairesis*, but these must be taken *intentionally*: synthesis is not the gluing-together of two separate things but is *object-giving* — synthesis and diairesis *give* objects; the decisive thing is that in this synthesis the q shows itself as belonging to S, the simple wholeness of S shows itself explicitly. The founded act of relating gives what simple perceiving can never grasp. Heidegger illustrates the ideal character with the case "a is brighter than b": the real relation *brighter-than* is already there in simple perception, but the *being-brighter-than* is accessible only in the founded predicative relating; the real relation is taken up into the whole of an ideal (state-of-affairs) relation, without thereby being itself thematically grasped — that happens only in a differently built assertion (this brightness-relation is more easily noticeable than that), where the brighter-than is *nominalized* and stands in the subject-position. Nominalization is the form in which we grasp a state-of-affairs thematically. The whole point: to call the state-of-affairs relation *ideal* (non-real) is not to call it non-objective or one whit less objective than the real; on the contrary, the objectivity of a being does not exhaust itself in reality thus narrowly defined — objectivity in the widest sense is far richer than the reality of a thing, and the reality of a thing is understandable in its structure only *out of* the full objectivity of the simply experienced being. Further synthetic acts are conjoining and disjoining, whose correlates are the "and" and the "or"; and the *figural* moment (the flock of birds, the avenue of trees seen as one closed whole without prior counting), which Husserl saw early in the mathematical investigations and which has since entered psychology as *Gestalt*.

**d) Acts of ideation.** These founded acts differ from the synthetic ones in that, while built on a founding objectivity, they precisely do *not* mean it. What ideation gives is the *idea*, *eidos*, *species* — the "look" of something, what one first and simply sees at the things. Moving through my environment I do not first see houses in their individuation but see, generally, "that is a house"; the general character is co-grasped in the simple intuition as what clarifies the pre-given. Ideation is the giving intuition that yields the species, the universal, from individuations; within many individual houses the species *house* is lifted out, from a set of individual reds *the* red. This lifting-out is a founded act, grounded in a pre-given grasp of individuation — but the objective it lets one newly see, the identical unity *red*, is *not* the individuation, this determinate red. The individual founds without being co-meant (unlike the conjunction, where the "and" co-means the terms): the founding objectivity is not taken up into the content of what ideation means. What one looks *toward* in comparing red balls for their sameness is the ideal unity of sameness as such, not the real object-samenesses of the balls; the ideal unity of the species is already there in every concrete grasp, though not explicitly as that toward which comparison looks. Four things follow for the object of ideating intuition: a *founding* individuation is necessary (an exemplary basis that gives something but is not itself meant); the range of concrete individuations is arbitrary; even the reference of the idea's content to its possible range is secondary; and the ideal unity of the species is one of *invariant* identity, findable in every concrete red as one and the same.

Provisionally, then, the difference between simple (sensory) intuition and founded (categorial) intuition is clear, and the full assertion "this S is p and q" fulfills itself neither in the sensory field alone nor in the categorial acts of "is" and "and" isolated: only a *founded* act — a sensory perception shot through with categorial acts — accomplishes the full intention. Concrete, explicitly object-giving intuition is never an isolated one-leveled sensory perception but always leveled, categorially determined intuition; and only this full, categorially determined perception is the possible fulfillment of the assertion that expresses it. If one seeks out the whole stock of object-giving acts and does not suppress the categorial acts as subjective additions and functions of a mythical understanding — merely because their correlates, as ideal being, are not found *in* the real, sensory — then even in the field of assertions the idea and possibility of adequate fulfillment holds.

**Warding off misunderstandings.** Because categorial intuition was won by leaning on traditional horizons and interpreted with traditional concepts, misunderstandings creep in easily — a sign that its own possibilities may not yet be exhausted. Heidegger issues the central corrective to the thesis "categorial acts are founded acts, all categorial rests ultimately on sensory intuition." This does *not* mean the categories can be interpreted as sensory; "rests" means *is founded*: every objective explication is not free-floating but is explication *of* the pre-given. This is only another formulation of the Aristotelian sentence that the soul never thinks without a phantasm — a thinking without founding sensibility is a countersense; the idea of a "pure intellect" could be conceived only *before* an elementary analysis of knowledge. Yet if the idea of a pure intellect is nonsensical, the concept of a pure categorial *act* has good sense: ideation rests on individual intuition but does not mean the intuited as such; acts that repel not only everything individual but everything sensory are *pure* categorial intuitions, as against the *mixed* (which still enclose sensory holdings). Here Heidegger widens the concept of "sensibility" decisively: sensibility is a *formal* phenomenological concept, the title for the total stock of beings pre-given in their material content, over against the categorial as the formal, objectively empty. Materiality-in-general, spatiality-in-general are sensory concepts, though the idea of spatiality contains nothing of sensation-data. One must therefore beware of imputing *sensualism* to phenomenology, as if only sense-data were in question. He then dismantles the mapping of the two intuitions onto the old opposition of sensibility and understanding, receptivity and spontaneity, stuff and form. Cast that way, the spontaneity of the understanding becomes a forming-principle working up a received stuff, and one has at a stroke the old mythology of an intellect that cobbles the world-stuff together with its forms — the mythology is the same whether metaphysical or (as in Rickert) epistemological. Categorial intuition falls prey to this only so long as one fails to see intentionality. The categorial *forms* are not products of the acts but *objects* that become visible *in* the acts, nothing made by the subject and still less anything brought *to* the real objects so as to modify them; they present the being more properly in its *being-in-itself*. "Constitute" is to be taken intentionally: categorial acts do not let things first arise somewhere; *constituting is not producing or making but letting-be-seen of the being in its objectivity*. This objectivity is not the result of the understanding's activity on a prior jumble of affections out of which a world-picture is built; the old stuff/form vocabulary, emptied and worn, merely abets that misreading.

**The significance of the discovery.** The decisive thing: there are acts in which ideal holdings show themselves *at* themselves, which are not products of these acts, not functions of thought, of the subject. Further, the possibility of this intuiting and of what presents itself in it gives the ground for lifting the structures of these ideal objects — for the working-out of the categories. So with categorial intuition the concrete path of an exhibiting, genuine *research into the categories* is won for the first time. In the narrower sense it gave a real understanding of abstraction (ideation), of the grasping of the idea, and thereby provisionally settled the old quarrel over the being of universals — since Boethius, whether they are a *res* or a mere *flatus vocis* — a quarrel in which one had, with the justified denial of the reality of universals (reality in the sense of a chair's), wrongly denied the *objectivity* of the universal too, barring the way to the being of the ideal. Categorial intuition broke this spell and set philosophy in a position to grasp the *apriori* more sharply. And the objectivity given in such acts is itself the objective manner in which *reality* can become more properly objective: with the exhibition of categorial structure the idea of objectivity is widened, and now itself exhibitable in its content. Heidegger draws the consequence that most matters for the whole course: in the phenomenological research breaking through here, the manner of research the *old ontology sought* is won — there is no ontology alongside a phenomenology; **scientific ontology is nothing other than phenomenology**. This identification is the bridge from Husserl to the question of being: because being and the categories are *given* in categorial intuition, ontology can be phenomenological inquiry into the given rather than deduction or construction.

#### § 7. The original sense of the *apriori*

The third discovery Heidegger keeps short, for three reasons: the *apriori* is still little clarified even in phenomenology; it remains largely tied to traditional questions; and clarifying its sense presupposes the very thing the course seeks — *time*. The last shows already in the name: *a priori — prius — proteron —* the earlier; *a priori* is what, in something, is already the earlier in it. That is a purely formal determination: a title in which lies something like a *temporal* sequence, though a very faded, indeterminate, empty one. Heidegger sets aside the historical motives (beginning with Plato) and asks only what the *apriori* has meant and means in phenomenology.

Since Kant, and in substance since Descartes, the title is ascribed first and mostly to *knowing*: an apriori cognition is one not grounded in empirical, inductive experience, not dependent on knowledge of the real, hence "experience-independent." On Descartes' interpretation of knowing, apriori cognition is what is available first and solely *in the subject as such*, insofar as it stays closed within its own sphere; so in every knowledge of the real — every transcendent knowledge — apriori knowledge is always already enclosed. Its counterpart, the *a posteriori*, is knowledge of the object, coming *after* the prior, purely subjective knowing. Underlying this partition is the thesis of the *primacy of the knowledge of subjectivity*, as Descartes grounded it with the *cogito sum*, the *res cogitans*; hence one still calls the *apriori* a character specifically belonging to the subjective sphere, apriori knowledge "inner" knowledge, inner intuition — and can even widen it to *all* subjective comportment as such, prior to any crossing of the boundary of its immanence. This Kantian sense is then read back into Plato (the soul speaking to itself, *Sophist* 263e), identifying *psychē* with consciousness and subject, so that even in Plato, the discoverer of the *apriori*, apriori knowledge is supposedly immanent knowledge. This Plato-interpretation, Heidegger says flatly, is countersensical and has no foundation in the matters. The entanglement of *apriori* with subjectivity became especially stubborn in Kant because he bound the question to his specifically epistemological problem, asking of the synthetic *a priori* judgments whether and how they have *transcendent* validity.

Against this, phenomenology showed that the *apriori* is not restricted to subjectivity — indeed has, primarily, *nothing to do* with subjectivity. The account of ideation as a categorial intuition made clear that there is a lifting-out of ideas in the field of the ideal (the categories) *and* in the field of the real: there are *sensory* ideas, of material structure (color, materiality, spatiality), already there in every real individuation and so *a priori* with respect to the here-and-now of a determinate thing-coloring; the whole of geometry is the proof of a *material apriori*. In the ideal and in the real alike there is, as regards objectivity, a liftable ideal, something in the being of the ideal and of the real that is *a priori*, structurally earlier. This already indicates that the *apriori*, phenomenologically understood, is no title of *comportment* but a title of *being*: it is neither immanent, belonging primarily to the subject-sphere, nor transcendent, specifically attached to reality. Three results, then: the *apriori*'s universal reach; its specific *indifference* toward subjectivity; and — implied — the manner of *access* to it. Because the *apriori* is grounded in the several subject- and being-domains, it is exhibitable *at itself* in a simple intuition; it is not indirectly inferred, conjectured from signs in the real, hypothetically calculated (as one infers unseen bodies from movement-disturbances). It is countersensical to transpose that procedure, which has sense in the physical, onto philosophy and assume a stratification of "bodies"; the *apriori* is rather *directly* graspable at itself. A fourth determination prepares: the "earlier" is no character in the order of *knowing*, nor in the succession of the *arising* of beings from beings, but a character of the *build-up-order in the being of beings*, in the being-structure of being. Formally the *apriori* prejudges nothing — whether this earlier concerns a knowing or a being-known or some other comportment, whether it concerns a being or being, and least of all whether it means being in the inherited Greek sense. Toward the course's end it will become evident that the discovery of the *apriori* hangs together with — is really *identical* with — the discovery of the concept of being in Parmenides and Plato, and that, owing to the dominance of that determinate concept of being *within phenomenology itself*, the *apriori* stands in *its* horizon — so that one has with some right spoken of a *Platonism* within phenomenology. This last remark seeds the eventual critique: phenomenology inherits, unexamined, the Greek sense of being, and time will be needed to loosen it.

#### § 8. The principle of phenomenology

The principle of a research is the principle of the *researching comportment* — of appropriating and accomplishing the idea of that research: the principle of winning the subject-field, of creating the aspect under which the matter is investigated, of forming the manner of treatment. It contains no result, thesis, or dogma, only the guidance of the seeking. Such a principle, insofar as it determines the accomplishment of a possibility of Dasein's existence, is also a *maxim* — and science, research-after, is nothing else, in its being-sense, than a determinate possibility of human Dasein. The phenomenological maxim is "*to the things themselves*," spoken against construction and free-floating questioning in traditional, groundless concepts. That so self-evident a maxim had to be made a battle-cry against free-floating thought characterizes the situation of philosophy. Heidegger hears in it a *double* demand: to research in a grounded, exhibiting way (the demand of exhibiting work), and *first to win and secure* this ground (the demand of laying the field free) — the second being the fundamental one, in which the first is contained.

He refuses to determine the field *from an idea of philosophy*, and instead reads the field off the *concretion* of the three discoveries: what matters are they seized? Intentionality is the basic field in which the objects of logic (meaning, concept, assertion, proposition, judgment, state-of-affairs, objectivity, fact, law, being) are findable — the totality of comportments and the totality of beings in their being. In both directions, *intentio* and *intentum*, one asks what the pre-given is *structurally* — what already lies in it as structural stock, what makes up its being. So the field of phenomenological research is *intentionality in its apriori*, taken in both directions. From which it follows that the so-called logical comportments of thinking, of objectifying theoretical knowing, make up only a *narrow* sphere within intentionality; the task-circle of logic in no way exhausts the region. That fixes the field and the aspect — intentionality and *apriori*. The second question is the manner of treatment. As the *apriori* and categorial intuition already showed, this is a simple originary grasping, not experimenting substruction; the whole content of the *apriori* of intentionality is graspable in simple measuring-up to the thing itself. Such directly-seeing grasping and lifting is traditionally called *description*; more exactly, description is a lifting-out articulation of the self-intuited, and lifting-out articulation is *analysis* — so the treatment is *analytic description*. Heidegger warns that "description" says nothing yet about the closer structure of phenomenological research: the character of description determines itself only from the content of what is to be described, so that description and description can be fundamentally different in different cases; the title means, for now, only direct self-grasping of the thematic, not indirect substruction. The clarification thus yields the formula: **phenomenology is analytic description of intentionality in its apriori.**

**b) The self-understanding of phenomenology as analytic description of intentionality in its apriori.** If one hears in "intentionality" what the tradition binds to it — intentionality and the *psychic* — then phenomenology becomes description of the psychic, "descriptive psychology," and, taking over the traditional partition into fixed disciplines (logic, ethics, aesthetics), a descriptive psychology of the logical, moral, artistic, social, religious comportments — a mere *pre-science* for the traditional philosophical disciplines in which the "real" problems are discussed, itself excluded from the trial of the problems. Heidegger rejects this self-misunderstanding: it relocates the research and the originality of its principle back into just what it had thrown off, what it is meant to *overcome* — as if one interpreted modern physics out of astrology, or chemistry out of alchemy, instead of characterizing astrology, from physics, as a surpassed preliminary stage. The definition won as clarification of the principle is to be understood from what lies in it as *task*, as positive possibility, from what leads its work — not from what is said about it. With the understanding of the phenomenological task we stand not at a happy end but at a meager yet liberating *beginning*: made explicit phenomenologically, the formula "analytic description of intentionality in its apriori" itself points, by its own maxim, toward a more radical grasp of this research out of itself — again only by way of advancing work. Heidegger closes with a striking dictum: phenomenology, so long as it understands itself, will keep this investigating course against all prophecy within philosophy and all tendency toward life-guidance. **Philosophical research is and remains atheism**, and precisely therefore can permit itself the "presumption of thinking"; in this atheism it becomes what a great one once called the *gay science*. (The point is methodological, not confessional: phenomenology brackets any appeal to a securing highest being and answers to nothing but the exhibitable matters.)

#### § 9. The clarification of the name "phenomenology"

Heidegger now asks what the name means with respect to the characterized matter, in three steps: clarifying the original sense of the name's components; determining the meaning-unity of the compound word and measuring it against the characterized research; and briefly treating misunderstandings that attach to an external interpretation of the name. "-logy," as in theology, biology, sociology, is commonly rendered "science of"; phenomenology correspondingly the science of phenomena — but "-logy" is formally indeterminate and here gets its character from what "phenomenon" says. So the first component is clarified first.

**The original sense of *phainomenon*.** *Phainomenon* is the participle of *phainesthai*, whose middle sense is *to show itself*; *phainomenon* is thus *what shows itself*. The middle is formed from *phainō*, to bring to the day, to make visible at itself, to place in the light — the stem *pha-* is *phōs*, light, the brightness in which something can be manifest, visible at itself. Fix the meaning: *phainomenon*, what shows itself; the *phainomena* are the totality of what shows itself, which the Greeks also simply identified with *ta onta*, beings. Now a being can show itself, from itself, in different ways according to the mode of access — and there is the remarkable possibility that a being shows itself *as* something it is not. Such a being we call not phenomenon in the proper sense but *semblance* (Schein). Hence the meaning-modification: over against the *agathon* one speaks of a *phainomenon agathon*, a good that only looks so but is not. Everything depends on seeing the connection between the ground-meaning (the manifest, the self-showing) and the second (semblance). *Phainomenon* can mean semblance *only because* semblance is a modification of *phainomenon* in the first sense: only because *phainesthai* means to show itself can it also mean to show itself merely as, to look merely like. Only what by its sense pretends to show itself can give itself out as something; only what pretends to be manifest can be semblance — indeed that *is* the sense of semblance: pretension to the manifest, and precisely not being it. So semblance itself shows that phenomenon means *the being manifest at itself, itself*; semblance is an *alleged* self-showing. Phenomenon means a mode of *encounter* of the being at itself: to show itself.

This genuine Greek sense has, Heidegger insists, nothing to do with our "appearance" (Erscheinung), let alone "mere appearance" — no word has wrought such devastation in philosophy. He marks the difference. We use "appearances" of, e.g., *symptoms* of illness: occurrences at a thing that point back to other occurrences, from which what does *not* come into appearance can be inferred; appearances are appearances *of* something not itself given as appearance, referring to another being. Appearing has the character of *reference* (Verweisung), and reference is distinguished precisely in that what the appearance refers to does *not* show itself at itself but only presents itself, mediately indicates, indirectly announces itself — indexing, symbolically presenting. So "appearance" means a kind of reference of something to something that does not show itself at itself and, by its sense, does not even *pretend* to. With the phenomenon there is precisely *no* reference-nexus but the peculiar structure of *self-showing*. And now the founding relation: semblance is a modification of the manifest (the manifest it pretends to be but is not); appearance is the presentation of what is essentially *not* manifest. Yet an appearance, a symptom, can be what it is — reference to something non-showing — *only because* the appearing thing itself *shows itself*, i.e. because what gives itself as symptom is a *phenomenon*. The possibility of appearance as reference is founded in the genuine phenomenon, in self-showing; something can be referential only as self-showing. Confusion mounts when "appearance" is taken *ontically*: two beings, appearance and the thing-in-itself behind it, the "behind" the properly real, so that the appearing gets stamped as *mere* appearance and a difference of *being-degree* is made between what shows itself and what merely appears; take this degraded being, mere appearance, and call it semblance, and the confusion is complete — the confusion on which traditional epistemology and metaphysics live. Summarizing: two ground-meanings of "phenomenon" — the manifest, self-showing; and what presents itself as manifest but gives itself only so, semblance. Mostly one does not even know the original meaning and simply calls "phenomenon" what is here called "appearance"; then, criticizing phenomenology, one takes the concept "appearance" that suits one and criticizes a substantive research with that word.

**The original sense of *logos* (*logos apophantikos* and *logos sēmantikos*).** *Logos* goes back to *legein*. In "theology," "biology," "-logy" means "science of," science understood as a nexus of propositions about the unity of a subject-field. But *logos* does not properly mean science; going back to *legein* it means *discourse*, discourse *of* something. And one may not fix its sense arbitrarily but must hold to what the Greeks understood by *legein* — which is not making and reciting words but *dēloun*, making manifest: manifesting what the discourse is about and how it is to be discoursed of. Aristotle determines the sense of *logos* more precisely as *apophainesthai* — to *let be seen* something at itself and, *apo-*, from itself. In discourse, if genuine, what is said is to be drawn *from* what is discoursed about, so that the communicating discourse makes manifest in its content what it says and what it is about. That is the strict, functional sense of *logos*. In concrete accomplishment discourse has the character of *speaking*, vocal utterance in words; in this respect *logos* is *phōnē*, voice — but this does not make up the essence of *logos*; conversely the character of the voice determines itself from the proper sense of *logos* as *apophainesthai*, as pointing-out, letting-see. *Logos* is, as Aristotle stresses, *phōnē meta phantasias* — vocal utterance with which a making-visible, a making-perceivable is given, something one can see. The essential thing in the utterance is the *phantasia*, the *apophainesthai*, what is discoursed and meant as spoken. Hence *logos* is quite generally a *phōnē sēmantikē*, a vocal something that shows in the manner of *meaning*, that gives something intelligible — but not every *logos* that means (*sēmantikos*) is *apophantikos*: an exclamation, a request, a wish, a prayer means something without communicating something in the sense of theoretical grasping. It is in the sense of the special *sēmantikos* *logos*, namely the *apophantikos*, that "-logy" is to be taken in "theology," "biology," and so in "phenomeno-logy": *logos* as *theōrein*, discourse in the sense of matter-grasping and only matter-grasping communication.

**b) The meaning-unity so won, and the corresponding research.** Taking the two clarified components together yields something surprising: the sense of *logos* as *apophainesthai* has *in itself* a substantive relation to *phainomenon*. Phenomenology is *legein ta phainomena = apophainesthai ta phainomena* — to let the self-manifest at itself be seen from itself. The maxim "to the things themselves" gives, at bottom, nothing but the name of phenomenology back again. This means the title differs essentially from other science-titles: it says nothing about the *material content* of its thematic object but names only — emphatically — the *how*, the manner in which something is and is to be thematic in this research. Phenomenology is a *methodological* title, a designation of the manner of experiencing, grasping, and determining what is thematic in philosophy. The objects of philosophical research have the character of *phenomenon*; the research treats phenomena and only such. "Phenomenon," in the original sense held fast in the name, means a mode of encounter — the distinguished one, showing-itself-at-itself; the term names the manner in which something is to be *there* for and through the *legein*, for conceptual interpretation. Since phenomenology treats intentionality in its *apriori*, the structures of intentionality in its *apriori* *are* the phenomena: they delimit the objects to be made present at themselves and explicated in that presence. "Phenomenon" says nothing about the *being* of these objects, only their *mode of encounter*; "phenomenal" is whatever becomes visible in this mode and belongs to the structural nexus of intentionality; "phenomenological" whatever belongs to the manner of such exhibition; "unphenomenological" whatever fails this manner, its conceptuality and method of exhibition.

Phenomenology, as science of the apriori phenomena of intentionality, therefore has *never* to do with appearances, let alone mere appearances. It is phenomenologically countersensical to speak of the phenomenon as things behind which something else would stand, of which they would be phenomena in the sense of presenting appearances. *Phenomenon is nothing behind which something else stands*; more exactly, of the phenomenon one cannot ask after a "behind" at all, because what it gives is precisely the something *at itself*. But what is exhibitable at itself *can be covered over* (verdeckt): what is visible at itself and accessible in its sense as phenomenon need not factually *be* so already; what is phenomenon in *possibility* is precisely *not* given as phenomenon but first *to be given*. Phenomenology is, as research, the *work of the freeing letting-be-seen*, in the sense of the methodically guided *dismantling of the coverings*. Coveredness is the counter-concept to phenomenon, and the coverings are the nearest theme of phenomenological consideration: what can be phenomenon is first and mostly covered, or known only in a preliminary determination. The covering can be of different kinds — a phenomenon may be simply *undiscovered* (no knowledge of it at all); or *buried over* (verschüttet): once discovered, then fallen back into a covering that is not total, so that the earlier-discovered is still visible, but only as semblance — and this *distortion* (Verstellung) is the most frequent and most dangerous kind, because here the possibilities of deception are greatest, the originally seen phenomena uprooted, torn from their ground, their substantive provenance unmade. And the covering, whether as the undiscovered or as burial and distortion, has again a double sense: *accidental* coverings and *necessary* ones, given in the very manner of being of discovering and its possibilities. Every originally drawn phenomenological proposition stands, as a communicated assertion, in the possibility of covering: passed on in an empty fore-understanding, it loses its groundedness and becomes free-floating naming. This possibility of *petrifaction* of the originally won lies in the concrete work of phenomenology itself; the covering proceeds *from it*, because it bears this radical principle in itself — with the possibility of radical discovering it harbors the answering danger of hardening in its own results. The difficulty of genuine phenomenological work lies precisely in making it critical *against itself* in a positive sense. The mode of encounter as phenomenon must first be *won* for the objects of research — which means the characteristic grasping, originarily-grasping interpretation, is itself a labor against covering. This closing point turns the whole method reflexively: phenomenology's danger is its own success, the freezing of live seeing into transmitted dogma, and its discipline is the perpetual dismantling of the coverings it itself lays down — the very stance from which Heidegger will proceed to criticize Husserl in Chapter Three.

### Chapter Three: The first formation of phenomenological research and the necessity of a radical reflection in it and out of it

Chapter Three turns phenomenology's own principle against its first systematic self-formation in Husserl's *Ideen I*. The reflection is *immanent*: Heidegger does not import an external standard but asks whether phenomenology, in laying out its own field, kept faith with "to the things themselves." The charge is that it did not — and the way this failure is grounded (in Dasein's falling, §13f) is what turns the critique of Husserl into the positive program of the Dasein-analytic that follows.

#### § 10. The working-out of the thematic field

Heidegger reconstructs how Husserl wins the field of pure consciousness. Intentionality was already seen to determine the experience-region: in experiences the transcendent world is in a certain way *there*, but — a guard against narrowing — this being-objective of the world is not the same as being-*grasped*. Grasping is one act-mode among others, not the predominant one; in loving I live "in" the beloved without the beloved being an object of grasping. Directing-oneself-toward is thus wider than grasping. When I reflect on an act — say a thing-perception — I am thematically directed at the *perceiving*, not the perceived; I can make the perception thematic so that its perceived is co-grasped, but then I live not directly in perceiving the chair but in the immanent reflective grasp of the chair-perception. This not-going-along-with the thesis of the material world is the *epochē*, the holding-back. Every phenomenological act-analysis treats the act without going along with it, without following its thematic sense, making the act itself thematic and thereby getting the act's object *in the how of its being-meant* into the theme. The perceived is meant not as such but in the how of its being. This modification is *bracketing* (Einklammerung).

Heidegger fixes the decisive corrective at once: bracketing takes nothing away from the being, does not assume the being is not; the switch of the gaze serves precisely to make the *being-character* of the being present. "Switching-off" is always misleading if one thinks that in switching off the existence-thesis phenomenological consideration has *given up* dealing with the being — the reverse: now, extremely and solely, it is a matter of determining the *being* of the being itself. The *epochē* can be carried out for all conscious comportments; winning the act-sphere and its objects in the unity of a determinate sphere is *reduction*. This first reduction (not going along with any transcendent thesis) is the "transcendental" reduction; its reduced field is still the field of a *unique* singularity, my own stream of consciousness. A second reduction, the *eidetic*, then studies acts and act-objects not as concrete individuations of *my* stream but ideatively, switching off every moment that determines this stream as individual, seeing only the structure belonging to a perception, representation, judgment *as such*. Through this double reduction the *pure* field of consciousness is lifted from the individual stream.

The stream of experience, Heidegger notes with Husserl, is a being-region of *absolute position*: every transcendent perception grasps its perceived bodily, but the perceived can always *not be*, whereas in immanent experience-grasp the experience is given in its absolute self — such a being's existence is in principle not deniable ("everything bodily given as thing can also not be; no bodily given experience can also not be"). So the sphere of immanence is distinguished by absolute givenness; the pure consciousness won through the two reductions is characterized by absolute-givenness, and the contingency of the thing-world changes nothing in the absolute being of experiences, which are presupposed to all of that. Heidegger flags the kinship to Descartes: what phenomenology works out as pure consciousness is the field Descartes had before him as *res cogitans*, the whole field of *cogitationes*, over against which the transcendent world is his *res externa* — and Husserl, at the analysis's high point, *expressly* invokes Descartes, saying that what Descartes sought in the *Meditations*, by a different method and aim, only now comes to fruition. Heidegger stores this admission for the critique: the whole layout is Cartesian. He ends with the aporia that drives the reduction: how can this sphere of absolute position, separated from all transcendence by an absolute gulf, at the same time unite with reality in one real human being who occurs *as* a real object in the world? How can experiences make up an absolute pure being-region and at the same time occur in the transcendence of the world?

#### § 11. Immanent critique: the four determinations of pure consciousness

The question Heidegger presses: in working out intentionality as its field, did phenomenology *ask after the being* of that region, the being of consciousness — and what does "being" even mean when consciousness is called a region of *absolute* being and the transcendent world a region of *reality*? Is the aspect from which the two being-spheres are divided — the *sense of being* — itself clarified? Is the methodical ground even won within phenomenology to pose the question of the sense of being that every phenomenological consideration presupposes and leaves unspoken? He leaves open for now whether this is a fundamental question, but insists that if it is necessary, the present position is phenomenologically insufficient. He then examines the four being-determinations Husserl gives pure consciousness, which are peculiarly interlocked (the same word often does duty for two): consciousness is *immanent* being; the immanent is the *absolutely given* being (also called absolute being *simpliciter*); this being is absolute in the further sense that it *needs no other thing in order to exist* (*nulla re indiget ad existendum* — the old definition of substance), where "thing" (*res*) means reality, the transcendent, everything that is not consciousness; and the absolute being in these senses is *pure* being, the essential being of experiences, their ideal being. The test throughout: are these determinations drawn from the matter itself, from consciousness and the being meant by that title?

**a) Consciousness is immanent being.** "Immanence" means formally: being-in-another. It is said of the experience-region with respect to the grasping acts, the acts of *reflection*, which direct themselves at experiences. Immanence names a *relation possible between experiences themselves* — between the reflecting act and the reflected — a relation of real mutual enclosure. It is asserted of experiences insofar as they are possible objects of an experience *by reflection*. So immanence is no determination of the being *at itself* in respect of its being, but a relation of two beings within the region "experience." A being-relation *of* beings is determined, not being *as such*. The first determination thus fails as an original one.

**b) Consciousness is absolute being in the sense of absolute givenness.** The reflected experience is at itself originarily given; experiences, over against the transcendent, are there in an absolute sense — they do not present themselves indirectly, symbolically, but are grasped at themselves, and on this ground are called absolute. But again this characterizes the experience-region in respect of its *being-grasped*, and is built on the first: it concerns not the regional belonging-together of grasped and grasping but the relation of one experience *as object for another*. Where immanence marked a being-relation between acts of the same region, absolute givenness marks the determinate manner in which a being of the region "experience" is object for another. Once more it is not the being *at itself* that becomes theme, but the being insofar as it is possible *object of reflection*.

**c) Consciousness is absolute being in the sense of *nulla re indiget ad existendum*.** The third sense of "absolute" is clarified against the first: while all experiences are immanently given, all *other* being is such as *announces itself in* consciousness. In principle the stream of experiences could form "a self-enclosed being-nexus" with a certain unambiguity even if nothing *really* corresponded to what is meant in it; in principle a "annihilation of the thing-world" would not touch consciousness "in its own existence" — a consideration, Heidegger notes, that Descartes already made. Real being can be otherwise or not be at all, yet consciousness can present a closed being-nexus in itself. This means: consciousness is absolute in that it is the *being-presupposition* on the ground of which reality can announce itself at all; transcendent being is always given *in presentation*, presenting itself precisely as object of intentionality. Consciousness is that in which every possible other being *constitutes* itself, in which it properly "is" what it is; the *constituting* being is absolute, every other being only in relation to it. Heidegger quotes Husserl: the common sense of being-talk reverses — the being first *for us* is in itself second, being what it is only in "relation" to the first. This first, which must already be there for the real to announce itself, has the privilege of not needing reality, reality needing it. So "absolute" is now won with a view to consciousness's role as *constituting*, seen in the horizon of a *theory of reason* — the question of the possible exhibition of reality in rational consciousness. Absolute being here means: not being dependent on another, with respect to *constitution*; consciousness is the earlier, the *apriori* in the Cartesian and Kantian sense — the primacy of subjectivity over all objectivity. This is no determination of the being *at itself* either, but one that grasps the consciousness-region within the *order of constitution* and grants it a formal being-earlier before everything objective. And Heidegger marks the point of infection: this is where *idealism* — more exactly neo-Kantian idealism — breaks into phenomenology.

**d) Consciousness is pure being.** The fourth is even less a being-characterization of the intentional than the other three. Consciousness is called *pure* insofar as it is considered no longer in its concrete individuation, its attachment to a living being, but purely according to its essential content — not the individuation of a concrete intentional relation but the intentional *structure as such*, not the concretion of experiences but their essential structure, not real experience-being but the ideal essential-being of consciousness, the *apriori* of experiences as the generic universal determining a class or structural nexus of experience. Consciousness is pure insofar as in it every reality and realization is precisely disregarded; it is pure because determined as *ideal*, non-real being. Here it becomes most evident that the issue is not the being-characters of the *intentional* but the being-determination of *intentionality* — not of the being that *has* the structure but of the structure itself, taken as detached in itself.

All four being-determinations — immanent, absolutely-given, absolute-in-constitution, pure — are in no way drawn from the being itself; insofar as they are set up *as being-determinations of consciousness*, they are precisely suited to *bar* the way to a question after the being of this being. They are won not with a view to the intentional in its being but insofar as it is put in view as *grasped, given, constituting, ideated as essence* — aspects at first *foreign* to consciousness. Heidegger cautions against too quick an inference: from the failure to determine the being of consciousness one may not straightway derive a neglect of the question of being *as such* — perhaps only the *region-being* of consciousness, its being a field for a determinate consideration, is meant to be determined, not the being of the being itself. Indeed all four are won with a view to working out the experience-nexus as a region of *absolute scientific consideration*. Husserl's primary question, Heidegger diagnoses, is not after the being-character of consciousness at all; it is led by the idea of an *absolute science* — an idea not invented but occupying modern philosophy since Descartes. The pure consciousness is won not phenomenologically, by return to the things themselves, but by return to a *traditional idea of philosophy*; hence none of the being-characters of experiences is original.

#### § 12. Exhibiting the neglect of the question of the being of the intentional

This is only the first station of the critique; the second asks whether the *way* pure consciousness is worked out — or the *starting point* of the whole consideration (the natural attitude, "the being as it first gives itself") — yields a genuine being-determination after all, and with it a fore-glimpse of the being of the concrete being called *the human*. The reduction, Heidegger observes, precisely *disregards* the reality of the intentional: it switches off the real experience as real to win the pure, absolute (*epochē*), disregarding the reality of consciousness given in the natural attitude at the factual human being. So by its methodical sense the reduction *gives out of hand* the very ground on which alone one could ask after the being of consciousness — a striking result, since Husserl might reply that the reduction's sense is precisely to disregard reality in order then to determine reality itself as it constitutes itself in pure consciousness. Heidegger takes the reply seriously: through the working-out of the ways the various regions of beings *constitute themselves* in consciousness, their respective reality and objectivity are to be determined; the real announces itself in consciousness as possible object of a directing-toward, and constitution-research is to lay the basis for determining the reality of a real. But — the objection stands — the being, the reality of the intentional itself, is determined only as a *possible object* for constitutive regard; even the actuality of the intentional is grasped as reality, as an intentional unity, "scientifically to be determined." What all constitution-analysis presupposes is that the sense of being at issue is being *as objectivity for a theoretical, scientific knowing*, being as *true being* fixable as an objective unity. The reduction never asks after the specific being of the acts; it determines the being of the acts in advance, *theoretically-dogmatically*, as being in the sense of *reality* — the reality of a natural process, an object present-at-hand in the world. So the starting point too, the "natural attitude," is not a genuinely natural experience in which the human being would give itself in its original sense, but a *theoretical* experience that gives the human as *zōon*, a natural object occurring in the world. Heidegger's question: is it the natural mode of experiencing oneself and others to experience oneself as a *living being*, a nature-object? The natural experience of the human may not be equated with this. Even where reality of the acts is asked after, the *specific* being of the intentional is not; the being of the acts is fixed in advance as reality.

#### § 13. Exhibiting the neglect of the question of the sense of being itself and of the being of the human

Why ask after being at all — does it not suffice to determine the *what* and its varieties? Heidegger answers that the "what for" is no primary criterion in knowledge, and that Husserl's own exposition of the field aims precisely to win the *fundamental distinction within beings* (being as consciousness / being as transcendent, reality) — that is, to answer the question of being. He quotes Husserl: the doctrine of categories must start from this most radical of all being-distinctions, won in its purity only by the phenomenological reduction. But then the remarkable thing: one claims to win the most radical *being*-distinction *without asking after the being of the beings that enter the distinction* — without asking after the being of consciousness, and above all without clarifying, or even asking after, *what directs the whole distinction*, the *sense of being*. Between being as consciousness and being as reality, Husserl says, "yawns a true abyss of sense" — yet the sense is never determined. From this it is clear that the question of being is no arbitrary, merely-possible question but the *most pressing* one in phenomenology's own ownmost sense. Two neglects, then: the question of the being of this specific being, the *acts*, and the question of the *sense of being* as such.

How is it possible that a research whose principle is "to the things themselves" leaves the fundamental consideration of its ownmost matter undone — that phenomenology is so *unphenomenological* as to exclude its own field from the phenomenological question? Before closing, Heidegger, in fairness, seeks out every approach that points toward determining the being of the intentional *from itself* — above all the necessary demarcation of phenomenology against psychology, which must handle the being of the acts.

**a) The demarcation against naturalistic psychology.** At its breakthrough phenomenology understood itself as a fight against naturalism in the special form of *psychologism* in logic. Against the confusion of the laws of thought with laws of psychic thinking-processes, Husserl (like Brentano) showed that the laws of thought are laws of the *thought*, distinguishing the real content of the acts (judging as such) from the ideal content (the judgment-content). In this distinction of real accomplishment and ideal content the rejection of psychologism is grounded, and so phenomenology was from the start secured against the naturalistic misunderstanding. But at just this demarcation one sees that, while real and ideal being of the judgment are distinguished, the *reality of this real of the acts* is left undetermined; the being of the judgment, the being of the intentional, stays unquestioned, so the possibility remains of grasping this reality as a psychic natural process. The rediscovery of the *ideal* fascinated, and the acts and processes were handed over to psychology.

**b) Dilthey's attempt at a personalistic psychology.** This critique of naturalism was open to Dilthey's tendencies, and Husserl took up their impulse — to carry out, phenomenologically, Dilthey's task of a *personalistic* over against a naturalistic psychology, understanding the psychic not as natural event but as *spirit and person*. Dilthey's lifelong drive was toward a science of the human that grasps the human primarily as *person*, as acting person in history, describing and articulating it rather than explaining it from general laws — a descriptive, articulating psychology against the explanatory, constructive one. Yet Heidegger fixes the limit: the *starting mode of experience* that gives the ground for all further characterization of consciousness turns out to be a *theoretical* one, not a genuinely natural one in which the experienced could give itself in its original sense; the human here gives itself as *objectivity for theoretical nature-consideration*. Even Dilthey's rich theses (the person finds itself over against a world on which it acts and which acts back; the *whole* person reacts in every moment; the life-nexus is in every situation one of development) are carried out with the crude means of traditional psychology; their value is the opening of new *horizons* for the question of the being of the acts, not their conceptual penetration.

**c) Husserl's uptake of the personalistic tendency in the *Logos*-essay.** In "Philosophy as Rigorous Science" (1910) the psychic is the immanent given, "the counterpart of nature." But when Husserl asks *what we investigate at it as its being*, he formulates it as: what can we grasp, fix as *objective unities*? Being means for him nothing but *true being, objectivity, true for a theoretical scientific knowing*. Not the specific being of consciousness is asked after but a distinguished *object-being for an objective science of consciousness*. His answer — that psychic phenomena, being no nature, have an essence graspable adequately in immediate seeing, so that passing from individual description to essence-consideration wins a being of consciousness that is *objectively determinable* — makes the primary thing in characterizing consciousness the sense of a possible scientific objectivity, not its own specific being that subsists *before* all scientific treatment. Even when Husserl insists that the unity-formations "person, personality, character" are of a *principally different kind* than the thinghoods of nature — not given through adumbrating appearances, so that the human is now to be set up as *non-nature* — the positive sense of this personal being throws us *back* on the immanent consciousness-structure already known from "pure consciousness"; we are led to the same basis, immanent reflection on acts, without the acts being genuinely determined. Heidegger reports Husserl's *Ideen II* (drafted with *Ideen I*, unpublished at the time): its third part, "the constitution of the spiritual world," expressly opposes the personalistic to the naturalistic attitude and even subordinates the latter to the former — "annihilate all spirits from the world and there is no more nature; annihilate nature and the individual spirit still remains." The primacy of the personalistic is theoretically pronounced. But when Heidegger looks at *how* the person is determined, it comes back to the familiar: the personalistic experience is *inspectio sui*, inner consideration of oneself as the I of intentionality, the subject of the *cogitationes* — Descartes again by the very expression — and the analysis runs into the old questions of the interlacing of personalistic and naturalistic attitude, soul and body, psychophysical parallelism, the relativity of nature and absoluteness of spirit. Ontologically all stays as before; Husserl only returns, under another title, to his primal division of being.

**d) Fundamental critique of the personalistic psychology.** That Husserl does justice to the personalistic attitude does not force a revision of the critique; on the contrary, *precisely with* the personalistic attitude the question of the genuine being of the acts is *barred* — a thesis holding against Dilthey's position too. Three points. The consideration stays within the question of the *constitution of reality and objectivity*. The mode of *access* to the person is nothing other than the already-determined immanent reflection (*inspectio sui*), from which all theses of absolute givenness derive. And the fore-determination of the unity of the experience-nexus as *spirit and person* keeps the traditional definition of the human — *homo animal rationale* — as its guiding thread; this last is the most important insight. The founding stratum remains nature-actuality, on it the psychic, on that the spiritual; so the question runs after the *constitution of the spiritual world* — the personalistic attitude's genuine naturalness is thematically stressed, but the executing analysis gives priority to the investigation of *nature*, and the being of the person is not primarily experienced as such. And even if the being of the acts and the unity of the stream were determined, there would remain the question of the being of the *full concrete human*: can its being be *built up* from the being of material substratum, body, soul, spirit? Is the person's being the *product* of these strata's ways of being? Or does it show here that on this path of prior partition and subsequent stitching-together one *cannot* reach the phenomena — that the person is taken as a *multi-layered world-thing*, whose being is never reached by any determination, however far driven, of the reality of its directing-toward? What one keeps is always only the being of a pre-given objective, a real object — being as objectivity for a consideration. This partitioning of the human and the ordering of the acts into the sequence physical–body–soul–spirit re-introduces exactly the consideration that governed the shaping of pure consciousness: the traditional definition of the human as *animal rationale*, with *ratio* as the rational person. The human is taken not as nature-reality but still as *world-reality*, constituting itself as transcendence in absolute consciousness. Husserl does not get beyond Dilthey, however superior his particular analyses; if anything Dilthey, who lacked the means to pose the question of being, at least harbored the *tendency* toward it. The upshot: behind all questions of the intentional, psychic, consciousness, experience, life, human, reason, spirit, person, I, subject stands the old definition *animal rationale* — and one must ask whether that definition is drawn from an experience *aiming at the primary being of the human*, or springs rather from the experience of the human as a *present-at-hand world-thing (animal) that has reason with it (rationale)*.

**e) Scheler's failed attempt.** Scheler, under stronger influence of Bergson and Dilthey, comes closer to the question, since he stresses the peculiarity of *being-a-person* and wants nothing of Husserl's specifically reason-theoretical orientation. He too demarcates the acts as the *non-psychic* against the psychic, and holds that the person is the *act-performer*; but he adds that the unity of the person is *not the product* of experiences (not a Gestalt-unity resulting from them) — rather the being of the person first determines the being of the acts — and that, as an essential law, the person's being is no general I-ness but always *individual* person. Person "may never be thought as a thing or substance that has faculties or powers, e.g. reason"; it is "the immediately co-experienced unity of ex-periencing," no thing behind the experienced. And still more expressly of the *acts*: "never is an act also an object, for it belongs to the essence of the being of acts to be experienced only in performance itself and given in reflection" — not in perception; acts are something *non-psychic* belonging to the essence of the person, which exists only in the performing of intentional acts and so can essentially be no object. "Every psychological objectification is identical with de-personalization." Yet when Heidegger asks positively *how* the being of the acts and of the person is determined, it stays at: acts are performed, and the person is the act-performer — over the *manner of being* of act-performance and act-performer there is *silence*. Scheler at least tries to press further into the acts, but on the question of the being-structure and the conceptuality in which that being is asked, he remains in the vague pair *performance/performer*; the connections of act-whole (person) to psychic, psychic to bodily, bodily to physical, are again determined in general traditional horizons.

**f) Result: the neglect is grounded in Dasein's falling.** The critical result: phenomenological research too stands *under the ban of an old tradition*, precisely where the most original determination of its ownmost theme, intentionality, is at stake. Against its own principle it determines its ownmost matter not from the things themselves but from a traditional, self-evident pre-opinion in whose sense it lies precisely to *deny* the original leap to the intended being. Phenomenology is therefore, in the basic task of determining its ownmost field, *un*phenomenological — while *taking itself* to be phenomenological. And more fundamentally still: not only does the being of the intentional stay undetermined, but *categorial primal-distinctions* in beings are made (consciousness / reality) *without* the guiding aspect — being — being clarified in its sense or even asked after. This deeper neglect is no mere carelessness, no accidental oversight, any more than the orientation to *animal rationale* is a chance slip. In the neglect of the primary question of the sense of being shows the *pressure and burden of the tradition* in a scarcely-overestimable measure. Wherever the being of beings is handled *without express questioning* — not only in the express so-called ontologies — the being-determinations and categories that Plato and Aristotle discovered in outline are *in effect*, ruling, without the ground from which they were drawn being kept in living research-experience. Plato's question in the *Sophist* — "what do you mean when you use the word 'being'?" — was once posed so vividly; but since Aristotle it has *fallen silent*, and so silent that one no longer knows it has fallen silent, because one thenceforth constantly handles being in the determinations and perspectives handed down from the Greeks, imagining one poses the question while never coming into its reach.

The two neglects — of the sense of being as such, and of the being of the intentional — are not chance negligences of philosophers; in them *the history of our own Dasein reveals itself* — history not as the totality of public events but as the manner of happening of Dasein itself. Dasein, in its manner of being as *falling* (Verfallen), which it does not itself escape, first comes to its being when it rears up against it. The rule of the ontological, anthropological, and hence logical tradition asserts itself the more easily and self-evidently in philosophy the more philosophy itself, in projecting its tasks and in the ways and means of answering them, places itself back into the tradition — with Husserl, the tradition of Descartes and the reason-problematic issuing from him (the anti-psychologistic essence-being, the priority of the reason-theoretical, the idea of a pure constitution of reality in the non-real, the idea of absolute rigorous science); with Scheler, Augustinian-neoplatonic and Pascalian motives that make his position more dogmatic still by taking the specifically *Christian* definition of the human into the person-idea. In both, the classical Greek tradition is covertly at work, and the definition *animal rationale* governs the question of spirit, reason, I, life. This grounding is the pivot of the whole course: the failure to ask the question of being is not a local error to be corrected by a sharper argument but an *event of Dasein's own falling* — which is why the positive answer must begin not with a better theory of consciousness but with an analytic of the being (Dasein) whose falling covers the question over. So the Preparatory Part ends by demanding exactly what the Main Part will deliver: an interpretation of the being of the questioner as the ground on which alone the question of being, and with it the phenomenon of time, can be won.

## Main Part: Analysis of the Time-Phenomenon and Winning of the Concept of Time

### First Section: Preparatory description of the field in which the phenomenon of time becomes visible

*What Heidegger actually delivers under this heading is the existential analytic of Dasein — the "field in which the phenomenon of time becomes visible." The immanent critique of Husserl issued in a demand: the question of being must be posed, and it can be posed only by way of the being that questions. That being is Dasein, and its analysis is the whole of the Main Part.*

#### Chapter One: The exposition of the question of being

##### § 14. Exposition of the question of being (unfolding it on the guiding thread of time)

The introductory considerations, as immanent critique, led to the fundamental question: what does being mean? What is the being of the intentional? Heidegger recalls that time was already given a distinguished function in dividing the kinds of being (temporal, supra-temporal, extra-temporal), and that the history of the concept of time is the history of the question of the being of beings — perhaps the history of the *decline* of that ground-question. Taking up the question of being now, we will strike upon the phenomenon of time and be led to an explication of it. The first piece of the actual considerations is therefore the *exposition of the question of being*, which is nothing other than what the critique has shown to be necessary: the "preparatory description of the field in which the phenomenon of time becomes visible." Heidegger heads off the worry that the long passage through phenomenology now stands unconnected to what follows and could have been spared — especially since the question of being can in principle be raised at *any* being, not only the intentional. His answer: he presupposes no phenomenological *results* to deduce from, but questions *only phenomenologically*, without going along with determinate theses; there is an inner, substantive connection, and the next steps lead straight back into the field the critique opened. That task he now treats under the title that shows the connection: the working-out of the question of being in the sense of a first explication of Dasein.

#### Chapter Two: The working-out of the question of being in the sense of a first explication of Dasein

##### § 15. The springing of the question of being from an indeterminate pre-understanding of Dasein — the question of being and the understanding of being

The question of being is to be *posed* — not raised blindly and arbitrarily, nor its answer guessed without orientation, but worked out as a *research question*, so that the working-out itself wins the secure horizon of questioning after the being of beings and pre-sketches the path of the answer-finding inquiry, from what the answer is drawn and against what it is confirmed. The question asks after being: what does being mean? Formally the answer runs, "being means such-and-such." It is a *determination-question*: it does not ask *whether* there is such a thing as being at all, but what is *meant* by it, understood under "being." And in asking after the *sense* of being, being is in a certain way *already understood* — in the manner of a wholly indeterminate pre-understanding whose very indeterminacy can be grasped phenomenologically. We ("the anyone") do not know what "being" means, and yet the expression is somehow intelligible to everyone. Whence this understanding flows to the individual — from handed-down theories, express appropriation, or mere takeover — is at first indifferent; there is an understanding of "being," though hard at the boundary of mere word-understanding. On the ground of this indeterminate pre-understanding the question is asked. Even this vague pre-understanding is *still* an understanding; it carries the possibility of the question in itself, and from it the express questioning rises. We constantly make use of this indeterminate "being" — so far-reachingly that we do not know we use it indeterminately: "what *is* being? what *is* proper to its being?" We always already live in an understanding of the "is" without being able to say what it means. That an understanding and a certain concept of being is *always already there* — why this is so, and how the fact is to be understood, is to be discussed later. (This "always-already-there" understanding of being is the ground on which the entire analytic rests, and the seed of its eventual claim that Dasein *is* its understanding of being.)

##### § 16. The question-structure of the question of being

The question asks what being means; the *sense of being* is what is asked-for. What is more exactly asked for? To ask after being is to ask after the ground-character of beings, what determines a being *as* a being — that is its being. Heidegger articulates the question formally into three moments. The *asked-for* (Erfragtes): the sense of being. The *questioned* (Gefragtes): the being *of* beings. The *interrogated* (Befragtes): the being itself. To determine a being in its being, the being must be interrogated *toward* its being; and this is exhibitable only if the being itself is accessible *at itself* in its being. Working through the three:

Begin with the interrogated (third moment). To ask after the being of beings, the being itself must be interrogated toward it, and so must be experienced *at itself*. But we call very much "being," in very different senses — everything we speak of, mean, comport toward (even the inaccessible), everything we are related to, and everything we ourselves are and *how* we are. *Which* being, then, is to be experienced at itself as the one from which the possible sense of being can be won and read off? And which is the *manner of access* to it, so that it becomes visible at itself? Rolling out the question thus includes a double determination of the interrogated: the determination of the being that is to *give* the sense of being originally, and the determination of the *right access* to that being.

Second, the question has its *questioned*: the interrogated (the being) is interrogated *toward* something — not simply taken at itself but taken *as*, in view of its being. This anticipating-toward needs the *direction* it must take to catch sight of the being at the being; not only the right experience of the being but the *aspect* in which the interrogated is to be taken must be fixed, so that one can glimpse "being" at it at all. Aspect is provisionally determined by two points of view: the *direction* of the searching look, and the *toward-which* at the being that is asked after.

Third, in the questioned lies the *asked-for* itself — being, whose sense is sought, that *as which* being is to be conceived. With the asked-for its *concept* is sought; the questioning must formally determine the possibility and kind of conceptuality proper to the asked-for — whether being is something like a category or not — apart from its material characterization. So the question contains three things: the originary pre-experience of the primary interrogated and the determination of its manner of experience; the look, set within the interrogating and itself concerned, toward the sought (being) at it; and the characterization of the *sense* of the questioned as such, its conceptuality.

The formal scaffolding is easy to mark; the *concrete* working-out faces peculiar tasks, above all the formation of the guiding thread. Heidegger presses the decisive turn: what is it that is to be determined here — the *access to*, the *experience of*, the *look toward*, the *addressing as*, the *conceiving as*? Are not such modes of access and experience themselves already *beings*? To pose the question of the sense of the being of beings rightly, must we not first determine and delimit a being — *the being that questions*? The question of the being of beings will become the more transparent the more properly we have made visible this being, the being of the questioning of the questioner itself. So answering the question of being requires the *prior working-out of a being toward its being* — the being we call *the questioning itself*.

Is this not a plain *circle*? Heidegger lets the objection stand, to dispose of it later, marking only this: the charge of a circle has sense only where it is a matter of *deriving propositions from others* — where C and D, to be derived from A and B, are themselves set in function for the grounding of A and B. But here it is not a deduction of propositions from one another; it is the working-out of *access to the matters* from which propositions are first to be drawn. And formal objections like "circle" are sterile at the start of such fundamental considerations, contributing nothing to the understanding of the matter and merely retarding the inquiry. The beginning is unambiguous: either we pose the question of the being of beings, or we leave it unposed and the answer in the dark — and renouncing it, one forfeits the right to say anything scientifically about being and beings at all. If the question is to be posed, there is no bargaining: the being characterized as access, experience, and so on must itself be illuminated in its being, at the risk of a circle — a *circle of investigating*, of the course, and of being, a "*being-circle*," a circularity *of a being* that must be understood, and from which the familiar traditional objection of a circle in grounding first springs. A circle of *grounding*, in any case, there is not.

##### § 17. The connection of the question of being and the questioning being (Dasein)

The more properly and purely this being of questioning, experiencing, and conceiving is worked out in its being, the more radically the answer to the question of being can be given — and it is worked out the more purely the more originally it is itself experienced, the more adequately conceptually determined, the more properly the being-relation *to* it is won and grasped. Such a being-relation is won the more genuinely the less it is decided in advance from pre-opinions and views, however self-evident and generally acknowledged, and the more the being can show itself *from itself* — it becomes determinable *as phenomenon*. Recalling that the questioning is itself a being: questioning is a being given, in the very performance of the question of the being of beings, whether expressly attended to or not. This yields a wholly distinguished questioning, since in the question-content, in the asked-for, is precisely what the questioning *itself* is. The asked-for in it, the sense of being, is given in all indeterminacy — as indeterminate as anything sought can be. If the questioning is genuine, it must be adequate to its asked-for, must rightly understand what it asks after (being); and the asked-for, as *this* asked-for, *strikes back* on the questioning itself, insofar as this is a being. Yet in asking after being we do not pose the question of the being of the being that the questioning itself is; we satisfy the sense of the question of being if we first merely think through the questioning, *as a being*, in what it is. We cannot yet explicitly ask after *its* sense, since it is precisely this questioning that seeks to determine itself more closely, as a being, in its *what*. As *what* is this being — of which we say questioning, looking-toward, addressing-as, relating — pre-given? *It is the being that we ourselves are; this being that I in each case am we call Dasein.*

To work out the questioning after the sense of being means: to *lay free the questioning as a being*, i.e. Dasein itself — for only so does the sought become sought in its ownmost sense. The questioning is here itself co-affected *by* its asked-for, because it stands under the question of beings and is itself a being; this *being-affected* of the questioning being by the asked-for belongs to the ownmost sense of the question of being, and to it the phenomenological principle must do justice if the question is to be posed transparently. The questioning is the being expressly given with the question, but also so given that in the very course of questioning it is *first and above all overlooked*. The attempt here is precisely *not* to overlook this being from the start — not to overlook it with respect to the question of being itself. The actual working-out of the question is thus a *phenomenology of Dasein*, and it already *finds the answer* — finds it purely as a research-answer — because the working-out concerns the being that encloses in itself a *distinguished being-relation*. Dasein is not only ontically decisive here but becomes ontologically decisive for us as phenomenologists.

Heidegger anchors the claim in the history of the question. Where the question of being first surfaced, in Parmenides, there is already a peculiar interlocking, so tightly drawn that what is asked after, and determined in its being, is *identified* with the questioning and experiencing comportment: *to gar auto noein estin te kai einai* — being is the same as the perceiving of the being in its being. Already in the question what being is, the *experiencing* of the interrogated is expressly reckoned in, though the question's structure is not yet explicit. Later, at a higher level, in Plato and Aristotle, to the question after *ousia* corresponds the co-consideration of the questioning determining, the *logos*; to the question of being, *dialegesthai*, dialectic; to the grasping of the *eidos*, *idein* — but characteristically *logos* and *idein*, addressing and looking, are treated *accessorily*, alongside, because such accompanying treatment is *necessary* to handle the question meaningfully at all. That Plato comes to the question of the *logos* as dialectic lies simply in the sense of the question he posed, the question of being, which itself demands that the questioning be determined as a being. The matter asked after — being — *demands* the exhibition of the being Dasein; the phenomenological tendency alone (to clarify and understand being as such) bears in itself the task of explicating the being that the questioning itself is.

Heidegger insists this explication is *not* driven by any special interest in the psychology of the human, no worldview-question about the meaning and purpose of our life, no residual problem such as a philosophical anthropology within the other disciplines — but *solely* by the fully understood, phenomenologically won sense of the question (asked-for, questioned, interrogated, questioning). The working-out is the prior experience and explication of the questioning being itself — the Dasein that we ourselves are. It concerns a being to which we stand in the distinguished, at any rate remarkable being-relation that *we are it* — a being that *is* only insofar as I in each case *am* it. It is the being *nearest* to us; but is it also the *nearest-given*? In that respect it is perhaps the *farthest*. So it happens that when this being is determined at all, it is *not* determined from an original grasp of itself. This being, which we ourselves are and which in respect of givenness is the farthest, is to be determined phenomenologically, brought to phenomenon — experienced so that it shows itself *at itself* — so that from this phenomenal givenness certain basic structures can be drawn, sufficient to carry out the concrete question of being transparently. That we rightly, indeed necessarily, first ask after this being (Dasein) by provisionally laying it free will ground itself in the growing insight into its being-structure: the necessity of starting, in the question of being, from the clarification of questioning as a being, is *demanded by this being itself*. For this being, the questioning, itself disposes over a determinate *sense of being* — precisely the sense that holds itself in a certain *not-understanding*, which is what is to be determined. The next task, then, is the explication of Dasein as the being whose manner of being is *questioning itself*. (The circularity is not vicious but constitutive: Dasein is the being for whom, in its very being, that being is *at issue* — the being that already understands being, however vaguely, and can therefore be interrogated as the site where the sense of being is legible.)

#### Chapter Three: The nearest explication of Dasein out of its everydayness. The basic constitution of Dasein as being-in-the-world

##### § 18. Winning the fundamental structures of Dasein's basic constitution

The theme is nearest, not foreign — and precisely therefore prone to being mis-seen. What constantly covers the phenomenal nexus is the entrenched mis-seeing and mis-interpreting that comes with closest familiarity; the self-evident never becomes a possible theme at all. So the first requirement is to win the direction of sight and shut off misleading questioning.

**a) Dasein is in the "in-each-case-having-to-be-it."** Dasein is the being that I in each case am, in whose being I as a being am "involved" — a being that *is*, in each case in my manner of being it. This shows the distinguished being-relation we "have" to it: to *be* it, not, as with a being of nature's character, merely to grasp it or have it available. Hence the name: *Dasein* (being-there). The name expresses no *what*: it does not distinguish this being by its what-content, as a chair from a house, but expresses its *manner of being* — a specifically ontological expression, chosen where we otherwise name beings from their what-content and leave their being undetermined because we take it for granted. This being-relation characterizes the "to-be" as the *in-each-case-mine* (je meine). The manner of being — to be it — is essentially in-each-case-mine to be, whether I expressly know it or not, whether I have lost myself in my being (cf. *das Man*) or not. So the fundamental character is grasped only in the determination: a being that *is* in the *in-each-case-having-to-be-it*. This "in-each-case-ness" (Jeweiligkeit) is constitutive of every being-character of this being; there is no Dasein that would not, by its sense, be in-each-case-mine. And precisely therein lies that, since Dasein is *being-possible*, it can modify itself in a Dasein-like way — toward *das Man* and back; this modification, as historicity and temporality, is no restless oscillation. The insight matters for *das Man* and for the concept of authenticity and inauthenticity.

With this fundamental character — that I am it in the "in-each-case-to-be-it" — the *starting* determination is won, and at the same time the *end* determination, the one to which every being-analysis returns; every being-character of Dasein is governed by it. It also gives directives: the determination "the being *to-be*" directs us to understand all phenomena of Dasein primarily as *modes of its to-be*. Prohibitively: Dasein is *not* to be experienced and interrogated by its "look," by what it consists of, by pieces and layers a certain consideration finds at it. No look, however far determined, ever gives the answer to the question of the manner "to be." Body, soul, spirit may in some respect mark what this being consists of, but with this composite the manner of being stays undetermined from the outset, and can least of all be *extracted* from the composite afterward — for with that determination one has already displaced oneself into a being-dimension foreign to Dasein. Whether this being "consists" of psychic, physical, spiritual, and how these realities are to be determined, stays wholly unasked. Heidegger sets himself in principle *outside* the experience- and question-horizon that the definition *homo animal rationale* pre-sketches: not the look of this being is to be determined, but from the outset and throughout solely its *manner of being* — not the *what* of what it consists of, but the *how* of its being and the characters of that how.

**b) Dasein in the "to-be" of the everydayness of its in-each-case-ness.** Heidegger fixes the *everyday* as the field of the analysis. Not a "humanity" is to be laid free but the manner of being in its *nearest everydayness*, factical Dasein — not a biography of a particular Dasein, not a particular day, but the *everydayness of the everyday*, the fact in its facticity: not the everyday of a given Dasein but the everydayness of the in-each-case-ness of being *as* Dasein. This does not mean describing Dasein at a *primitive* stage: everydayness is not identical with primitivity; a highly differentiated life is still — indeed all the more properly — determinable in its everydayness. Nor does analyzing everydayness mean deriving the other being-possibilities *from* it, as though a genetic account could extract every other possibility from the everyday. Everydayness attests how Dasein *has* to be and how it *is* in the everyday, if in different ways. Heidegger flags forward: everydayness will prove to be a specific *concept of time*.

##### § 19. The basic constitution of Dasein as being-in-the-world. The in-being of Dasein and the being-in of present-at-hand things

Keeping to the fundamental character, Heidegger lays free a *basic constitution*: Dasein shows itself as *being-in-the-world* (In-der-Welt-sein). This is a *unitary and original* determination; it is a *necessary* Dasein-structure, though far from a sufficient one. Three moments can be lifted from it, each pursued to its phenomenal stock: being-in-the-world in the special sense of *world* — world as the *how* of being, ontologically the *worldhood of the world*; the being as determined from the *who* of this being-in-the-world and the how of its being; and *in-being* (In-Sein) as such. When the basic constitution becomes theme under three aspects, it is in each single consideration always *wholly present as itself*; what the aspects lift out are not *pieces* from which the whole would first be assembled, but purely *thematic* liftings, each of which is always the proper grasp of the whole structure at itself. To signal this, Heidegger prefaces the analysis of "in-the-world" with an orienting characterization of the *last-named* moment, in-being as such.

What does *in-being* mean? We complete it to "in-the-world" and incline to understand it as "being-in..." — the being-relation of something "in" something. Giving the "in" intuitive exhibition, we say: the water *in* the glass, the clothes *in* the cupboard, the benches *in* the lecture hall — a *spatial containedness* of one in another, the being-relation of two spatially extended beings in respect of place and space; both are "in" space and have their place; both are only *in* space and have no *in-being*. Extending fittingly: the bench in the hall, the hall in the university building, the building in Marburg, Marburg in Hesse, in Germany, in Europe, on the earth, in a solar system, in space, in the world — one uniform being-relation, in principle no different across all these. This being-"in" belongs to the *look*; the beings whose being-in-one-another is at issue have throughout the same manner of being, *present-at-hand* (Vorhandensein), as occurring things discoverable as worldly.

But *in-being* as a being-structure of Dasein, the being that I in each case am, does *not* mean this described being-in-one-another, the spatial containedness of a body-thing "human body" in a spatial container (hall, building) called world. It cannot mean this if we hold to the fundamental character: Dasein is not to be taken as a being in respect of its look but only in its manner of being. Heidegger grounds the point etymologically: "in" derives from *innan*, to dwell, *habitare*; "*an*" means "I am accustomed to, familiar with, I tend something" — Latin *colo* as *habito* and *diligo*. And the "*bin*" ("am") of "*ich bin*" hangs together with "*bei*" ("alongside"): "I am" means I dwell, I reside *alongside* the world as the familiar. Being as *in-being* and "I am" means *dwelling-alongside*; "in" means primarily nothing spatial but *being-familiar-with*. A local sense can attach to correctly understood in-being — I as being-in-the-world am always somewhere — but even my standing-here at this place, insofar as it characterizes being-in-the-world, is *essentially different* from the present-at-hand-ness of the chair in this room. This in-being, never to be understood in a primarily local, spatial sense, is as *being-alongside* determined by in-each-case-ness: it is in each case mine and in each case *this*.

Possible modes of in-being belonging to everydayness: handling something with something, producing, cultivating and tending, taking into use, employing something for something, keeping in safekeeping, giving up, letting get lost, interrogating, discussing, carrying through, exploring, considering, determining. These modes have the character — still to be clarified — of *concern* (Besorgen): taking something into care, having something in care. The corresponding modifications of non-concern, omitting, resting, standing-off are in principle of the *same* manner of being; even when I do nothing and merely drowse and so reside in the world, I have this specific being of concernful being-in-the-world. Stating the genuine meaning of in-being does not yet guarantee *seeing* the phenomenon; it is more than a word-explanation, fixing the direction of sight above all *prohibitively* — indicating where we are *not* to look. Dasein, insofar as it is at all, is in the manner of being of in-being; so in-being in this sense is no *property* the being called Dasein has or lacks, that could accrue or not, without which it could be just as well — as though the being of Dasein could first be grasped otherwise, "in-being-free." In-being is rather the *being-constitution* of Dasein in which every manner of being of this being is grounded. It is no mere addition to a being that would be Dasein even without this constitution, as though the world in which each Dasein always already is came to it only occasionally, or the being to the world, to then occasionally take up a "relation" to it. Such taking-up of relations to the world is possible *only* because Dasein already is being-in-the-world on the ground of its being-alongside. This peculiar phenomenon — that in-being determines Dasein in its being itself — must be held clearly in view as the *apriori of every determinate relation to the world*.

##### § 20. Knowing as a derivative mode of Dasein's in-being

From early on Dasein's relation to the world was determined primarily from the manner of being of *knowing*; the "relation of subject to object" was first grasped as a *knowing* relation, and a "practical relation" built in afterward. Even if it were admissible to grasp knowing-being-in-the-world as the primary manner (which it is not), everything depends on first seeing it phenomenally. On the usual view, when the being-relation between subject and object is reflected on, a being called *nature* is pre-given, which is known and which Dasein — because it is being-in-the-world — first finds, tends, is concerned with. At *that* being the knowing that knows it is not itself met with; so knowing must be somewhere else. But at the *knowing thing*, the human-thing, knowing is not present-at-hand either, not perceivable like its color and extension. Yet knowing is supposed to be *at* this thing; if not "outside," then "inside" — in this subject-thing, *in mente*. The more unambiguously one holds that knowing is "inside," the more presuppositionless one believes oneself in asking after the essence of knowledge — for now the question arises: how does knowing, which by its being is inside, in the subject, get *out* of its "inner sphere" into "another, outer sphere," to the world?

Heidegger exposes the concealed premise: one has assumed, expressly or not, a being-relation between *two present-at-hand beings*, and brought it onto the determination of an *inside/outside* relation — asking how this relation between subject and object is possible, supposedly in accord with the facts of knowing, *without in the least* determining the being-sense of this knowing, of this subject-object relation, without clarifying the being-sense of the subject and marking it off against the object. One assures that the "inside" is not properly spatial, no "casing" — but *what* this immanence positively is, and how the being of the subject is to be understood insofar as it is at first only immanently *with itself*, one is never told. However the "inside" be determined, so long as the question is posed how knowing gets "out" of it, the treatment has *betrayed itself as founded on a semblance*. In the whole starting-point, one is blind to what one thereby says about Dasein when one ascribes knowing to it as a manner of being. For nothing more and nothing less is said than: knowing of the world is a manner of being of Dasein, *ontically founded* in its basic constitution, being-in-the-world.

To the traditional objection that this interpretation *destroys* the problem of knowledge, Heidegger replies: what instance decides whether and in what sense there can be a problem of knowledge except the *matter itself*? Every knowing already accomplishes itself on the ground of the manner of being we call in-being, always-already-being-alongside-a-world. Knowing is not a comportment starting in a being that does *not yet* "have" a world; it is always a manner of being of Dasein on the ground of its *already-being-alongside* the world. The basic defect of epistemology is precisely not to see what it means by knowing in its original phenomenal finding as a manner of Dasein's in-being. The assertion that there are no more knowledge-problems, once it is held in advance that Dasein is alongside its world, is no assertion but the rendering of a finding any unprejudiced seeing sees; and nowhere is it prescribed that there *must* be a knowledge-problem — perhaps it is the task of substantive research to strip many problems of their *semblance*-existence. But with the right interpretation of knowing as a mode of in-being, a knowledge-problematic is not denied but *first made possible* — especially if knowing is understood as a mode of in-being that is *not* the *basic* kind. Then the really researchable question arises: how does Dasein, in each case in a determinate but primarily *not* knowing and not only knowing manner of being, *disclose* the world in which it already is? Correlatively: what modes of *covering* belong essentially to Dasein's ways of being in its world; what is the range of discoverability proper to a given manner of being; what are the apriori being-conditions of this in-being, and hence the original, all-transcending, *transcendental-ontological*, i.e. Dasein-ontological-existential understanding of being and the being-possibilities of being-toward-the-world; what does truth mean each time for the knowing of beings of the most various kinds. The basic problem is precisely to *see* this fundamental structure and determine it ontologically in its genuine apriori.

Heidegger then sketches knowing's own *leveled structure*, the connection in which it temporalizes itself as a manner of Dasein: the first level is *directing-oneself-toward* something, but already on the ground of in-being in the world; the second, *dwelling-alongside* what Dasein directs itself to, itself grounded in the directing (which holds through and anticipatingly governs all further comportments — here "directing-toward" means the *look-taking*, the grasping-as, the "from where" of viewing); on the ground of the second, the actual *perceiving*, laying-apart, interpreting; and this forms itself as *having-perceived*, the safekeeping of what is perceived. The knowing process would then look thus: the knowing directing-toward, as dwelling-alongside and perceiving, tends toward the perceived so that it is *safekept*, so that in having-perceived, i.e. in *knowing*, knowing has the known even when not actually standing to it, keeping the cognition as a *possession*. This is intelligible only from the primary character of Dasein as "to-be," *not* if the subject is something psychic with representations that are stored and of which it must be said how they agree. And this safekeeping retention is itself nothing but a *new mode of in-being*, a being-relation to the known being: the in-being of the first point is now modified with the cognition. Through the whole sequence, from primary directing-toward to retention, only the formation of a *new being-stance* of Dasein toward the known shows itself — a being-possibility of which science and research were already the marks; ultimately these are modes of *temporalizing*, the first anticipating all the others, holding itself in them, and itself only as in-being anticipating.

The decisive corrective: in directing-toward and grasping, Dasein does not first go *out* of itself, out of an inner sphere in which it is encapsulated; by its very sense it is always already "outside," in the world, in the correctly understood sense of in-being and dwelling-alongside the world already somehow discovered. Dwelling-alongside the thing to be known is not a *having-left* the inner sphere, as if Dasein sprang out of its sphere and now merely occurred at the object; rather Dasein, even in this "being-outside" alongside the object, is in the correctly understood sense "inside" — it *itself*, as being-in-the-world, is what knows the being. Perceiving the known is no return of a grasping going-out with its captured booty into the "casing" of consciousness; even in perceiving and in having, safekeeping, retaining the perceived, the knowing Dasein remains "outside." In knowing a being-nexus of the world, even in merely *thinking* of it, merely *representing* it without originarily experiencing it, I am no less *alongside the being outside in the world* and by no means with myself in the interior. When I merely represent the Freiburg minster, this does not mean it is only immanently present in the representing; this mere representing is precisely, in the genuine and best sense, *alongside the being itself*. Even *forgetting* something, in which the being-relation to the known is apparently extinguished, is nothing but a determinate modification of being-alongside, possible only on its ground; all deception and error, in which no being-relation to the being is won but rather falsified, are again only modes of being-alongside.

So in the whole build-up of knowing on the ground of in-being, it is not that with grasping the subject first *initiates*, first *establishes* its being-relation to the world; grasping is grounded in a prior *letting-be-seen* of something (Sehenlassen), which is possible only on the ground of a *letting-be-encountered* (Begegnenlassen), and this only on the ground of an always-already-being-alongside. *Only a being that has in its being the fitness to let another being (world) encounter it* stands in the possibility of grasping, of knowledge. Knowing is nothing but a manner of being-in-the-world — and no primary one but a *founded* one, possible only on the ground of a non-knowing comportment. Heidegger closes with a historical anchor: what he sets out here as in-being is the ontological foundation of what Augustine and above all Pascal knew — they called what *properly* knows not knowing but *love and hate*. All knowing is only appropriation and mode of accomplishment of what is already discovered through other, primary comportments; knowing has rather the possibility of *covering* what was originally discovered in non-knowing comportment. What Augustine determined, one-sidedly, as love and hate as the properly knowing manner of Dasein, Heidegger will later grasp as an original phenomenon of Dasein without that restriction — learning to understand that knowledge as such cannot be grasped at all unless one sees from the outset the specific being-nexus in which knowing is possible. Once this is properly understood, it will always appear grotesque to clarify knowledge from itself by way of an epistemology, and countersensical to determine the being that as Dasein has this being-constitution by starting from the encapsulated subject.

*Heidegger closes §20 with two clarifications. The **snail analogy**: comparing the "encapsulated subject" to a snail in its shell, he grants the comparison holds only negatively — but it exposes the confusion. A snail that crawls out toward food does not thereby *first* enter a being-relation to the world; crawling out is only a local modification of its already-being-in-the-world. Even in its shell it is, correctly understood, "outside"; it has the inside of its shell as world — something it bumps against, feels, warms itself in. It does not acquire a world by feeling; it feels *because* its being means nothing other than being-in-a-world. So too a subject credited with knowing: to posit it as a being that can know is already to understand it as a being that in the manner of its being is in a world — one merely performs the positing blindly. And the **idealism/realism critique**: the epistemological positions idealism and realism, their variants and mediations, are all possible only on the ground of an unclarified in-being, about which they theorize without having laid it free. Idealism lets the subject first create the being-relation to the object; realism lets the object first effect it through causal relations; a third position (Avenarius's "principal coordination") presupposes the relation but leaves its being-manner undetermined. What Heidegger says of knowing as a mode of in-being stands neither this side nor beyond idealism and realism, and is neither of them, but stands wholly outside orientation to them. And a final anticipation: knowing can be *true* — can have truth as a distinguished predicate — only because truth is not so much a property of knowing as a **being-character of Dasein itself**.*

#### § 21. The worldhood of the world

Heidegger turns to the first structural moment of being-in-the-world: *world*. From the fact that in-being means no spatial being-in-one-another, it can be read formally that *world is the "wherein" of the being of Dasein* — and correspondingly the "wherein" is no spatial container; containment cannot make up the primary character of world. "World" in the *ontic* sense is the being that Dasein, which is *in* it, is manifestly not itself — the being *alongside which* Dasein has its being, *to which* it is. This being-to, being-toward-the-world, has the character of *concern* (Besorgen), and concern as being-in-the-world in its various ways Heidegger determines more exactly as *commerce* (Umgang) with it. In such commerce the in-each-case being-in-the-world that Dasein is *temporalizes* itself.

Taking world correlatively to in-being as the "wherein," it is now seen on the being-manner of in-being *as commerce*, in the *with-which* of concernful commerce. In such commerce Dasein always already *finds* its world beforehand, and this finding is no theoretical grasping; the already-being-alongside is *care in concern*. As concernful commerce, Dasein *lets its world be encountered*; concern, the basic manner of Dasein, *lets encounter*, and in such letting-encounter Dasein *discloses* the world. All knowing that builds on it as a mode of concern only *interprets* the disclosed world. A peculiar connection shows here: the more the first-experienced world becomes *de-worlded* (entweltlicht) — the more it becomes *bare nature*, e.g. the objectivity of physics — the more knowing itself becomes fit to disclose and discover; knowing celebrates its triumph of discoveries in mathematics. But it is the concernful being-in-the-world that lets the world be encountered; we are still too burdened by theories to see that precisely the *non-theoretical* comportment is what discovers not only the world but Dasein itself. *Care as the being-constitution of Dasein discovers world.*

Asking after the phenomenal structure of world, we ask after the *how of the being* in which the being called world shows itself *from itself* as the encountering. The encounter-structure of this being is no *conglomerate of apprehension-modes* with which a subject clothes an object, no forms with which a world-stuff is draped; the encounter-structures are those of the being of the world itself, insofar as it can show itself in everyday Dasein. This being-character Heidegger fixes terminologically as *worldhood* (Weltlichkeit) — to avoid the unclarity of taking worldhood as a being-character of *beings*; it is a being-character of *Dasein*, and thereby and at once of beings. To determine the worldhood of world means: to lay free the structure of the *how* of the self-encountering of the being wherein Dasein, by its basic constitution as in-being, is. Phenomenological interpretation of worldhood is *not* a narrative description of the look of world-things — that there really are mountains, rivers, houses, tables — nor would running through the sum of all world-things ever yield the sense of world; in every such characterization world is already meant beforehand as "world-thing." The question is not what all can occur in the world but the *how of the being* of any such being: worldhood as the being-possibility of the letting-encounter of in-being, a *transcendental exhibition* of worldhood from the being of Dasein qua in-being, not a narrative report of world-occurrences but an interpretation of worldhood that characterizes everything occurring *as worldly*.

The question after worldhood is not self-evident, Heidegger stresses, and it is not obvious that one wins the right starting-point for grasping the being of world originally. Previous philosophical consideration was throughout led by quite determinate presuppositions, both about the possibility of an original mode of grasping the world and about the sense of being the world must have. He will try to set aside these distorting presuppositions and make worldhood clear as it *shows itself in Dasein's everyday commerce with its world* — and only from there understand how this nearest-given world can, on the ground of motives partly lying in itself, be discovered as *nature*, whose discovery the natural sciences carry out. The world is to interest us as *environing world* (Umwelt) with respect to its worldhood — the structure that characterizes each thing as an environmental thing — and against the traditional question of the *reality of the external world*, it is to be held fast that we ask after the worldhood of the world *as it is there in nearest-everyday concern*: after the world as it encounters in commerce, i.e. after the *environing world*, more exactly its worldhood. In asking specifically after a characterization of the "around"-character (das Umhafte), Heidegger sets the genuine sense of place and space *within* the build-up of worldhood into its right: place and space play a constitutive role, but the environing character of the "around" is *not primarily spatial* — not spatial at all if spatiality is defined as the dimensionality of metric, geometrical space. The constant warding-off of spatiality nonetheless points to *some* sense of spatiality being in play; hence it is decisive not to start from a spatiality that is the geometrical one, first cut out of the primary, original space of the world. The metric space and its modification are to be understood *from* a more original spatiality — but first the sense of worldhood must be understood.

#### § 22. The traditional overleaping of the question of the worldhood of the world, on the example of Descartes

Heidegger proceeds prohibitively, delimiting the phenomenal horizon by switching off the direction of sight that does not lead to the phenomenon — important here because the question of the being of the world has always been posed as the question of the being of *nature*, not only since modern natural science but in a sense already among the Greeks, so that the whole stock of concepts we have for characterizing the being of the world stems from the consideration of world *as nature*. In an original analysis of the world that does *not* set nature as the primary, we are in complete embarrassment as to concepts and expression.

The most extreme counter-case, in method and result, is Descartes. He stands at a characteristic place: he takes over the determinations of the being of the world as the Middle Ages, and thereby Greek philosophy, pre-sketched them, yet by his extreme manner of asking after the being of the world he pre-sketches the problems that surface in Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* and beyond. When Descartes asks after the being of a being, he asks in the tradition's sense after *substance* — mostly, strictly, after *substantiality*, a determinate manner of being, the distinguished, primary being-manner that can accrue to a being at all; beings with that manner are *substances*. *Substantia* means a double: the being itself in the manner of substance, and substantiality — corresponding to the distinction world/worldhood — though the sense of worldhood and the structure of substantiality are *fundamentally different*. By substance, Descartes says, we can understand nothing but a thing (*res*) that so exists that it needs *no other thing in order to exist*. Substantiality means *present-at-hand-ness* that as such is unneeding of another being — unneeding of a producer or sustainer. And a being wholly unneeding of any thing can only be understood as *one*, namely *God*. "God" is here a purely *ontological* concept, the title for the being in which the idea of being *properly* realizes itself, called *ens perfectissimum* — nothing religious lies in it. God is the single substance, the single being that *is* in the supposedly "proper" sense of being (present-at-hand-ness); everything else *is* only with God's help, needing production and preservation, and so, understood from proper being, is *ens creatum*.

Heidegger draws out the consequence that decides everything: for created beings the name "substance" does *not* apply to God and to them *univocally* (as the Schools said) — no signification of the name can be distinctly understood common to God and creatures. Because between the being-manners of these two beings there is, for Descartes, an *infinite* difference, "being," though used of both, cannot be used univocally but only (as scholasticism said) *analogically*: the concept of being, insofar as it is used of the whole manifold of possible beings, has the character of an *analogical* concept. This analogy Aristotle first discovered, and this is the real advance over Plato's grasp of the being-concept; the question whether being is analogical or univocal regarding God and world played a great role in the later Middle Ages and negatively influenced Luther's theological development. Heidegger's point is that Descartes, working with an *unclarified* concept of being (present-at-hand-ness), settles the being of the *world* by a purely deductive theology of substance — and thereby *overleaps* the worldhood of the world entirely. Descartes wins his basic determinations of the being of the world by orienting the *res extensa* toward the properly infinite substance, God; and the being of the corporeal thing is fixed as *extension* — determinable by length, breadth, and depth, graspable by the mathematical mode of apprehension. This is not read off from a corporeal thing showing itself; rather a determinate *idea of space* is laid at the basis, and from it the being of the world is decided. Descartes even knows (in the sixth *Meditation*) that his determination of the body leaves out what the senses give — that things are *useful or harmful* (*prodesse aut nocere*) — and *deliberately* pushes the original mode of access represented in *touch* into a determinate direction (the mathematical), so that the being of the corporeal is fixed as extension and the true being is the *mathematically knowable*. Because for Descartes *verum ens = certum ens*, the true known being is the proper being. So a determinate mode of apprehension, the mathematical, *prescribes* the being of the world, and the original encounter-manner of the environing world is passed over.

The upshot: a whole chain of presuppositions drives away from the genuine phenomenon of world. What Descartes does — determining the being of the world from the mathematical, from *nature* as the nature of natural science, seen through causality — is only the most transparent case of a tendency that from Descartes onward makes the being of the world *relative* to a determinate theoretical apprehension. And this overleaping can itself be clarified only from the *essential being-manner of Dasein*: the stubbornness and the constant intruding of the apprehension-mode as *theoretical thing-perceiving* is grounded in the fact that the world, in the most natural and immediate being-in-the-world, when Dasein goes up in its concerned world and is as it were carried along by the concerned world, is precisely *not thematically experienced in its worldhood*. The world is expressly experienced only where it is grasped in some *theoretical* meaning; and only when its being is asked after theoretically does it become thematic. So the prejudice for the primary analysis of the world is made harmless only when the being-manner of this theoretical grasping *and its priority* is itself understood.

#### § 23. The positive exhibition of the basic structure of the worldhood of the world

How, then, is worldhood to be determined *positively*, in abstraction from all theory and this extreme objectification? Heidegger builds the analysis to answer four traditional questions at once: *why* the proper being-structure of the world, primary worldhood, was overleaped in philosophy from the start; *why*, even when a substitute phenomenon (value-predicates) was introduced for it, this structure was still held to need explanation and derivation; *why* it is "explained" by grounding it in a *fundamental stratum of actuality*; and *why* this founding actuality is grasped as the being of *nature*, indeed as the objectivity of mathematical physics. Understanding the analysis requires transposing oneself into the specific natural manner of commerce in which we constantly move — or rather not transposing but *expressly lifting* the comportment in which we already, everydayly, move, and which for that very reason is least of all at first visible.

**a) The encounter-characters of the world: reference, reference-totality, familiarity, the "anyone."** How does world show itself in everyday concern? This being, world, presents itself in the character of "serviceable-for," "conducive-to," "detrimental-to," "of relevance for," and the like. The worldly encounters *always in and as reference* (Verweisung) to another. What is drawn into presence along with such reference is the *toward-which* — the *for-what* the conducive is what it is. In these reference-relations a manifold of environmental things shows itself — a public square with its surroundings, a room with its furnishings. The thing-manifold that encounters is *not* an arbitrary set of occurring things but is present first and solely in a determinate *reference-nexus*, which is itself a *closed totality*. This totality shows *from out of itself* the single piece of furniture. The room does not encounter by my apprehending one thing after another and composing a manifold to then see a room; primarily I see a *reference-totality as closed*, from which the single piece of furniture is what is there. Such an environing world, of the character of a closed reference-totality, is at the same time distinguished by a specific *familiarity* (Vertrautheit): the closedness of the reference-whole is grounded in familiarity, and familiarity means that the reference-relations are *known*. Everyday concern, as using and handling, constantly follows these relations; one dwells in them. So the *references* are the "wherein" the concernful commerce dwells — not isolated environmental things, and least of all thematically perceived objects. The things step *back* into the reference-totality; more properly, in the immediacy of everyday commerce they do not *step out of it at all*. This not-stepping-out — such that the totality itself primarily encounters in the character of familiarity — characterizes the *self-evidence and unobtrusiveness* of the reality of the environing world; the things step back into the relations, do not press themselves forward, precisely so as to be there *for* concern.

This phenomenon is overleaped precisely when one sets the world as *given for a beholding*, or (as often in phenomenology too) sets the world as it shows itself in an *isolated sensory perception* of a thing and then asks after the givenness-mode of *that* isolated, free-floating thing-perception's object. A basic phenomenological deception operates here — peculiarly frequent and stubborn — that the theme is determined *by the phenomenological mode of investigation itself*: insofar as phenomenological investigation is theoretical, it easily makes a specifically *theoretical* comportment to the world its theme, setting up an exemplary theoretical thing-grasping as a mode of being-in-the-world, instead of transposing itself into the *inconspicuous* pull of everyday commerce and holding fast phenomenally what encounters there. Precisely this inconspicuousness of comportment and of the corresponding having-there of the world must be *won* to see the specific *presence* of the world.

This priority of the reference-totality over the things springs to the eye precisely when an environmental thing becomes present in an *emphatic* sense — namely when a being of the character "serviceable-for" *fails* in its serviceability, becomes *unusable*, is *damaged*. Then the environmental thing *becomes conspicuous*; concern, its natural pull, is *held up*; the reference-nexus and the reference-totality suffer a peculiar *disturbance* that forces a dwelling. On damage or unusability the tool becomes *properly present*, obtrudes into the foreground of the environing world — but the dwelling-with such a conspicuous thing is *not* a staring and beholding; it keeps the being-manner of concern (repairing, setting-right). The disturbance is present not as pure thing-alteration but as a *break of the familiar reference-totality*; all alteration in the world, down to mere change of something into something, is first experienced *through* this encounter-manner. Heidegger adds a decisive social point: the everyday commerce is not so much with an *own* world as a movement in a *common* surrounding-totality — "one" (das Man) moves in a world familiar "to one," without knowing the particular environing world of the individual. Even the workshop of a craftsman whose craft is wholly unfamiliar encounters not as a conglomerate of jumbled things but shows, in the nearest commerce-orientation, tools, material, finished pieces, work in progress; we experience primarily *the world of the man in which he lives* — foreign, yet as *world*, as closed reference-totality. And whatever encounters *innerworldly* as "hindering," "in the way" (of concern), is possible only on the ground of the specific presence of world as a firm, familiar reference-totality; only against a background of primary familiarity can there be such a thing as *not-belonging*. The broken familiarity, as broken, makes the standing-out of the otherwise faded, unobtrusive presence of world. Still more clearly: a surrounding — the most familiar — presses its presence upon us precisely when something in it is *missing*. Because the specific presence of the environing world lies in the familiar reference-totality, *missing* — always the absence of a belonging in the closed nexus — lets precisely the *unobtrusively present-at-hand* be encountered. Heidegger risks an extreme formulation: the specific *readiness-to-hand* of the equipmental environing world constitutes itself in *un*-readiness-to-hand — but he keeps to the positive sense: this specific absence points to what underlies it as its possibility, the *always-already-there* of a familiar reference-nexus that is disturbed when something is missing and that *lifts itself* through this specific absence.

**b) The interpretation of the encounter-structure: the founding-nexus of the encounter-characters.** Two things stand out. Environmental things encounter *in* references and *from out of* them; the adequate phenomenal presentification lets *world* encounter first, not an isolated thing — a priority of reference over the thing that shows itself in it. And *absence*, in the distinguished sense within the concerned world, is constitutive for the encounter of the otherwise inconspicuous world — absence having this encounter-function only on the ground of an *always-already-presence* of world; the reference-break (on missing) is what it is only as a break *of a reference-totality*. So the encounter of environmental things in their references accomplishes itself *from out of a reference-totality*: the references let the things be present, and the references are themselves made present — *appresented* (appräsentiert) — by the reference-totality. The graspability and thereby the objecthood of a thing is grounded in the encounter of the *world*; objecthood is *not* the presupposition of the encounter. The nearest present-at-hand is there in its "there" only from a "already-there" *with and before* it — not in the sense that factually several things are present and one goes from the nearest to another, but that it is *world* that appresents a world-thing; world-things do *not*, as real, compose reality.

To exhibit this, Heidegger gives the *work-world* (Werkwelt) a central position. Setting environing world and commerce of the simplest kind exemplarily — *craft and craftsman* — the *tool* has the being-character of the "in-order-to" (Um-zu); its circle of usability is narrower or wider (a hammer wider than a watchmaker's instrument). The narrower the circle, the more unambiguous the reference. In concern the tool is not investigated for its *look*; the genuine relation to it is *using* commerce, and it *goes up in the reference*. Something essential lies here: concern in a sense *looks away* from the tool-thing; as such a thing it is primarily not there at all but *used* as tool — "equipment-for" (Zeug-zu). Its genuine reality shows itself precisely in the *looking-away* from it as a merely present-at-hand thing; and using commerce is characterized by the omission of every *beholding objectification*, including objectification of the *reference* (the usability) in which concern dwells. To have the tool *in use* means precisely *not* to have the reference itself objectively. But the concernful *following* of the references has put the *work to be produced* into care — that to which the work refers in its usability. Commerce with the tool accomplishes itself as *going-up in the reference*, from the *already-having-present* of that toward which the reference goes, the work to be produced. This work is the concerned as such; it *bears* the reference-totality of the craft. And the work to be produced is itself, more exactly, a *work-world*: the work (shoe, table, clock) is itself of the character "conducive-to" (for wearing, for use, for time-telling), and it *is* what it is only from this usability, which in turn pre-sketches and modifies the mode of production. In simple craft-conditions each work even carries the reference to the determinate future *wearer and user* — cut to his body, made to measure. Even mass-produced goods do not lack the reference but have a characteristic one: an indeterminacy, a reference to indeterminate others.

The work-world *appresents*, along with the work's usability, the *world in which the users and consumers live*, and thereby these themselves. In this relation the own environing world is appresented as at once *being into a public world*: this public world is always already there with the work to be concerned, and *from out of it* the work-world encounters in an eminent sense. The boundary between own and public environing world can be fixed by modes of differing availability and by locality (the room one works in can belong to another), but at first these differences are not to be drawn into the analysis; only the one relation matters — that the nearest, own work-world *essentially* appresents a wider, public world, not occasionally but by its very sense. And the work-world shows still further essential relations. The work (the shoe) is usable *for* something, but producing it is itself a *using of something for* the shoe; the work bears in itself the reference of usability and has *materials* used in it — the shoe is *directed-upon* leather, thread, nails; leather from hides taken from animals bred by others; and these world-things (animals) *produce themselves* in reproduction and growth. Here is the reference to a worldly that, for the concernful commerce of producing, is *always already there from itself*. Likewise in the tools (hammer, tongs, nails): steel, iron, ore, minerals, wood — the reference strikes on beings that are, for concern, ultimately production-*un*needing and precisely therefore in an emphatic sense usable, always already present. So the work refers, along with the reference into the public environing world, into the *world of nature* — nature here in the sense of the world of the *available*, the determinate world of natural products. And the public environing world in turn lets *nature* encounter — not as object of nature-consideration. These reference-relations, and the peculiar co-presence of nature in our everyday environing world, are mostly *covered*: when we tell the time by the clock, we do not know that what we take into use is the *shown time*, that every day is oriented by official astronomical time to the sun's stand, that in every look at the clock we make use only of the *co-presence of the world-system*.

**The specific reality-character of the ready-to-hand.** The concerned world has within the environing world an in-each-case eminent *presence*; the work-world, wherein concern goes up, has a primary function in the encounter of the whole environing world. But the world to which concern is in each case *fallen* is *not* thematically perceived, thought, known — and precisely in this is grounded the possibility of an *original reality*: its presence means *non-objecthood as grasped*. Heidegger presses the thesis: the specific concerned world is that *from out of which* the world as a whole encounters; the worldhood of the world builds itself up *neither* from the nearest-given things or sensation-data *nor* from the always-already-present of a nature "existing in itself," but is grounded in the specific *work-world*. Following the work-world's encounter-function, we find two reality-characters characteristic of the whole environing structure: *readiness-to-hand* (Zuhandenheit) — the ready-to-hand as the nearest-available — and *presence-at-hand* (Vorhandenheit), the always-already-there. Own environing world, public environing world, and world as nature are *not* regions set side by side in themselves; they are environmentally present on the ground of a peculiar *change of presence*. The *concernedness* — the for-the-sake-of-which of concern, what is primarily put into care — lets everything toward which it is oriented, the reference-nexuses of serviceability and conduciveness, encounter, and these references in turn let what stands in them encounter. What is meant by "putting into care" and "standing in reference" can be clarified only later, from the *phenomenon of time*. Taking distance from the reversed interpretation — not explaining the encounter of the world from grasping, but understanding grasping as grounded in that encounter — it becomes clear that the *concernedness-presence* first brings to appearance what, oriented by apprehension-theory, one calls "the nearest given." (The ready-to-hand is thus *not* the present-at-hand plus a value-coloring; the present-at-hand of bare nature is the *founded*, de-worlded residue won by looking away from the work-world's references — the exact inversion of the traditional order that §22 traced in Descartes.)

**The sense of the encounter-structure as significance (Bedeutsamkeit).** To get clearer about worldhood in the direction of the reference-nexuses, the phenomenon of *reference* must be characterized more closely. "Reference" is a *formal* concept, taking on different sense when de-formalized. The reference at issue as the encounter-structure of world Heidegger now names *signifying* (bedeuten), and the encounter-structure of references as signifying he calls *significance* (Bedeutsamkeit). By indicating significance formally through reference, one misunderstanding is warded off: that "significance" says the environmental things have, over and above being natural things, a *meaning*, a rank and value. In natural speech "signify" does carry that; but the question is whether the phenomenon is *hit* or precisely *distorted* by interpreting it as nature-thinghood laden with *value-predicates* — indeed whether "value" is an original phenomenon at all, or something that arises only on the presupposition of a specific *nature-ontology*: the pre-assumption that things are first natural things and *then* have something like value, value taken ontologically as a specific back-reference to nature-thinghood. It may be unavoidable to regard values as values *once one sets being as nature in advance*. Significance says, negatively, nothing about meaning in the sense of value; positively it hangs together, much more properly, with meaning in the sense of *word-meaning* — the sense in which word-combinations can have meaning. Heidegger confesses the term is not the best but that he has found no other over years, above all none that expresses the essential connection of the phenomenon with *word-meaning*, with *discourse* (Rede) — a connection that will remain dark for now. Reference is a formal structure appearing in various phenomena — sign, symbol, symptom, trace, document, testimony, expression, remnant. Heidegger declines to pursue these, since a unified treatment lacks the ground he is still trying to win. And a decisive corrective on method: to understand the reality of the real, one must always look to the *structure of being*, not to the founding-relations of beings among themselves. So oriented, it is right to say that Dasein itself — ultimately the beings we call humans — is in its being possible only because *there is a world*; here lies a peculiar interlocking of the being of world with the being of Dasein, that Dasein is a being that is *in* its world but at the same time *on the ground of* the world in which it is. The basic structure of worldhood, the being of the being called world, lies in significance: the references and reference-nexuses are at bottom *meaning-nexuses*. Heidegger surveys the temptation (Leibniz's *characteristica universalis*, Spengler's symbol, Cassirer's *Philosophy of Symbolic Forms*, which extends Kant's critique of reason to a critique of culture) to take a formal thread — Gestalt, sign, symbol — as guide for interpreting the whole of beings; the universal applicability of such threads easily deceives about the *originality* of the interpretation reached, and what is apt for aesthetic phenomena can be the opposite of clarification for others.

#### § 24. The inner structuring of the question of the reality of the external world

Heidegger takes the significance-structure out of the distorting horizon of any theory of the *reality of the external world* or *ontology of actuality*. The preliminary clarification of significance *precedes* every such epistemology or ontology, along with the exposition of the question of being (the interpretation of Dasein); the question-nexuses epistemology (subject-object) and ontology (of nature) do not even *touch* the interpretation of Dasein on its being. He runs through five theses.

**a) The real-being of the external world is exempt from every proof and belief in it.** The question of worldhood is not the question *whether* an external world is real. That framing implies the reality of the world must and could be *proved*, or at least (as Dilthey held) that the *right* of our belief in the external world's reality is to be justified. Both are countersensical. To want to prove the presence-at-hand of world misunderstands the questioning itself, since such questioning has sense only on the ground of a being that has the constitution being-in-the-world; it is countersensical to put under an existence-proof what founds all questioning after, proving of, exhibiting of, a world. World is, by its ownmost sense, the *already-present for all questioning*. The question sustains itself only on a constant misrecognition of the being-manner of the one who poses it — a being for which it is *constitutive* that world is always already discovered. Heidegger inverts Descartes' formula: not *cogito sum* is the formulation of a primary finding, but *sum cogito* — and this *sum* not in Descartes' ontological indifference (present-at-hand-ness of a thinking-thing) but *sum* as an assertion of the basic constitution of my being: *I-am-in-a-world*, and therefore I can think it at all. Descartes wanted the sentence read the reverse way, so that one does *not* ask after the *sum* at all but takes consciousness as the inner, absolute starting-point — whence arise all the riddles of "inner" and "outer." The reality of the world is not the problem *whether* it is present; the question of the reality of world stands only as the question *how worldhood is to be understood*. And it is not enough to say the first question is "obviously" senseless; one must let its countersense *run up against* the phenomenon, from the positive sight of in-being and world — the ontic-existentiell self-evidence of the presence of world is given with the being of Dasein, but ontologically it is *riddling*.

**b) The reality of the real (worldhood of the world) is not to be determined from its being-object and being-grasped.** The question after the reality of the real cannot mean an inquiry into how the world "manages" to be. Such a question presupposes one understands what "being" means when explaining how the being *makes it* that it is — but that prior understanding already forbids asking so, since here *being* is taken as *a being* of itself, being explained *from beings*. To recognize the countersense of crediting being with a "trick" by which it is, and to refer such a question back to beings, does *not* imply that one can settle nothing about "being-in-itself" but only about beings insofar as they are grasped, objects in a consciousness — the familiar "sentence of immanence" (beings are only for a consciousness) on which epistemologies pro and con turn, without asking what "immanence" could mean. What the sentence at bottom *sees* is not that beings depend on consciousness in their being, but the phenomenal finding: *world encounters*. From the phenomenon the directive is given to interpret the *encounter-structure*; the more prejudice-free this is undertaken, the more properly the encountering being is determinable in its being. The being of beings does not *consist* in encountering, but the encounter of beings is the phenomenal ground — the only one — on which the being of beings becomes graspable. Precisely *because* beings are "in themselves," independent of any grasping, their being is findable only in encounter and explicable only from the phenomenal exhibition of the encounter-structure. "Explaining" is here inadequate, since explaining is a *derived, fallen* mode of interpretation: all explanation (nature-explanation) dwells in the *unintelligible*. Nature is the in-principle explainable-and-to-be-explained *because* it is in principle *unintelligible*; it is the unintelligible as such, because it is the *de-worlded world* — beings as discovered in physics, admitted only insofar as determined by invariant laws of motion. The sentences of physics are intelligible *as* sentences, but what they speak *about* is the unintelligible — the being that wholly lacks the character of Dasein. Dasein, by contrast, is in principle *intelligible*, since understanding belongs to its being as being-in-the-world; world is intelligible to Dasein insofar as it encounters in the character of significance.

**c) Reality is not interpreted through the "in-itself"; this character is itself in need of interpretation.** The "in-itself" is no original character; it has a phenomenal *genesis* and needs interpretation, though it is held to need none. One likes to characterize the reality of world with the "in-itself" and rests content with merely positing it because one brings it *reactively* into the field, against an interpretation of the being of world as being-grasped, against determining the actual as the objectivity of scientific knowledge; one appeals to the fact that all natural and scientific knowing aims at determining an in-itself being, but leaves *that* unquestioned. If the being of world were determinable only as being-grasped, the sole prospect of clarifying the "in-itself" would be an ever-greater *looking-away from the subject* — but how, without the basic constitution of in-being? Because the being of world becomes graspable *in encounter*, the understanding of the in-itself being opens only in a *radical interpretation of Dasein*. Because objects are independent of the subject, their being can be explicated only in *correctly understood subjectivity*, but cannot *consist* in being-a-subject.

**d) Reality is not primarily to be understood from the bodily presence of the perceived.** Bodily presence (Leibhaftigkeit) is a genuine phenomenal character insofar as I dwell in the mode of access of mere beholding perceiving — but precisely in this access, and still more if perception is grasped as simple *thing*-perception, the world is *no longer* accessible in its full worldhood, its full significance as it encounters concern. In pure thing-perceiving the world shows itself in a *deficient* significance (*deficiens*): something is lacking that world properly has and should have; the originality of the world suffers a break in the mere still-only-beholding of it as thing-manifold. The traditional categories of thinghood — which one determines, for definite reasons, as *the* being-categories: thinghood, substance, accident, property, causality — have their phenomenal genesis in this deficient significance; they are drawn from an access-mode (a fore-having of *presence* and its fundamental determinacies) that is in the process of a characteristic *de-worlding*. Why *these* categories are the first-discovered is the same question posed earlier: why does natural Dasein, in clarifying the world it is in, precisely *overleap* the environing world? The Aristotelian categories (*ousia, poion, poson, pou, pote, pros ti* — substance, quality, quantity, place, time, relation) are all drawn from the peculiar dimension of a mere thing-grasping and a determinate manner of speaking about it, the *theoretical assertion* — yet these became for Aristotle the being-categories *simpliciter* and the ground for the object-categories in general. So bodily presence too is no primary character of the environing world.

**e) Reality is not sufficiently clarified from the phenomenon of resistance as the object for drive and striving.** The Greeks had this in view when they set the *sōmata* (bodies), with respect to pressure and impact, as the proper *ousiai*. Dilthey pointed to resistance as the *correlate of impulse*: to every impulse issuing from and living in the subject a resistance is correlative. He saw early that reality is experienced not only in knowing but in the whole "living subject," this "thinking, willing, feeling being" — aiming at the *wholeness* of the subject that experiences the world, not a bloodless thinking-thing — but he tried it within a *traditional anthropological psychology*, and does not see that taking over that old psychology must drive him from the phenomenon. Scheler represents a similar theory on the clearer basis of phenomenological act-analysis, a "voluntative theory of existence": the presence of world is primarily correlative to *will*, impulse, striving; the being of objects is given immediately only in drive- and will-relatedness, not in any knowing. Heidegger notes his own agreement (he has taught the like for seven years, from the common root of Dilthey's impulse and the phenomenological question) and credits Scheler's work on the *biological* and on the specific function of the *lived body*. Yet resistance is *not* the original phenomenon: resistance can be understood only from *significance*, and the genuine correlation of world and Dasein (if one may speak of correlation at all, which Heidegger doubts) is not impulse and resistance, or will and resistance, but *care and significance* — the basic structure of life, which Heidegger also calls *facticity*. Something can encounter *as* resistance, as that against which I do not get through, only if I live in a wanting-to-get-through, a *being-out-for* something — i.e. only if something is already present for caring and concern, from whose presence there can first be a presence of the resistant. No resistance, however great, can *give* something objective: pressure and counter-pressure of material things never let anything like *world* arise; resistance is a phenomenal character that *presupposes* world. Moreover resistance, like Dilthey's version, is oriented only to the *correlation of acts*, forcing Scheler back to the old positing of a subject that has acts, and to the division *in mente / extra mentem*. Resistance and bodily presence alike are grounded in worldhood already being there; they are phenomena of an *isolated* encounter, isolated to a determinate access-mode of bare striving. Scheler's grasp of world-beings as resistance hangs together with his biological orientation — how world is given for *primitive living beings* — and Heidegger judges the path from the analogy of primitive organisms *fundamentally reversed*: only when we have grasped our own being-relation to the world can we perhaps, by modified consideration, determine the worldhood of the animal, not conversely. Finally: this grounds the requirement for all attempts to *decide* between realism and idealism. It is not a matter of settling them or finding a solution but of seeing that *both* rest on a neglect — presupposing concepts of "subject" and "object" without clarifying them from the being-stock of Dasein. Every serious *idealism* is right insofar as it sees that being, reality, actuality can be clarified only when the real is *present*, encounters; every *realism* is right insofar as it tries to hold fast the natural consciousness of the presence of world — but errs at once when it tries to *explain* this reality by means of the real itself, i.e. by a causal process. Realism is therefore, purely methodically, always a *deeper stage* than any idealism, however far the latter be driven toward solipsism.

#### § 25. The spatiality of the world

Heidegger did not start from *extensio*: against the whole tradition, spatiality is not the primary determination of the being of world, and the reality-characters of world are not founded on spatiality. The reverse question arises: whether spatiality is not to be explicated *from worldhood* — whether the specific spatiality of the environing world is to be made visible at the world *as environing world*, i.e. only when the environing-space is understood from within the worked-out significance. Space and spatiality *do* belong to world in some sense, are a *constitutive* of world; but the "around" (das Um) and the "round-about" are not primarily spatial, and not spatial at all if spatiality means the dimensionality of *metric*, geometrical space. Heidegger's analysis (developed under the headings of the "around"-character, the environmental as primary character of world and spatiality, and the *making-spatial* of the environing world as a *de-worlding* of the world) shows that the primary spatiality of Dasein is not the homogeneous manifold of geometry: the environing spaces (a house, a room) are articulated by what predominantly stands in care; I cannot *wander about* in this primary environing spatiality in which I constantly am, for it is the spatiality that belongs to *my* being. The metric, geometrical space is a *modification*, motivated out of a more original spatiality, and is won by the same de-worlding process that turns the ready-to-hand into the present-at-hand. (This inverts the Cartesian priority of extension yet again: geometrical space is not the being of world but a founded objectification of a prior, concernful, oriented spatiality of places and regions.)

#### § 26. The "who" of being-in-the-world: being-with and *das Man*

**a) Dasein as being-with; the being of others as co-Dasein (critique of the empathy-problematic).** The analysis of world had thematized the *wherein* of Dasein, but not all phenomena that showed themselves were lifted out. In the craftsman's environing world the *public world* appeared: in the concerned work, the material, the used tools, *others* are co-present — those for whom the work is, by whom the tool was made. In the world of concern *others encounter*; the encountering is a *co-being-there* (Mit-da-sein), not a presence-at-hand. Heidegger stresses that he *deliberately* narrowed the earlier analysis to environmental things — a violent narrowing demanded by the theme. But he also never set out from "at first I am alone in the world" or "at first only the I is given without world"; with the rejection of that starting-point and the uncovering of being-in-the-world, there can be no talk of an *isolation* of the I. Even "at first an I is given with my being-in-the-world" is a failed formulation; hence, in treating being-in-the-world, he always spoke of *concern*, of concerned going-up in the world, in which the *other is co-present* — never of a "subject" and "I" over against an "object." The indeterminacy in which the *who* was left, and the un-lifted-out character of the others, were *methodically intended*.

What does this indeterminacy of the "who" mean for the being of Dasein? Nothing other than: *Dasein, as being-in-the-world, is at the same time being-with-one-another — more exactly, being-with* (Mitsein). The phenomenological statement "Dasein as being-in-the-world is being-with others" has *existential-ontological* sense; it does not merely state that I factually do not occur alone and that other beings of my kind are present. If *that* were meant, I would speak of my Dasein as an environmentally present-at-hand thing, and being-with would be something Dasein has only in-each-case *on the ground of the presence-at-hand of others* — Dasein would be being-with only *because* others factually occur. Rather, *being-with means a being-character of Dasein as such, equiprimordial with being-in-the-world*, and it is the formal condition of the possibility of the *co-disclosedness* of the Dasein of others for one's own Dasein. This character determines Dasein even when no other Dasein is factually addressed or perceivable: *even the aloneness of Dasein is being-with in the world*. Aloneness means only a *deficiency* of being-with — the other is *missing* — and thereby the *positive* character of being-with is indicated. The missing of the other, which can be only insofar as my being is itself being-with, is a being-modification of my own Dasein and as such a *positive* mode of my being; *only as being-with can Dasein be alone*. Conversely, aloneness is not removed by a second specimen of the genus human standing beside me, or ten others; being-with-one-another does not ground in a being-present-together of like specimens of *subject-things*. Just as little as Dasein is at first a worldless subject to which world is added, so little does it first *become* being-with by another factually turning up.

This co-Dasein of others in *everydayness* characterizes in-being as going-up in the concerned world: the others are co-present in the world one is concerned with, even when not bodily perceived. My tool is bought *from* someone, the book is a gift *from*..., the umbrella is forgotten *by* someone. The dining table at home is not a round board on a frame but a piece of furniture at a determinate place with determinate places at which determinate *others* daily find themselves; the *empty place appresents* the co-Dasein in the mode of the *missing* of others. What is concerned can be present so that it looks like something to *benefit* others, to *impress* them, to *outdo* them — mostly without express knowledge. Everywhere in what we deal with, and in the world-things themselves, the others are co-present — those with whom one everydayly is. So the being of others encountering *in* the environmental things is not readiness- or presence-at-hand but *co-Dasein* (Mitdasein): even in worldly encounter, encountering Dasein does *not* become a thing but keeps its Dasein-character and encounters *from the world*. Others can encounter environmentally: with the ill-tended field I walk along, its owner or tenant is appresented; with the anchored sailboat, a determinate person who makes trips with it. But this encounter has a *different* appresentation-structure: these others do not stand in the reference-nexus of the environing world; they encounter in the *with-which* of their commerce (field, boat) *as those dealing with it* — as themselves in *their* being-in-the-world, not as occurring things but as those who till the field or sail. And insofar as they are so there for me, they are there *with* me, who myself have this being of being-in-the-world; they are with me in the *one* world. The distinction of personally meeting or being-away of the other moves itself on the ground of this environmental encountering-one-another. Even in the most everyday passing-and-dodging on the street lies this environmental encounter, from the *common street*; dodging has sense only for beings-with-one-another, for oriented, concernful being-in-the-world. The strangest human we meet is *with me in my world* and is experienced as such in the dodging; a stone or brick falling from the roof does not, strictly, move *past* a window. Everyday and far-reaching *not-mattering-to-one-another* is not *nothing* but a determinate *privation* of being-with as being-dependent-on-one-another; only because Dasein has the basic constitution of being-with is there for-, against-, and without-one-another, down to indifferent going-alongside-one-another.

Heidegger warns that all these phenomena are *equiprimordial*, not derived from one another; only from the basic constitution of in-being can they be understood, but they do not first set in over the course of some development. Being-with-one-another must be understood from being-in-the-world; he does *not* lay down a concept of the human and, because the human is supposedly a "social being," assert that the structure of being-with belongs to Dasein — rather, from the phenomenal stock of everydayness it becomes clear that not only the *others* but, remarkably, "*one oneself*" (man selbst) is there in what one everydayly concerns. And a decisive corrective to his own earlier lectures: the worldhood of the world appresents not only world-things (environing world in the narrow sense) but also, *though not as worldly being*, the co-Dasein of others and one's own self. So worldly encounter does not by itself decide the being-manner of what encounters; the encountering can be appresented as readiness-/presence-at-hand, co-Dasein, or self-Dasein. Heidegger explicitly retracts the terminology of his earlier lectures ("with-world" / "self-world"): it is *fundamentally false*, since the others, though they encounter worldly, never have the being-manner of world; the worldly *encounterability* of Dasein and co-Dasein is constitutive of being-in-the-world, but they never thereby become something *worldly*. Hence the term *being-with*, not "with-world." The world itself is never co-there, never co-Dasein; it is that *wherein* Dasein as concern in each case is.

**Critique of empathy.** As senseless as the presuppositionless starting-point "at first there is only a subject to which a world is then brought" is the assumption that at first a subject is given only for itself, and the question is *how it comes to another subject* — how, since at first only my own inner experiences are given, I can grasp or *empathize into* (einfühlen) the experiences of others. One sets out as though a subject were given encapsulated for itself, tasked with empathizing into another subject. This is a countersensical framing, because a subject, so posited, never *is* so. Once the constitution of Dasein is seen presuppositionlessly as in-being and being-with in the presuppositionless everyday, the *problem of empathy* is as countersensical as the *question of the reality of the external world*. Also: the experience of foreign and of one's own "soul-life" does *not* first require an experience-reflection in the traditional sense to grasp one's own Dasein; I understand the other not by empathizing into another subject but *from the world in which he is with me*, which is disclosed and intelligible through the aspect in being-with-one-another. Only because understanding is drawn *from world* is it possible to understand a foreign world, or a world mediated through sources, monuments, remains — for then I no longer have the persons to empathize into but *remnants of their world*. Within this intelligibility of world, un-intelligibility and *distance* are first possible. Rejecting the pseudo-problem of empathy does not mean being-with-one-another needs no phenomenal clarification; only the question of co-Dasein must be understood as a question *of Dasein itself*.

**b) *Das Man* as the who of everyday being-with-one-another.** The question returns: *who* is this Dasein in its everydayness? One must not fall into the deception that saying "Dasein is in each case mine — the being I myself am" already answers it. Precisely because the *who* asks after the who of its being, it determines itself from the being of Dasein, which in each case *is* in its being-manner what it is. Taking Dasein in the everyday, in concernful going-up-with-one-another in the world: *the who of everyday being-with-one-another is "das Man"* — the *anyone*. In the immediacy and mostness of everyday concern, Dasein is *what it pursues*: one *is* what one does; the everyday self-interpretation takes the horizon of interpretation and naming from what is concerned — one is shoemaker, tailor, teacher, banker. Here Dasein is something *others* too can be and are. And in concern the others are present not only as those for whom the concerned is usable and conducive, but also as those who concern themselves with the *same*; in both respects being-with them stands in a *relation to them* — one's own concern is, with respect to the others and to what they pursue, more or less successful, more or less outstanding, lagging, esteemed. The others are, in concern, not simply present but the concern lives constantly in a *care about a difference* from them, even if only to *equalize* the difference (the lagging Dasein wanting to catch up, or, in priority over them, out to hold them down). This being-structure Heidegger calls *distantiality* (Abständigkeit) — Dasein's care about *distance* — regardless of whether it is known; on the contrary, precisely when everyday concern does not know it, this being-manner is perhaps all the more stubbornly and originally there. (These determinations carry, he stresses, *no moral evaluation*; they characterize movements Dasein makes in its everydayness.)

One's own concern — Dasein as being-with — has put the others into its care in this way; more adequately: *Dasein as being-with is lived by the co-Dasein of the others* and by the world it so concerns. Precisely in its ownmost everyday pursuit, Dasein as being-with is *not itself*; the *others* are the ones who live one's own Dasein. And these others need not be *determinate* others; every other can represent this other. The in-each-case *who as this one* is precisely *indifferent*; what matters are the *others* to whom one's own Dasein itself belongs — these others, to whom one belongs and which one *is* in being-with-one-another, are as it were the "subject," what constantly is there, that pursues every everyday concern. Insofar as Dasein, in concerning its world, is being-with and as such goes up with the others in the world, this common environing world is at once the *public* environing world of which one makes use, to which one takes account, in which one moves so-and-so. Here everyone is just as much the other as I; being-with-one-another *dissolves* one's own Dasein completely into the being-manner of the others, so that the others in their differentness *disappear all the more*. In the circle of its being-possibilities each is *completely the other*. Here the peculiar "subject" of everydayness — *das Man* — first has its full dominion: public being-with-one-another is wholly *lived out of* this anyone. *One* enjoys and amuses oneself as one enjoys; we read and judge literature as one judges; we hear music as one hears; we speak about something as one speaks.

This *das Man*, which is nothing determinate and which "all" are (though not as *being*), dictates the being-manner of everyday Dasein. It has its own ways to be: *distantiality*; *averageness* (Durchschnittlichkeit) — an existential determination of *das Man*, that about which it essentially cares, keeping to the average of what is fitting, what one lets count, watching over every pressing exception, so that every exception is short-lived and silently held down and everything original is smoothed *overnight* into what is accessible to all and closed to none; and *leveling-down* (Einebnung), given in *das Man*'s going-up in the world, the leveling of all differences. Between distantiality, averageness, and leveling there is an *existential connection*. *Das Man*, as what forms everyday being-with-one-another in these ways, *constitutes* what Heidegger calls, in the proper sense, *publicness* (Öffentlichkeit). Herein lies: the world is always already primarily given as the *common* world; it is *not* that at first there are individual subjects each with its own world, to be then shoved together by some agreement into a common world — as philosophers imagine when they ask after the constitution of the *intersubjective* world. The *first* given is the common world — *das Man* — the world in which Dasein goes up, so that it has *not yet come to itself*, and can constantly so be *without having to come to itself*. Dasein at first does not live in its own and nearest; at first and everydayly the own world and the own Dasein are the *farthest*: one *is* the world in which one is with one another, and only out of it can one grow, more or less genuinely, into one's own world. This common world, primarily there, into which every growing Dasein first grows, regulates as public *all* world- and Dasein-interpretation; it gives the claims and needs in advance, keeps right in everything — *not* on the ground of an original being-relation to world and Dasein, but by *not entering into the matters*, by insensitivity to every difference of level and genuineness. (This is the answer to the "who": in everydayness Dasein is *not* the isolated I of the tradition but the anyone — an ontological structure of being-with, the ground on which any subsequent talk of conformism or mass society becomes possible, but not itself such talk.)

### Chapter Four: A more original explication of in-being: the being of Dasein as care

#### § 27. In-being and care: outline

The analyses of world and *das Man* were always liftings of the one structural whole, being-in-the-world. That *das Man* is only a determinate way of being-in-the-world, and that the *who* is in each case a way to be (authentic or inauthentic), shows that the being of Dasein is ultimately to be determined from *in-being as such* — and that only the right explication of this basic phenomenon guarantees the founding of the other equiprimordial structures. Heidegger sharpens the sense of "in": the being of Dasein is not the being-manner of world (neither present- nor ready-to-hand), nor the being of a "subject" (which would again be tacitly taken as presence-at-hand). If one held to the orientation to "world" and "subject," one could say: the being of Dasein is the being of the *"between"* subject and world — a between that does not first arise when a subject meets a world, that *is* Dasein itself, yet again not as a property of a subject. Strictly, Dasein may *not* be grasped as a "between," since talk of a "between" already presupposes two beings given, between which a relation should hold; in-being is *no* between of real beings but the being of Dasein itself, to which a world in each case co-belongs. Hence it is always wrong (if one wants to speak conceptually strictly) to call human Dasein a *microcosm* over against the world as macrocosm, since the being-manner of Dasein is essentially different from every kind of *cosmos*. Heidegger names the being-structure sought *care* (Sorge), and orders the explication of in-being fourfold: the phenomenon of *uncoveredness*, *falling* as a basic movedness of Dasein, the structure of *uncanniness* (from being-at-home — familiarity), and *care*. The order shows a founding-nexus among them.

#### § 28. The phenomenon of uncoveredness (*Entdecktheit*)

The analysis of *das Man* showed that in the concerned, public world Dasein itself — "one oneself" — is *co-uncovered*: the world is not only disclosed in its significance as the oriented wherein of Dasein's being, but Dasein is itself *there for itself*, in its co-being-alongside what it concerns, and is in a certain sense uncovered. These two — the disclosedness of world and the co-uncoveredness of in-being — make up the unitary phenomenon *uncoveredness*. (Heidegger notes that the terms *Entdecktheit* and *Erschlossenheit* are not yet as fixed here as in *Sein und Zeit*: here the tendency is reversed, *uncoveredness* ascribed to Dasein as existential, *disclosedness* to world.) The point of the term: this is not a *thematic knowing* of world or self but the being-structure of Dasein that first founds such knowing and makes it possible for world, as disclosed, to encounter into a "there." The "there" is the being itself we call Dasein: the "here" and "there" of the demonstrative adverbs are possible only because there is something like a "*there*" at all, which "there" is our being toward being-with-one-another. A material thing occurring in the world is never itself a "there" but encounters *into* one. Hence Dasein means, terminologically, not to occur "here" and "there" but *to be the "there" itself* — Dasein *brings its there along from the outset*, not as a dead property but as what to-be, namely to be its there, is the very being-sense of Dasein. And this "there," per the analysis of *das Man*, is at first always the *co-there with others*, the publicly oriented there, in which every Dasein constantly remains, even when it withdraws wholly onto itself.

**a) The structure of Dasein's uncoveredness in its world: disposedness (Befindlichkeit).** Concern goes up in significance; it is concernful dwelling-alongside the world in its conduciveness, usefulness, and the like. Insofar as world so encounters, it *addresses itself* constantly to a being that is dependent on the world, that has the sense of *caring*, of being-in-care about something. This concernful dependence is constantly *approached* (angegangen) by the world so or so — the world *concerns* it, encountering not a mere beholding of something present-at-hand but a *caring being-in-it*, so that being-in-the-world is, as it were, constantly *called upon* by the threateningness and unthreateningness of the world. In all commerce Dasein as in-being is in some way approached and called upon (a *mode of disposedness*), be it only as the undisturbedness of performing, the calming evenness of an unthreatened using, the indifference of everyday dispatching of what is put into care. These indifference-characters can at any time be replaced by unrest and anxiousness, or by unburdenedness and letting-loose to flying frenzy. Such being-approached is possible *only* because concernful being-in-the-world is, from the outset, *addressable* through threateningness and unthreateningness — through the world as significance. And only because *Dasein is itself, at itself, care* is the world experienced in its threateningness, its significance. This does *not* mean the caring Dasein "subjectively" apprehends the world so — a complete inversion of the facts; rather caring in-being *uncovers* the world in its significance. In every such being-lifted or being-depressed the same phenomenon shows itself: that in all commerce Dasein always *finds itself* so or so, in this or that mood. The "*self*" in "finds itself" does not yet mean a formed, thematically known "I"; it can be, and in everyday going-up in *das Man* is, this *das Man* itself in its indeterminacy. This co-uncoveredness of being-in-the-world in being-approached is possible only because Dasein from the outset always *finds itself* in each of its ways to be, because Dasein is itself uncovered for itself. This basic form of the primary co-uncoveredness Heidegger names *disposedness* (Befindlichkeit). A stone never finds itself but is merely present-at-hand; a wholly primitive unicellular organism, by contrast, already finds itself, however dull and dark — essentially different, in being-structure, from the mere presence-at-hand of a thing. Heidegger chooses the term to avoid, from the outset, any *reflection* on oneself: disposedness is a basic manner of the being of Dasein, of its in-being, and does *not* first find itself alongside itself and then look around for a world — it is a character of *in-being*, of always-already-being-in-a-world. Disposedness is the *apriori for uncoveredness and disclosedness*, an equiprimordial character *beside* understanding.

**b) The being-enactment of uncoveredness: understanding.** With disclosedness and disposedness, possibilities are given for Dasein to *be* its there, its uncoveredness, so or so; the being-enactment of these possibilities Heidegger calls *understanding* (Verstehen). The genuine definition of understanding is thus won from the being-structure of Dasein itself, *not* as a mode of knowing — even if one takes knowing as a being-manner of Dasein. Understanding is a being-manner of the being of the character of in-being: it is the being-alongside disclosed concernability, and a *disposed* being-alongside that always co-uncovers itself — understanding as disposed disclosing and having-disclosed of world is, as this, a *disclosing self-finding*. As uncoveredness makes up the being-structure of the *full* Dasein-constitution (world, in-being, every way to be), so the corresponding enactment, understanding, always extends to the *full intelligibility* — world, co-Dasein, and one's own Dasein. This is an *apriori* proposition for understanding, without which one constantly goes astray: it is a deception, bound up with ignorance of the genuine Dasein-structure, to think there is a *separate* understanding of mere world or of foreign Dasein. From this structure follow the decisive guide-points for all problems of *hermeneutics*: hermeneutics is possible only from the explication of Dasein, to whose being-manner understanding belongs; and only with the circle of intelligibility pre-sketched by uncoveredness (world, in-being, co-Dasein) is there the possibility of an *unintelligible*, and thereby access to the in-principle-uninterpretable, to *nature*. Only because *history* is — Dasein being the primarily *historical* being — can something like *nature* be uncovered; only therefore are there natural sciences. Herein is grounded the so-called *circle in understanding*: one finds a circle only when one does not see that to every understanding belongs understanding of world, Dasein, and co-Dasein. So it is no accident and no mere inconvenience that in the historical disciplines one says something "unfortunately depends on the historian's personal standpoint," which one must accept, though the ideal would be freedom from that subjectivity. This view is *countersensical*: the ideal is precisely that the understanding Dasein *belongs* to the understanding of itself, and the consequence is not to lament but to see there a *task* — to bring Dasein itself into the appropriate being-manner of understanding, so that as understanding it can gain access to the matter to be understood. The primary sense of "understanding": when I say to another "you have understood me," I mean "you know where you stand, with me and with yourself." Understanding is the uncoveredness of *what one is at* with something — the uncoveredness of the *involvement* (Bewandtnis) that it has with the environing world, with my own Dasein, with the being of others. Understanding is subject to the same modifications as uncoveredness: because it underlies the possibility of *semblance*, every understanding needs *appropriation, consolidation, safekeeping* — understanding can slip, and the understood can be *distorted*, become inaccessible; understanding becomes non-understanding. This does not mean nothing is there any longer (countersensical, since uncoveredness always belongs to Dasein); rather something *more fundamental than nothing* is there: *semblance-understanding*, the looking-as-if this non-understanding were itself a genuine one. In Dasein itself lies the possibility that it brings itself into deception.

**c) The forming-out of understanding in interpretation.** Understanding forms itself out in *interpretation* (Auslegung); interpretation is the enactment-mode of the being-enactment of uncoveredness, and *the basic form of all knowing*. Interpretation as such does not properly *disclose* — that is done by understanding, by Dasein itself; interpretation only ever accomplishes the *lifting* of the disclosed, as the forming-out of the possibilities proper to an understanding. The nearest everyday mode has the functional form of *appresentation* — appresentation of significance, i.e. the lifting of the accessible reference-nexuses. The child's question, what a thing *is*, is answered by giving *what it is used for*, determining the found from what one does with it; with such interpretation the thing first comes properly as present, intelligible, into the environing world, once one has *entered* into the involvement it has. Interpretation appresents the *what-for* of a thing and lifts the reference of the "in-order-to"; it brings to the lift *as what* the world-thing is to be taken, i.e. understood. The primary form of all interpretation, as the forming-out of understanding, is the *addressing of something from its "as-what,"* the addressing of something *as* something — an appresenting in the discussing of what is thus primarily and guidingly appresented. This "addressing of something as something" need not necessarily express itself in the *linguistic form of a sentence*; the grammatical sentence-form is always only an expression-form of the primary and proper "sentence," namely this addressing of something as something. In this lifting the significance comes, as it were, *to word*, and only then arises the possibility of distinguishing the lifted meaning *as word-meaning* from the meant thing. Here lies something essential: *word-expression — language — is only insofar as there is this addressing*, which is possible only insofar as there is interpreting, which is only insofar as there is understanding, which is only insofar as Dasein has the being-structure of uncoveredness, i.e. is itself determined as being-in-the-world. With this founding-nexus — addressing, interpreting, understanding, being-uncovered, in-being, Dasein — *language* is defined, or rather the horizon is given from which the essence of language can first be seen. Language is nothing but a *distinguished being-possibility of Dasein itself*.

**d) Discourse and language.** Language *makes manifest*; it does not first produce uncoveredness but presupposes uncoveredness and its being-enactment (understanding, interpreting) as conditions of the possibility that something can become manifest. These enter, as being-conditions, into the essential definition of language; and the *apriori* of the Dasein-structures must underlie the science of language. Heidegger treats everyday *discourse* (Rede) under the moments of *hearing*, *keeping-silent*, *discoursing and idle talk*, and *discourse and language*. Two points bear on what follows. In *hearing*, we hear first the *said*, not the being-said as such; hearing is not the reception of bare phonetic data but is always already understanding hearing, hearing *of* something discoursed. And in *keeping-silent* (Schweigen) — genuine silence, not mere muteness — Dasein can give the other to understand more than in many words, precisely because keeping-silent is a positive mode of discourse grounded in having something to say. The decisive phenomenon for what follows is *idle talk* (Gerede), treated in the next section.

#### § 29. Falling as a basic movedness of Dasein

**a) Idle talk (Gerede).** As discourse, idle talk has the being-tendency of uncovering; the everyday interpretedness lives in idle talk. Its forms differ, but always they are those of a specific being-in-the-world: Dasein comes toward *das Man*, which takes over what "one" holds to be the proper events in which Dasein decides. Heidegger illustrates polemically: today one decides about metaphysics — or higher things — at *congresses*; for everything to be done there is first a conference; one comes together and keeps coming together, and all wait for the other to say it, and if it is not said that too is of no matter, for one has now "spoken oneself out." However little the speakers may understand of the matter, one is of the opinion that through the *accumulation of non-understanding* an understanding finally springs out. So there are people who travel from conference to conference with the consciousness that something is really happening, that they have *done* something, while at bottom one has *shirked the work* and seeks in idle talk a lodging for one's own — unadmitted — helplessness. (No moral sermon is intended; the point is a phenomenon constitutive of Dasein-structure. Ancient *sophistry* was, in essential structure, nothing else. The supposed being-in-on-it is dangerous above all because one is *bona fide*, believing all is in order.) For discourse to *cover*, to interpret deficiently, no *express intent to deceive* is needed; the *groundless being-said and being-passed-on* suffices to modify the being-sense of discourse — interpretation as the forming-out of uncoveredness — *into a covering*. Idle talk is, from the outset, on the ground of its own omission of the original understanding addressing, *covering at itself*: as a communicating, it places before the disclosed or to-be-disclosed matter *a view, an opinion*. The deficient mode of disclosing world is *distortion*; the corresponding mode of covering *disposedness* is *inversion* (Verkehrung) — by pre-giving determinate ways of being-attuned, a disposedness can form that inverts a Dasein into a foreign one, so that one is no longer who one properly is. Idle talk covers *more* than it uncovers — above all by *retarding* the uncovering, through its accompanying pretension of *having-already-uncovered*. Insofar as Dasein at first dwells in *das Man*, and *das Man* has its interpretedness in idle talk, the very being-tendency of Dasein toward *das Man* shows the tendency to *covering*; and since this covering sets in *against all express intent*, there is manifested in it a being-structure given with Dasein itself — the *pushing-away of Dasein from itself*, from its proper original disposedness and disclosedness. That Dasein, in its being as everydayness, pushes itself away from itself: this being-manner is *falling* (Verfallen). The term names a *being-movedness* of Dasein-happening and is not to be taken as an evaluation, as if it marked a temporary bad property to be lamented and perhaps abolished in advanced stages of human culture. Falling means, like uncoveredness, being-with, and in-being, a *constitutive* being-structure of Dasein — a specific phenomenon of in-being in which Dasein at first constantly has its being. Oriented to the "between," the dwelling in *das Man* and in idle talk is this being's *uprooted state of suspension*; and this uprooting is precisely what makes up the *solid everydayness* of Dasein, a mode of falling in which Dasein loses itself.

**b) Curiosity (Neugier).** A second mode of uprooting shows in *curiosity*. All concern is uncovering and interpreting, appresenting its disclosed work-world in its references, and has its *orientation and guidance* — the *circumspection* (Umsicht) oriented to concernedness-presence, which gives all grasping and performing the path of accomplishment, the means, the right occasion and time. This "sight" of circumspection is the formed possibility of concernful uncovering, *seeing*; and "seeing" is not restricted to the eyes but taken in the wider sense of concernful appresentation. Heidegger recalls Augustine's *concupiscentia oculorum* (*Confessions* X): "seeing" properly belongs to the eyes, yet we use the word for all the senses when we take them as knowing — we say "see how it sounds, see how it smells, see how hard it is" — so that the general experience of the senses is called *lust of the eyes*, the other senses appropriating to themselves, from a certain likeness, the office of seeing when it comes to *cognitio*. And Aristotle's *Metaphysics* opens: "all humans by nature stretch toward seeing" (*eidenai*, not to be translated "knowing") — for Aristotle curiosity becomes an original comportment from which the theoretical, *theōrein* (in the Greek sense), is motivated. When everyday concern *comes to rest* — as interruption or as being-finished — this coming-to-rest is a mode of concern, since in rest care does not vanish; only the world is no longer appresented for concernful accomplishment but encounters a *resting tarrying-alongside*. In this tarrying the sight of circumspection *becomes free*, unbound by the specific references of the work-world; and this freed seeing is still care — care now lays itself into the freed bare seeing and perceiving of world. But seeing is a *perceiving of the distant*: the care of such freed seeing is a *concern of the distant*, a leaping-over and springing-away from the nearest concerned everyday work-world. The resting tarrying becomes, as free-floating seeing, a *no-longer-tarrying-alongside the nearest*, the care of discovery and nearing of the not-yet-experienced, the care of the "away from" the constantly and nearest ready-to-hand. With rest, the care of curiosity becomes *free* — "free" meaning it is always already there, only bound, only following the encountering world by seeing. Its first structure is *not-tarrying* (Unverweilen): not dwelling with something thematically grasped but the characteristic *springing-away* from one to another. What curiosity appresents does not become *thematic*; it appresents something merely *to have seen* it, in order to move on again to the next. The *for-the-sake-of-which* of curiosity is not a determinate presence but the *possibility of the constant change of presence*; the not-tarrying of curiosity at bottom concerns the *not-having-to-grasp*, the bare *being-entertained* by the world — a being-in-the-world with a characteristic disposedness of *unrest and excitement*, undisturbed by the urgency and need of everyday Dasein, harmless and non-binding. In short: the not-tarrying concern of seeing-just-to-see concerns *distraction* (Zerstreuung). Not-tarrying and distraction form in Dasein a peculiar *unsettledness* (Aufenthaltslosigkeit): the care of nearing ever-new world and foreign Dasein (someone constantly wanting to meet new people), only in the tendency to have something new present, reveals a possibility in which Dasein *loses itself* from the everyday-familiar into the unfamiliar — a mode of uprooting in which it is everywhere and nowhere, tending to get free of itself. In such curiosity Dasein organizes a *flight from itself*. *Das Man*, which in idle talk determines the public interpretedness, at once governs the paths of curiosity: it says what one must have seen and read, and what curiosity discovers comes into idle talk; there is no side-by-side of the two, but one uprooting-tendency drags the other along.

**c) Ambiguity (Zweideutigkeit).** In everyday being-with-one-another, everything is accessible to everyone and everyone can talk about everything; soon it is no longer decidable *who* lives from genuine understanding and who not. What plays out in the atmosphere of public interpretedness becomes *ambiguous*: it looks as if genuinely seen and discussed, and at bottom is not; and it looks as if not, and perhaps is. Ambiguity concerns not only what is accessible to use and enjoyment but extends further: everyone knows in advance what is to be done, what is not yet at hand but "properly ought to be made"; everyone has "already sensed it in advance." This *being-on-the-track* (from hearsay) is the most insidious way ambiguity holds Dasein down — for supposing the sensed were one day *realized*, ambiguity has already seen to it that interest in the realized matter *dies off*, since the interest existed only in the sense of curiosity and idle talk, as long as the possibility of a non-binding *co-sensing* was given. Idle talk concerns only the *being-able-to-co-sense* without having to grasp; being-in-on-it while on the track fails the *following-through* when the sensed is to be carried out, for carrying-through demands Dasein's *being-thrown-back onto itself*. Publicly, such being is boring; and idle talk, faced with a carrying-through, is always at hand with "one could have done that too — one sensed it along." Because the *time* of the Dasein that sets itself in, in the reticence of carrying-through, is another, publicly a slower one than that of faster-living idle talk, the idle talk has long since arrived at the newest, while what was long sensed now comes, in view of this newest, *too late*. So idle talk and curiosity see to it that the genuinely new-created is *eo ipso outdated* for publicness and mostly first becomes free in its positive possibilities only when the covering hold of idle talk has become ineffective — which happens in the process of the genuine freeing of the past *as history*: *genuine historiography* is nothing but the struggle against this being-movement of Dasein in the sense of covering through its own public interpretedness. Second, ambiguity dominates *being-with-one-another* itself: the other is co-there from what one has heard about him, what one talks about him; between the original being-with-one-another (from the matter concerned together) *idle talk shoves itself first of all*. Everyone watches how the other will comport himself; being-with-one-another in *das Man* is not an indifferent side-by-side but a *tense watching-of-one-another, a secret mutual eavesdropping*. This can creep into the most immediate relations, so that a *friendship* consists no longer in a resolute, mutually freeing setting-in together in the world, but in constant prior watching how the other manages with what one means by friendship — the being-with-one-another a *secret against-one-another under the mask of a for-one-another*. Dasein in *das Man* moves in a *whirl* that whirls it into *das Man*, constantly tearing it from the matters and from itself.

**d) The characters of the proper movedness of falling.** Falling shows in its structure a proper movedness. Since *das Man* is a being-manner that Dasein itself *temporalizes*, it forms with idle talk, public interpretedness, and ambiguity a possibility of *missing itself*, seeking itself only in *das Man*. This being-manner does not consist through circumstances and accidents from the world; falling Dasein is itself *tempting* (versucherisch), presenting itself the possibility of falling. As tempting, it holds itself fast in its fallenness, since the pretension forms that with going-up in *das Man* the full wealth of Dasein's ownmost being-possibilities is *guaranteed*. So the tempting falling is *tranquilizing* (beruhigend), and thereby breeds a growing *un-needfulness* ever to bring the being in *das Man* before a question or to modify it. This tranquilizing is not a standstill but a *creeping intensification*: in the tranquilized self-evidence of such a being, Dasein drives toward *alienation* (Entfremdung) from itself, so that it leaves itself no being-possibility open except that in *das Man*. The characters — tempting, tranquilizing, alienating — make clear: the Dasein essentially given over to the world *entangles itself* in its own concern, and can drive this tendency so far that it *bars itself* the possibility of returning to itself, no longer even understanding such a return.

**e) The fundamental structures of Dasein from the horizon of fallenness.** From falling itself the fundamental structures of Dasein become visible. The leveling and disappearing of Dasein in *das Man* is a *covered decay* of Dasein, which temporalizes itself as a *falling-off* of Dasein from its *authenticity* into falling. *Authenticity* (Eigentlichkeit) must be understood in the literal sense: *having-oneself-as-one's-own, alongside-oneself*. The falling-off is a *falling constitutive for Dasein itself*, insofar as it is a being of the character of being-in-the-world and Dasein is in each case mine. Such a being-tendency of falling is *a priori* possible only on the ground of a *hang* (Hang) toward it; this hang, to which the analysis of falling phenomenally comes back, makes up a basic structure Heidegger calls the *proneness* (Verhängnis — used not as a "fatality-fact" but, like *Begegnis*, *Erkenntnis*, as an *existential structure*): the flight of Dasein from itself, into the world it has itself uncovered. A hang is, in its being, nothing original; it points back to a possible *urge* (Drang). Hang and urge are, in turn, to be understood only from the more original structure to which they belong — *care*.

#### § 30. The structure of uncanniness (*Unheimlichkeit*)

The explication of the falling-movedness as *flight of Dasein from itself* leads to the phenomenon of *anxiety* (Angst) as the basic disposedness of Dasein toward itself. Heidegger builds up to it through *fear*, arguing that fear is a *derived* phenomenon grounded in anxiety: *anxiety is not a mode of fear; rather all fear is grounded in anxiety*. He treats fear (with Aristotle's *Rhetoric*, and the theological tradition of *contritio*) as *being-afraid-of* something *innerworldly* — the before-which of fear has the character of the worldly-encountering, the being-character of significance, something detrimental (a *kakon*) drawing near. Fear always includes a *fearing-about* (oneself); and there is a *fearing-for* another, a distinguished mode of being-with: I fear for him from what threatens him worldly, and this fearing-for is a genuine mode of being-with, possible even when the other does not fear himself (indeed then I am perhaps rightly in fear for him, because the endangerment does not encounter *him*, because he is blind to it or reckless). The threatened in fearing-for is the *co-Dasein* of the other, and therein my own being-with him — yet in this fearing-about-and-for the other I do not *properly* fear *for myself*.

**b) Anxiety and uncanniness.** Beside all modifications of fear there is a being-afraid that at bottom may no longer be so called, because its *before-which can remain indeterminate* — no longer this or that worldly present-at-hand. The threatening is nothing determinate-worldly and yet not without the characteristic *nearing*; this indeterminate threatening can be *quite near*, so that it *presses in*, so near and yet not present as this or that, not a fearsome from a determinate reference of the environing world. In the midst of the most familiar environing world — often not even needing the accompanying phenomena of darkness or aloneness — anxiety can "come over" one; we say then, *it becomes uncanny* (unheimlich): one is no longer at home in the nearest-familiar environing world. Not that a determinate circle of the previously known world fails in its orientation, not that one would be at home *elsewhere* — rather, *being-in-the-world as such becomes a complete "not-at-home" simpliciter*. And the before-which of anxiety: when anxiety has released, we say "it was really *nothing*," and this speech hits the fact exactly. It was nothing; *the before-which of anxiety is the nothing* — nothing worldly, nothing determinate — yet insofar as it can be there obtrusively, it is *more* than a threateningness for fear: it is *the world in its worldhood itself*. The indeterminacy of the before-which, this nothing as nothing-worldly, is *phenomenally wholly determinate*: it is the world in its worldhood, which does not give itself like a world-thing. This nothing as the threatening is quite near, so that it "lays itself around one and takes the breath," without being something of which one could say "this here." And the *about-which*: threatened is not this or that concern but *being-in-the-world as such* — and to being-in-the-world belongs the world in its worldhood. The before-which of anxiety (nothing worldly) is the *wherein* constitutive of Dasein, of in-being itself; the about-which of anxiety is *this same being-in-the-world itself*, in its primary uncoveredness of the "not-at-home." So in anxiety the before-which and the about-which *coincide* — more exactly, in anxiety it does not yet *come* to such a separation: the before-which and the about-which is *Dasein itself*. In anxiety being-in-the-world as such discloses itself, not as this determinate fact but in its *facticity*. *Anxiety is nothing other than the disposedness in uncanniness.*

The before-which and about-which are both Dasein itself — more exactly, the *fact that I am*: I am, namely, in the sense of the *naked being-in-the-world*. This naked facticity is not the presence-at-hand of a thing but the being-manner constitutive of self-finding. Dasein is, in a radical sense, "present" — in the sense of *facticity*. It does not merely find itself present-at-hand as ground-and-soil *that* it is; the ground is an *existential*, disclosed ground — and indeed an *abyss* (Abgrund). This is the *existential positivity of the nothing of anxiety*. Facticity, as a constitutive of existence, is not grafted onto a present-at-hand; the human is not existence as the *combination* of present-at-hand soul and present-at-hand body — rightly understood existence is not the combination of separated things but the *original being-manner* that ontologically determines this being. Dasein is such that it *is* this peculiar factical, i.e. its facticity itself; *that* Dasein "is" at all and "is not not" is not a mere property at it but can be experienced by it in an original experience — the disposedness of *anxiety*. And this experience *can* (though it need not, as all being-possibilities stand under a "can") set in, in a distinguished sense, in *death* — more exactly, in *dying*: this Heidegger calls *death-anxiety* (Todesangst), wholly to be distinguished from *fear of death*, since it is not fear *of* death but anxiety as the disposedness of the naked being-in-the-world itself, the pure Dasein. There is the possibility that precisely in the moment of *going-out-of-the-world*, where the world and every other has nothing more to say to one, the *being-in-it and the world simpliciter* show themselves. Heidegger notes the phenomenon was long seen, if not conceptually: touched in Augustine's *De metu*, treated by Luther on *contritio* and *poenitentia* in his Genesis commentary, and made thematic by Kierkegaard in *The Concept of Anxiety* — whose defect is that the conceptual, existential structure of Dasein is not properly seen, so that anxiety becomes a *psychological* problem.

**c) A more original explication of falling and anxiety (uncanniness) in view of the basic constitution as care.** Anxiety is anxiety *before this being itself*, so that this being-anxious-before-it is a *being-anxious-about* this being. Herein lies: *Dasein is the being to which, in its being — in its being-in-the-world — its own being is at issue*. That is the sense of the *sameness* of the before-which and the about-which of anxiety. This sameness must not be understood as a *fusion* of the two structural moments; it consists precisely in the *essence of anxiety being Dasein itself*. In the disposedness of anxiety, Dasein, as it were, occurs *twice* — but this formulation is the most inverted; it can only serve, preparatorily, to point to the peculiar fact that Dasein is a being in whose being its being is at issue. Against this seems to speak *falling*, the flight of Dasein from itself — for in falling everydayness *moves away from itself*, so that one cannot say Dasein is, in everydayness, *alongside itself*. But that was a blind, unphenomenological argument: *in falling — in the flight from itself — Dasein is precisely constantly there for itself*; in the flight from itself it comes, as it were, *after itself*, so that in falling it unexpressly, constantly *sees* itself, if in the insidious mode of a *not-wanting-to-see*. And whither Dasein flees — into the being of *das Man* — is itself only a being-manner of Dasein, a determinate being-at-home. So falling does not *impair* the fact that Dasein is a being for which its being is at issue; rather falling first becomes *intelligible* from it.

#### § 31. Care as the being of Dasein

**a) The determination of the articulated structure of care.** How is this peculiar being-structure — that Dasein is a being to which, in its in-being, its being itself is at issue — to be grasped more closely? Terminologically, *care* (Sorge); Heidegger marks it as a *pre-structure* (Urstruktur) in the sense of Dasein itself — not the *last* nexus of the being of Dasein (that will be time), but the *penultimate* phenomenon through which the proper being-structure can be reached. Care is the term for the being of Dasein *simpliciter*, with the formal structure: *a being to which, in its being-in-the-world, this being itself is at issue*. This shows precisely that "being" is *not* a simple, let alone the simplest, concept — the tradition's most *fateful* error, grounded in starting the determination of being from beings *as world* and formally abstracting the being of world (worldhood) from every determinate world-thing to reach a formal concept.

Already the determination shows that care, which properly grasps *being*, has a *manifold structure*. Indeed care was in play from the start: *concern* (Besorgen) is only a being-mode of care — care qua structure of Dasein *is* in-being as concern; caring that is in the world is *eo ipso* concern. The expression "it is at issue" (es geht um das Sein) is now determined more exactly. That Dasein's own being is at issue for it presupposes that in Dasein there lies something like a *being-out-for* (Aussein auf): Dasein is out for its own being, out to *"be"* its being. Care, as such being-about, is this being-out-for the being that this being-out-for itself is — such that Dasein, as it were, *takes itself in advance* (nimmt sich vorweg). If the being of Dasein is what care is at issue about, then Dasein has always already *held its own being ahead of itself*, though not in the sense of a thematic consciousness. The innermost structure of this caring of Dasein about its being can be formally grasped as: the *being-ahead-of-itself* (Sich-vorweg-sein) of Dasein. This being-ahead is not a psychological process or a property of a subject but a *moment of the being* that, by its sense, is *in the world* — that, as long as it is at all, is *always already alongside something*, namely the world. So the total structure of care in the formal sense: *the being-ahead-of-itself of Dasein in its always-already-being-alongside something*. This formal structure concerns *every* comportment; there are only different modes of the individual structural moments, so that care can take on the being-manners of *urge* or *hang*.

Care has the formal character of *being-ahead-of-itself-in-already-being-alongside something*. The being-ahead means the structure that care is always *being-about* something, such that Dasein, in concern — in every performing, procuring, and producing of something determinate — at the same time *co-concerns its Dasein*. This being-ahead-of-itself means that care, or Dasein in care, has *thrown its own being ahead of itself as existential facticity*. The being-out-for the own being, at issue for it, always accomplishes itself already *in a being-alongside something*, out of an always-already-being-in-the-world-alongside. (In-being is therefore constitutive for *every* being-manner of Dasein — even the authentic one.) In the structure of "being-out-for something I do not yet have, but being-out in an already-being-alongside," the phenomenon of the *not-yet-having* of something one is out for comes to light — which Heidegger names *want* or *privation* (Darben): not a sheer objective not-having, but always a not-having *of something one is out for*, and only thereby is the wanting, the lacking, the needing constituted. This basic structure of care will later lead back to the being-constitution we come to understand as *time*.

**b) The phenomena of urge and hang.** In the two structural moments — being-ahead-of-itself and already-being-alongside — lies a riddling character proper to care that, as will be seen, is *nothing other than time*: the peculiar character of the *"ahead," the "before."* Care in this structure is the *condition of the possibility* of urge and hang, and *not* conversely composed out of them. *Urge* (Drang) has the character of the "toward" something, and this toward shows a *pressing* moment coming from the "toward" itself; urge is a "toward" that brings the drive along from itself. Seen on care: in urge the pressing-character and the being-out-for something come to light; care modifies itself to be *predominantly* in these two moments. *As urge, care crowds out* (verdrängt): the crowding-out concerns the remaining structural moments given in care — they do not fall away but are *there as crowded-out*, i.e. (since Dasein is determined by uncoveredness) *covered*. Crowded out in urge — since urge takes over the primary being-manner of Dasein — is the already-being-alongside something and the alongside-which, and likewise the express being-ahead-of-itself. In urge, care is merely still concern of a "toward and nothing else"; urge as such *blinds*. One says "love makes blind" — but there love is meant as *urge*, and a wholly different phenomenon is supposed of it; love, by contrast, precisely makes *seeing*. Urge is a being-mode of care — the *not-yet-freed* care — but care is *not* an urge: in urge the full structure of care does not yet come into its proper being, for urge is only care of the "toward," at any price, in blind disregard of everything else. *Hang* (Hang) differs from urge and is likewise a modification of care, but in *other* of its structures — precisely in what urge crowds away: the *already-being-alongside* something. As in urge lies a specific exclusiveness of the drive-like "only toward, at any price," so in hang an "only" — namely "*only-always-already-being-alongside* something." Hang is a being-out-for the *staying-away* of Dasein from itself in the being-alongside; the "only-always-already-being-alongside" is also a "toward," but a "toward" not determined by drive — a "toward" of *letting-oneself-be-drawn* from an alongside-which. And as urge covers the being of care by crowding-out, so hang too covers — the care of the "being-able-only-to-let-oneself-be-drawn." (Urge and hang are thus not the elements from which care is assembled but *founded modifications* of care in which one or another of its moments predominates and covers the rest — and both point ahead to the temporal "ahead" that is the theme still to come.)

**The Cura fable.** Heidegger reports that the thesis "the being-structure of Dasein is care" is a *phenomenological* self-interpretation, not a pre-scientific one like "life is care and toil" — in the latter a next everyday aspect is voiced, in the former a basic structure is struck. He insists it is drawn from the matters, not thought up from a standpoint ("I have no philosophy at all"), and that he first struck on the phenomenon of care seven years earlier while trying to reach the ontological foundations of Augustinian anthropology. He then found a naive *self-interpretation of Dasein as care* in an old fable of Hyginus (Fable 220, "Cura"): as "Care" crosses a river she shapes a figure from clay; Jupiter gives it spirit; a dispute over its name among Care, Jupiter, and Earth (Tellus, who gave the body) is settled by Saturn (Time): Jupiter shall receive the spirit at death, Earth the body, but *Care*, since she first formed it, shall *possess it as long as it lives*; and it shall be called *homo*, from *humus* (earth). Such interpretations have the value of springing from an original naive look at Dasein — the being that in life belongs to *care*.

#### § 32. The result and the task of the fundamental Dasein-analysis: the working-out of the question of being itself

The interpretation of Dasein in everydayness brought to light the fundamental constitutions — being-in-the-world, in-being and being-with, *das Man*, uncoveredness, understanding, falling, care. The last shows the *rootedness* of the manifold of structures always said to be equiprimordial: equiprimordiality means they always already belong to the phenomenon of care, invested (though un-lifted-out) *in* it. They are no arbitrary additions to something that would first be care without them, nor something that first forms itself *by composition* into what we call care; whenever the being of Dasein is interrogated, it is always already meant in the equiprimordiality of these structures. *Dasein is neither a coupling of comportments nor a composite of body, soul, and spirit* (whose unity-sense is then sought in vain), nor a subject or consciousness that occasionally acquires a world, nor a center of acts from which these shoot forth (neither the being of the center nor of the acts being determined). The exhibited structures are themselves being-manners of this being, understood only from the being always already meant with them — *care*. Dasein understands itself, from itself, as care. Care is thus the *primary wholeness* of the being-constitution of Dasein, which as this wholeness lays itself in each case into this or that way of its being-able-to-be; *in every being-manner of Dasein this being-wholeness as such is wholly there*. What is won with care is no abstract, general concept underlying every way to be as a genus, nor a resultant of the interplay of ways to be; the interplay of the various being-manners is only what it is as the in-each-case *counter-play of the primary wholeness-structures of Dasein itself*.

Why was the analysis undertaken? To work out the question of being as such — to win the *concrete foundation* for every possible being-research, the foundation for the question "what does being mean?" — an answer not formulated in a formal sentence but one that pre-sketches concrete paths for being-research. Heidegger sharpens the phenomenological ground-question methodically: *phenomenological research is interpretation of beings toward their being*. Such interpretation places into its *fore-having* (Vorhabe) a being or being-region; this is interrogated toward its being, and the *look-toward* — that toward which it is seen and to be seen — is *being*, placed into the *fore-sight* (Vorsicht). The question after the being of beings is a transparent and secure guide *only when that toward which beings are interrogated, being itself, is sufficiently worked out and conceptually determined*; the more original and unprejudiced the working-out of what is placed into fore-sight, the less use is made of accidental, opaque, seemingly self-evident and worn concepts, the more surely concrete being-research reaches its ground and stays ground-standing. This phenomenon "being," which precedes all being-research as determining, must be worked out — and that requires the interpretation of *questioning* itself, i.e. clarifying what questioning, understanding, look-taking, experience of beings, and the being of beings mean: everything meant by *Dasein*.

### Chapter Five: The laying-free of time itself

#### § 33. The necessity of thematically positing the phenomenological interpretation of Dasein as a whole. The phenomenon of death

The phenomenological interpretation of Dasein stands, as interpretation, under the guide-thread given with the interpretation-structure itself: it must ask whether it has, *from the outset*, brought its theme — Dasein — originally and genuinely into fore-having, i.e. whether, with the starting-point of the analysis, the *whole* being in its *wholeness* came to fore-having; for only if the being is made thematic *as a whole* is there the guarantee of reading off its wholeness of being. The success of the preparatory interpretation grounds in Dasein *in its wholeness* being won from the outset in the thematic positing. So: has the previous consideration taken Dasein in its wholeness and kept it, so that the wholeness of its being can be read off? This *transitional consideration* asks: does this phenomenon give the wholeness of the being-structures?

The problem is that Dasein's being as care is, *as long as it is*, always *unfinished* — something is always still *outstanding* (aussteht); there is always a *not-yet*. When Dasein *is* finished — an end one calls *death* — nothing is outstanding at it, but with this "nothing-more-outstanding" it is also *no longer Dasein*: with reaching its wholeness, and precisely in it, it becomes *no-longer-Dasein*; its wholeness makes it *vanish*. So Dasein as a whole can *never* be forced into fore-having. And even if it could somehow, the earlier determination holds: Dasein is essentially *in each case mine* — an uneffaceable character, and only because Dasein is essentially mine can I *lose* myself in *das Man*. When Dasein reaches wholeness in its death, it is precisely, *as mine*, no longer experienceable by me: *in wholeness no understanding self-finding is any longer possible*, for the being that should find itself is, with reached wholeness, precisely *no longer*. There is thus a *fundamental impossibility* of finding oneself in Dasein's wholeness — an impossibility anchored not in some irrationality of experiences, nor in the limits of our cognitive powers, nor in the unsuitability of the dying-moment for phenomenological inquiry, but *solely in the being-manner of this being itself*.

An apparent way out: since Dasein is being-with, the *others* with whom this Dasein once was, or the still-being Dasein as being-with, could survey the deceased Dasein as *finished* and read off the wholeness of such a being. But making the finished Dasein of *others* a substitute-theme is a *bad expedient*, for reasons of principle. *First:* the deceased other's Dasein too is a *no-longer-being-in-the-world*; when they have died, their being-in-the-world *as such* is no more; their being is no longer being "in," "alongside" a disclosed world; their still-being-in-the-world is a *mere presence-at-hand of a corporeal thing* — here the peculiar *turn* of a being from the being-manner of Dasein to the merely-present-at-hand becomes especially clear, the extreme counter-case; strictly, with the dying of the other, beings are still present-at-hand, but *not their Dasein as such*. *Second:* the expedient presupposes that any Dasein can be *represented* by any other. Representation *does* belong to being-with in everyday concernful going-up-in-the-world — I can represent the other *in* what he does, in his concerned world and in the concern itself (one is what one does). But representation is always only "*in*" something, oriented to a concern, a determinate *what*. And it *fails simpliciter* where it concerns the being that makes up Dasein's *end* and gives it its wholeness: *no one can take the other's dying from him*. One can indeed *die for* another — but this always means *for the sake of a determinate matter*, in the sense of concerning the other's being-in-the-world, and does not mean the other's death is taken *away* from him. *Dying every Dasein must take upon itself as itself*; more exactly, every Dasein, insofar as it is, has already taken this being-manner upon itself. *Death is in each case mine — it belongs to me insofar as I am.* The primary difficulty is not securing Dasein as an *object* of consideration (that is secondary) but whether the being that is in each case mine *has the possibility of being its wholeness*.

Yet this impossibility yields something *positive*: the understanding of a *difference* in Dasein's being-manner from the being we call *world-thing*. When Dasein reaches the being-mode of "nothing more outstanding," i.e. is *finished as Dasein*, then in its being-finished it *no longer is* what it is: being-finished, said of Dasein, means *no-longer-being*. A world-thing (table, book, tool), by contrast, can fulfill its function only when *finished*, present-at-hand; being-finished, said of a present-at-hand world-thing, means precisely *first coming to be present and available*. Two different phenomena of reaching-wholeness, then; the difference concerns the *relation of the reaching-of-wholeness to the being of what has become whole*. So the impossibility of experiencing Dasein as a whole must be shown *expressly from the phenomenon of Dasein itself and from death as a being-manner of this being* — and only when this is phenomenologically clear does the impossibility stand scientifically, setting an unsurpassable *limit* to the inquiry. To secure ourselves scientifically of this, the *phenomenological concept of death* must be worked out — death as a *pure phenomenon of Dasein*, understood from the being-structures already exhibited. And the phenomenologically pure execution shows the remarkable thing: *the said impossibility is mere semblance*. The genuine interpretation of death is the *only* way to open the sight for Dasein having a being-possibility *to be itself genuinely in its wholeness*; and in the genuine being-wholeness that reveals itself with the correctly seen phenomenon of death, the *primary being-wholeness itself* shows itself — the working-out of death as a Dasein-phenomenon leads *eo ipso* before the being of this being, so that the wholeness of the being of Dasein is understood.

#### § 34. The phenomenological interpretation of death as a Dasein-phenomenon

Starting from the difference between the being-finished of tools/world-things (which means *first* being present-at-hand) and of Dasein (which means *no-longer*-present-at-hand): the latter is at first taken as the *entering* of what was hitherto still outstanding. But so, Dasein is grasped as a *sequence-nexus* of comportments and processes finding a conclusion at a moment, a wholeness that first becomes full with the entering of the missing *remainder* — a wholeness of the sense of a *composite* (Kompositum), death seen from the being-structure of *world-things*. **Death is neither a wholeness-constitutive for a whole in the sense of a composite** — such that with being-finished it would *no longer be* — but, if death is a being-character of Dasein, cannot be conceived primarily from the presence- and absence-of world-things. Dasein *as care* is *being-toward* something. *Death is not something still outstanding at Dasein but something that impends* (bevorsteht) — and *constantly* impends, as long as Dasein is. As always-already-impending, death belongs to Dasein itself *even when it is not yet whole, not yet finished*, i.e. not in dying. Death is *not a missing part for a whole as composite* but *constitutes the wholeness of Dasein from the outset*, so that Dasein first has, *from this wholeness*, the being of its in-each-case parts, i.e. possible ways to be.

Even so, "something that impends" does not belong to death *alone*; much in Dasein "impends." The characterization must be sharpened: death is not an *outstanding objective event* that parataxically joins the run-off sequence of states, but something *the care itself is out for* as an impending, unavoidable. And the decisive question: is this impending an *event that encounters me*, that strikes me as something *foreign* from the world — or is it something I shall *never encounter* at all, but that in a certain way *I myself, as Dasein, am*? Only then is death understood as a character of Dasein: not a *worldly* impending encounter that first collides with me, but grasped from the being-structure of Dasein, from *care*, from the *being-ahead-of-itself*.

*With death, which is in each case only as my dying, my ownmost being — my being-able-to-be at every moment — impends for me.* The being I shall be in the "at last" of my Dasein, that I can be at every moment — this possibility is that of my ownmost "*I am*"; *I* shall be my ownmost I. This possibility — death as *my* death — *I myself am*. *Death in general there is not.* Care, as being-ahead-of-itself, is at once *being-possible*: "I can" — more exactly, *I am this "I can" in a distinguished sense*, namely "*I can die at every moment*." This is a being-possibility in which I always already am; a *distinguished* one, for I *am* myself this constant, *utmost* possibility of myself, namely *no-longer-to-be*. Care, essentially care about the being of Dasein, is *innermost* nothing but this being-ahead-of-itself in the utmost possibility of one's own being-able-to-be. *Dasein is thus essentially its death.* With it, nothing *worldly* impends; Dasein itself *impends for itself*, not in some arbitrary possibility but in that of its *no-longer-being-there*. Insofar as Dasein, qua being-possible, essentially *already is* its death, it is as Dasein *always already whole*. Because Dasein means "being-ahead-of-itself as care," it can, *from itself*, be its being *whole* at every moment of its being.

Heidegger marks what this interpretation *cannot* do. It gives *no content-description* of death (causes, ways of dying, how people comport themselves in dying) — that would teach less about death than about the *life* of those people, and could be carried out only on the guide-thread of the strict concept of death itself. And it does *not prejudge* a *stance* toward death: nothing is decided about whether something comes after death, or what, or whether nothing comes; nothing about the beyond and immortality, nor about the here — not how one *should* comport oneself. Yet the explication accomplishes itself in *the most radical this-sidedness* (Diesseitigkeit): in view of what the death of *this Dasein* can be. Such this-sided interpretation so little prejudges the traditional questions of immortality and resurrection that *only with it* is the sense and ground for eventually asking them given at all. As long as speculation moves in confused, mythical folk-concepts of death, it stays groundless; only the phenomenological concept of Dasein and death is the presupposition for even *meaningfully* posing the immortality-question — which does not, however, belong within a philosophy that understands itself.

To win the phenomenological concept of death is to make visible the *being-manner of Dasein in which it can be its utmost possibility*. Here: the being-relation to a possibility that a being *itself is* (Dasein and its death) is itself *being-possible* — being a possibility means essentially *this being-able-to-be-possible*. So Dasein *can* be this utmost possibility either so or so; and it is at the same time constantly the possibility of its death, since death is constitutive. Dasein is this possibility *even in its everydayness*.

**a) The utmost possibility of death in the being-manner of everydayness.** Everydayness determines itself through going-up in *das Man*. In publicness, death is a *firm everyday encounter*, interpreted as "*one dies too, sometime*." This "*one dies*" harbors an *ambiguity*: this "one" (*das Man*) is precisely what *never dies and can never die*. Dasein says "one dies" *because* therein is said "*no one* dies," i.e. in each case not I myself. In "one dies," death is *leveled* in advance to a being-possibility that is in a certain sense *nobody's* possibility — and death, in what it is, *pushed away* from the outset. A further ambiguity: "one dies, but *for the time being* death is not yet coming" — one speaks as if death must first come *from somewhere*, whereas Dasein itself *already is* this its possibility. This pushing-away has the character of *tranquilizing*: the public self-interpretation goes so far that in being-with one even *talks the dying person* into believing he will soon recover, return to everydayness, supposing this to *console* him — whereby Dasein is only pushed back into going-up in the world, the specific situation of its being kept covered. The same public interpretedness settles *in advance* what to make of *thinking about death*: it counts publicly as *cowardly anxiety*, gloomy world-flight; publicness does not let *courage for anxiety before death* arise but operates a *forgetting* of it, interpreted as self-security and superiority over this alleged darkening of life — characters of the being-manner of *das Man*, the way of everydayness in the being-manner of *falling*.

But precisely in this *not-wanting-to-think* of death, Dasein *attests death itself its being*: what Dasein flees from in its falling everyday flight, even without express thinking of death, is *nothing other than Dasein itself, insofar as death is constitutive for it*. Conversely, death is not first *in* Dasein by its thinking of it. And falling, as covering, moves in a *re-interpreting*, a *not-letting-be-seen* of what death is — which implies a *prior and constant seeing*: what it conceals is its *own being*. The inconspicuous concern of not-thinking-of-death covers a basic character of death, its *certainty* (Gewißheit). With the ambiguity "one dies sometime," this certainty is re-interpreted into *uncertainty*: one breaks the point off the certainty in the public interpretedness that says "everyone will have to face it sometime" — an assertion about death addressed, as it were, to *no one*, when precisely the sense of death is that it is the being-possibility *of myself*. *This certainty, that it is I myself in my coming-to-die, is the basic certainty of Dasein itself* and a genuine Dasein-assertion, whereas the *cogito sum* is only the *semblance* of one. If such pointed formulas say anything, the adequate one, concerning Dasein in its being, must read: ***sum moribundus*** — and not *moribundus* as gravely ill or wounded, but: *insofar as I am, I am moribundus* — the *moribundus* first gives the *sum* its sense. The uncertainty with which Dasein covers its original being-certainty is further consolidated by *reckoning* that now — by common estimation, as one is used to seeing things — death is in no case to be expected; one calculates the *when* of death's coming and overlooks that this *indefiniteness* — that death can come at *any moment* — belongs *essentially* to its certainty. This indefiniteness, positively, means the possibility that death can come *any moment*; it in no way weakens the certainty *that* it comes but first gives it its *sting* and the character of an *utmost and constant* possibility that Dasein *is*. These two characters — that death is *absolutely certain*, and that this certainty is at once *indefinite* — make up the being-manner of this possibility. *Death is the utmost, though indefinite, yet certain possibility, in which Dasein in everydayness flees* — flees by making it *ambiguous*. So everydayness does *not* have the ownmost, most original being-relation to death, since a being-character of death — that it is *in each case mine* — is precisely overlooked or covered.

**b) Dasein's authentic being-relation to death.** The authentic being-possibility death is grasped only when the being-relation to this possibility understands it as a *certainty of my being* — a certainty of the character of the *indefinite*, and a certainty of the being that is *mine*. Is there a being-possibility in Dasein in which it gains an *authentic* being-relation to death? The being-relation to a possibility must let the *possibility stand as possibility* — not so that the possibility becomes *actuality* (as by bringing oneself to die in *suicide*, which precisely *gives up* the possibility as possibility, inverting it from the ground into an actuality). The possibility is only what it is *while it remains*, i.e. remains *impending*. A being-relation to it must be such that in it *I precisely am the possibility itself*. This means it cannot be a *wanting-to-possess* the possibility, in the way worldly concern appresents and makes the concerned *available*; rather, being must *run ahead* (vorlaufen) to the possibility that is to remain what it is — *not draw it near as present but let it stand as possibility* and so be toward it. In such *running-ahead into the possibility* I come, as it were, into the *nearest nearness* to it — yet in this coming-near it does *not* become *world* but *ever more and more properly only possibility*. This possibility, into which I can run ahead, is essentially and in an extreme sense *mine*. The possibility of the dying going-out-of-the-world is determined, as being-in-the-world, by the fact that the *world is then only present-at-hand as the wherein I leave*; in dying the world is only what has nothing more to say for my own being, what Dasein as being-in-the-world *gives up*, no longer dependent on — world is only the *pure wherein of just-still-being*. Herein lies: the utmost possibility of death is the being-manner of Dasein in which it is *simpliciter thrown back upon itself*, so absolutely that even *being-with*, in its concretion, becomes *irrelevant*. Dasein remains, even in dying, essentially being-in-the-world and being-with others, but its being now *properly first lays itself into the "I am"*: only in dying can I, as it were, *absolutely* say "*I am*." The utmost possibility of death, as the being of Dasein in which it is *wholly from itself*, is to be *grasped in Dasein itself*; and since Dasein is in everydayness, that means it must *fetch itself back* out of everydayness into the utmost possibility of the "I am." *Running-ahead to death at every moment of Dasein means the fetching-back of Dasein out of das Man, in the sense of self-choosing.*

#### § 35. The phenomenon of wanting-to-have-conscience and of being-guilty

With the *choosing of myself as my possibility*, I choose my being. This possibility chosen in running-ahead to death is a *certain* one and, as certain, at once *indefinite*. Self-choosing in running-ahead must, as *authentic*, come into an adequate being-relation to the characters of the possibility: the indefiniteness of death is grasped when I have understood the possibility *as possibility for every moment*, i.e. when, in having chosen myself, I am *absolutely resolute* (entschlossen); its certainty is grasped when every other possible being-able-to-be of myself is *subordinated*, i.e. when *resoluteness toward oneself* is such that only *from it* the possibility of this or that determinate *action* springs. If Dasein can bring itself, in running-ahead, into such absolute resoluteness, that means: *in this running-ahead to its death, Dasein can make itself responsible in an absolute sense* — it *can* choose the being-presupposition of itself, namely *choose itself*. And in this choice nothing other is chosen than *wanting-to-have-conscience* (Gewissenhabenwollen). The choice need not accomplish itself *only* in this running-ahead — wanting-to-have-conscience can be actuated otherwise too — but insofar as it is a matter of choosing oneself in the *full transparency* of Dasein *as a whole*, there is only this one possibility of running-ahead to death, to choose Dasein *in its being itself* and not merely for the next two days. *Running-ahead is the choice of wanting-to-have-conscience.* But the *acting* one, as Goethe already said, is always *conscienceless* (gewissenlos) — and one can *properly* be conscienceless only if one has chosen wanting-to-have-conscience. The acting one is conscienceless, i.e. becomes, in being-with-one-another, necessarily *"guilty"* (schuldig) — not in the sense of committing this or that misstep, but as *acting being-with others*: as such, Dasein is *eo ipso guilty*, even — and precisely then — when it does *not know* that it harms another or destroys another in his Dasein. *With the choice of wanting-to-have-conscience I have at once chosen the having-become-guilty.* The genuine being-manner of Dasein toward its utmost and ownmost possibility (the ownmost being-ahead-of-itself, accomplished *from itself*) is the characterized *running-ahead of wanting-to-have-conscience*, which at once means *the choosing of the essential being-guilty of Dasein itself, insofar as it is*.

#### § 36. Time as the being in which Dasein can be its wholeness

Running-ahead into the ownmost being-possibility is nothing other than the *being of my ownmost becoming-of-being* — the *future*. The being-guilty co-posited in it is the being of the *ownmost having-been-ness* (Gewesensein); and the being of having-been-ness is *past* (Vergangenheit) — such that *in* such a being I am myself, and nothing other than, the *future* of Dasein and therewith its *past*. **The being in which Dasein can authentically be its wholeness, as being-ahead-of-itself, is *time*.** Not: *time is*; rather, *Dasein temporalizes qua time its being*. Time is nothing that occurs somewhere *outside* as a frame for world-events, and just as little something that reels off somewhere *inside* in consciousness; it is *what makes possible the being-ahead-of-itself-in-already-being-alongside — the being of care*. The time we everydayly know and reckon with is, seen more exactly, nothing other than *das Man*, to which Dasein in its everydayness is fallen: being in being-with-one-another in the world — and that means also in the *co-uncovering of the one world* in which we are — is being in *das Man* and a determinate kind of *temporality*. The movements of nature, which we determine spatio-temporally, do *not* run off "*in* time" as "in" a hinge; as such they are wholly *time-free*; they encounter only "*into*" time, insofar as their being is uncovered as pure nature. *They encounter into the time that we ourselves are.*

*Here the lecture course breaks off.* Heidegger had reached, at the very end of the semester, the identification of time with the being of care and of Dasein — the threshold that *Sein und Zeit* would cross with the full analysis of temporality (*Zeitlichkeit*) as the meaning of care, the ecstatic-horizonal unity of future, having-been, and present. The promised Second and Third Parts — the destructive history of the concept of time in Bergson, Kant/Newton, and Aristotle, and the working-out of the horizon for the question of being and the being of history and nature — were never delivered. What GA 20 leaves us is thus the first, and in its Husserl-engagement uniquely full, execution of the "preparatory description of the field in which the phenomenon of time becomes visible": the existential analytic of Dasein, carried from the critique of phenomenology's unclarified sense of being, through being-in-the-world, worldhood, *das Man*, and care, to the point where care shows itself as *time* — the being in which Dasein can be its own wholeness, and from which, had the course continued, the question of the sense of being was to have been unfolded.

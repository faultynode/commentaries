# ***Kritik der reinen Vernunft (pp. 1-190)***

*Based on the Meiner edition (Philosophische Bibliothek 505), edited by Jens Timmermann, which prints the A and B editions in parallel.* 

## **Dedication**

The dedication is addressed to Karl Abraham Freiherr von Zedlitz, Prussian Minister of State for ecclesiastical and educational affairs under Friedrich II and a personal patron of Kant. The text opens by identifying the promotion of the growth of the sciences with Zedlitz's own interest: Zedlitz, Kant says, is bound to the sciences not merely by his elevated position as their official protector, but by the much more intimate relation of a lover and enlightened connoisseur. The conceptual move here is significant. Kant declines to flatter Zedlitz as a mere institutional figure; he distinguishes the externally official relation (the post of *Beschützer*) from an inner relation of *Liebhaberei* and *Kennerschaft*. The latter, he insists, is the genuinely intimate (*innigst*) bond. The dedicatory rhetoric is thus already calibrated by Kant's lifelong distinction between merely external and properly intrinsic determinations — a distinction that will reappear, in transposed form, throughout the Critique itself (e.g., in his treatment of authority through reason rather than through office, in the preface).

The second paragraph performs the strictly performative act of dedication. Kant says he dedicates this *second* edition under the same gracious attention with which Zedlitz had honored the first, and along with it he dedicates all the remaining concerns of his literary vocation (*meiner literärischen Bestimmung*). The phrase *literärische Bestimmung* is worth dwelling on: *Bestimmung* in Kant's idiom denotes a vocation, a destination, a calling-toward-an-end, and here he marks his entire intellectual production as one continuous task with a single addressee in the public sphere. The closing — "Königsberg, den 23sten April 1787" with the signature *unterthänig-gehorsamster Diener / Immanuel Kant* — uses the formula of submissive obedience standard for early modern address to ministers, while the editorial apparatus notes that the original 1781 dedication of the A edition had a different wording (more elaborately metaphysical: about the "speculative life" being content under modest wishes with the approval of an enlightened judge), which Kant replaced in 1787 with the present more compact formula. Already in this small editorial change one sees Kant's increasing willingness to allow his system to speak for itself and to drop the somewhat baroque self-justifications of the first version.

## **Preface \[A\]**

The preface to the first edition opens by stating the distinctive fate (*besondere Schicksal*) of human reason in one species of its cognitions: namely, that reason is burdened (*belästigt*) with questions which it cannot dismiss, since they are imposed upon it through the very nature of reason itself, yet which it also cannot answer, since they exceed every power of human reason. This opening is one of the most famous passages in the whole Critique and it states the basic problem-situation with austere precision. Two features must be marked. First, the questions are not external impositions, not curiosities or accidents of culture; they are *aufgegeben durch die Natur der Vernunft selbst* — *given as a task* by reason's own nature. Reason is constitutively self-tasked. Second, the same nature that imposes them prohibits their answer in the only direction reason has at its disposal: theoretical knowledge. This *Doppelnatur* of reason — task-giving without answer-providing — is what the entire Critique will undertake to diagnose and to discipline. The verb *übersteigen* ("they exceed/overstep all capacity") foreshadows the technical sense of *Transzendenz* in the Dialectic: an objective use that goes beyond the bounds of possible experience.

Reason falls into this predicament, Kant goes on, without any fault of its own (*ohne ihre Schuld*). It begins from principles whose use in the course of experience is unavoidable and which experience sufficiently confirms. With these it climbs (*steigt sie immer höher*) toward ever more remote conditions; since its work would otherwise remain incomplete (because questions never cease), it is forced to take refuge in principles that exceed all possible experiential use yet appear so unsuspicious that even common human understanding agrees with them. But thereby it plunges into obscurity and contradictions, from which it can indeed gather that hidden errors must lie at the bottom, but which it cannot uncover, since the principles it employs — going beyond the boundary of all experience — recognize no touchstone of experience. The architecture of the description is decisive: there is a *unavoidable* upward movement of principles, an unsuspicious appearance, a transition into a region beyond experience, and a consequent loss of the only available criterion. Kant is here giving an aetiology of metaphysical contradiction. He locates the *origin* of metaphysical error not in carelessness, but in the legitimate ascent of reason itself when it is not yet aware of the discipline of its own bounds. The phrase *Probierstein der Erfahrung* — touchstone of experience — already names the criterion which empirical realism preserves and which transcendental illusion suspends.

The battlefield (*Kampfplatz*) of these endless disputes, he concludes, is now called *Metaphysik*. This is the first naming of the discipline, and the choice of *Kampfplatz* is not accidental: the word is military, agonistic, and implies that we have not yet a science but a contest of factions. The image will recur in the second preface and in the closing of the Doctrine of Method as the polemic of pure reason: metaphysics has the shape of a war because, lacking a *Probierstein*, every faction can with formal correctness deduce its conclusions, and none can be decided except by a meta-level critique. The diagnostic move from "the questions are unanswerable" to "metaphysics is a battlefield" is thus already the move from psychology of reason to history of philosophy — the *factum* of perennial dispute is the empirical sign of the structural defect Kant has just described.

There was a time, Kant continues, when metaphysics was called the queen of all sciences, and, if we take the will for the deed, on account of the eminent importance of her subject-matter she certainly deserved this title of honor. Now the fashionable tone of the age (*Modeton des Zeitalters*) is to show her every contempt, and the matron, cast out and abandoned, laments like Hecuba: *modo maxima rerum, tot generis natisque potens — nunc trahor exul, inops* (Ovid, *Metamorphoses* XIII 508–510: "lately the greatest of things, mighty in so many sons of her stock — now I am dragged into exile, destitute"). The Hecuba reference is a learned trope but performs important conceptual work. It marks metaphysics' decline as a tragic, not a comic, fall: not the death of a fraud but the displacement of a sovereign by historical accident. Kant's attitude toward metaphysics is therefore from the start ambivalent — neither Humean dismissal nor Wolffian piety, but a tragic recognition of a fallen sovereign whose throne is now contested precisely because no one knows whether the dynasty is legitimate.

He then sketches the historical phases of this fall. Initially metaphysics' rule (*Herrschaft*) under the administration of the dogmatists was despotic. But since their legislation still bore traces of the old barbarism, it gradually degenerated through internal wars into complete anarchy, and the skeptics, a kind of nomads who detest all stable cultivation of the soil, broke up the civil union from time to time. Since they fortunately were only a few, however, they could not prevent the dogmatists from always again attempting, although by no plan unanimously agreed upon, to rebuild. The political metaphor is sustained and significant. Dogmatic metaphysics is *despotic*: it commands without justification. The internal wars among dogmatists (Leibnizian, Wolffian, Cartesian, occasionalist, etc.) produce *anarchy*. Skepticism is *nomadic*: it has no permanent settlement and produces no positive science, but it disrupts. The implicit ideal toward which Kant gestures is *rechtmäßige Herrschaft* — legitimate rule, established by a constitution. The Critique will, in this allegory, function as the constitutional law that establishes legitimate metaphysics on grounds that neither despot nor nomad can dispute.

In recent times, Kant continues, it once seemed that all these disputes were to be ended through a certain physiology of human understanding by the celebrated Locke, and the legitimacy of those claims fully decided. But it turned out that, although the birth of the supposed queen was traced back to the rabble of common experience, and her pretensions thereby should justly have become suspect, this genealogy was nonetheless ascribed to her falsely — for she still asserted her claims, by which everything fell back into the old worm-eaten dogmatism, and thence into the contempt from which one had wanted to extract the science. The Lockean strategy of tracing concepts to experience is here recognized as the right *kind* of move (it locates the *birth-place*, *Geburtsort*, of cognitions), but the wrong *execution*: Locke's genealogy from common experience cannot decide the lawfulness of the resulting claims, since he leaves intact the metaphysical claims themselves while merely changing their stated source. The result is that empiricism becomes the unwitting servant of dogmatism — a charge that Kant will later level against Hume's predecessors and that motivates his own distinction between the *quaestio facti* and the *quaestio iuris*.

Now, he continues, after all paths (one assures us) have been tried in vain, what reigns is weariness and complete indifferentism, the mother of chaos and night in the sciences, but at the same time also the origin, or at least the prelude, of a near transformation and enlightenment, where ill-applied diligence had made them obscure, confused, and useless. — For it is *vain* to want to feign indifference with respect to such inquiries, whose object cannot be indifferent to human nature. The diagnosis of indifferentism is psychologically and historically acute: the failure of dogmatic metaphysics produces not just skepticism but apathy, and apathy is *more dangerous* than skepticism because skepticism still cares. Yet Kant immediately turns the apathy itself into evidence: those who pretend indifference cannot maintain it, because reason's questions are not optional. Indifferentism is therefore *pretended* (*affektiert*) — and the very pretension proves that the questions remain alive in those who claim to have abandoned them. This argument is one of Kant's deepest moves in the preface: it converts the empirical history of metaphysics from a record of failure into evidence for the unavoidability of metaphysical questioning, and thereby into the motivation for a Critique that does not eliminate metaphysics but disciplines it.

The so-called indifferentists, Kant continues, however much they think to disguise themselves by changing the language of the schools into a popular tone, in so far as they think at all, inevitably fall back into the very metaphysical assertions for which they professed such contempt. Yet this indifference, which arises in the midst of the flourishing of all sciences, and indeed precisely those sciences whose attainment we would least want to surrender, is nevertheless a phenomenon that deserves attention and reflection. It is evidently the effect not of the levity but of the *ripened power of judgment of the age* (*gereiften Urteilskraft des Zeitalters*), which will no longer be put off with apparent knowledge, and a call to reason to take up anew the most difficult of all its tasks, that of self-knowledge (*Selbsterkenntnis*), and to institute a court of justice (*Gerichtshof*) that will secure it in its rightful claims while dismissing all groundless pretensions, and this not by mere ordinances of power but according to its eternal and unchangeable laws — and this court is nothing other than the Critique of pure reason itself. The reversal is total. The decline of metaphysics is not a sign of intellectual decadence but of intellectual maturity. The same maturity that has discredited the old dogmatism now demands a higher tribunal. The image of the *Gerichtshof* — court of justice — is the controlling political-juridical metaphor of the entire Critique. Reason is to put itself on trial; the *eigene Gesetze* by which it judges are *its own* unchangeable laws; the verdict will be neither the despotism of dogmatism nor the anarchy of skepticism, but the lawful constitution (*Verfassung*) of legitimate metaphysics.

I do not understand by this, Kant clarifies, a critique of books and systems, but the critique of the faculty of reason as such, with respect to all cognitions for which it can strive independently of all experience; thus the decision of the possibility or impossibility of a metaphysics in general, and the determination of the sources, as well as the extent and bounds, of the same, but all from principles. This delimitation is decisive. The Critique is not literary criticism or doxography; it is not a comparison of Leibniz with Wolff or of Hume with Berkeley. It is the inquiry into the *faculty itself* (*Vermögen der Vernunft überhaupt*) — the source-, extent-, and boundary-determination (*Quellen, Umfang, Grenzen*) of reason's a priori cognitions. The phrase *alles aber aus Prinzipien* signals that this inquiry will itself proceed from principles, not from empirical psychology or biography. The Critique is a transcendental, not an empirical, investigation of reason.

This path, the only one left, Kant continues, I have now taken, and I flatter myself to have found upon it the avoidance of all those errors that had hitherto put reason into discord with itself in its experience-independent use. I have not evaded the questions through pleading the incapacity of human reason; rather I have specified them completely from principles, and after I have discovered the point at which reason has misunderstood itself, I have resolved them to reason's full satisfaction. The flat statement of completeness (*vollständig*) here is one of the most striking — and to many readers, scandalous — claims of the preface. Kant does not promise a contribution; he promises a *finished* solution to the antinomies of metaphysics, deriving them and dissolving them from a single principle. The mention of reason's *self-misunderstanding* (*da sie sich selbst mißversteht*) is the seed of the entire later doctrine of transcendental illusion: error arises not from poor data but from reason's failure to recognize the conditions of its own legitimate use.

The answering of those questions, he continues, has not, indeed, turned out as the dogmatically enthusiastic thirst for knowledge might have wished; that thirst could not be satisfied otherwise than by magical powers in which I am no adept. But this was not the intention of the natural vocation of our reason, and it was the duty of philosophy to remove the illusion which had arisen from misinterpretation, even though much praised and beloved delusion be thereby destroyed. Here Kant makes the crucial admission: the Critique's answer is *negative* with respect to what dogmatic metaphysics wanted (proof of the existence of God, the immortality of the soul, the freedom of the will, the eternity of the world, all as theoretical knowledge of supersensible objects). The answer is *positive* only at a higher level: it discloses why those answers could not be given and thereby liberates reason from a task that was misconceived from the start. The *vermeinte Wissen* — alleged knowledge — must be sacrificed to genuine knowledge of bounds. The phrase that Kant will famously deploy in the B-preface — *Ich mußte also das Wissen aufheben, um zum Glauben Platz zu bekommen* (I had to suspend knowledge to make room for faith) — is already implicit here, though without the practical-philosophical extension that the second preface will give it.

In this work, Kant insists, I have made completeness (*Vollständigkeit*) my chief aim, and I venture to assert that there can be no metaphysical problem that is not solved here, or for whose solution at least the key has not been provided. Pure reason is, indeed, so perfect a unity that, if its principle were insufficient for a single one of all the questions which its very nature presents to it, one could still throw it away, since it could not be sufficiently equal to any of the others. The argument is structurally elegant: because reason is *systematic*, a critique of reason is either complete or worthless. A partial critique is inconsistent with the unity of the faculty it would critique. This commitment to completeness is what later structurally distinguishes Kant's project from any merely problem-by-problem refutation of metaphysical errors, and it is what justifies the architectonic ambitions of the Critique as a whole — the system of categories, the table of principles, the four antinomies, the three ideas of reason, etc., all generated from a single principle.

While I say this, Kant continues, I think I notice on the reader's face an indignation, mixed with contempt, at claims that are apparently so pretentious and immodest, and yet they are incomparably more moderate than the claims of every author of the most common program who pretends to prove the simple nature of the soul or the necessity of a first beginning of the world. For he commits himself thereby to extending human cognition beyond all bounds of possible experience, of which I humbly confess that this surpasses my powers entirely; instead of which I have to do solely with reason itself and its pure thinking, the comprehensive knowledge of which I do not need to seek far from me, because I encounter them in myself, and common logic also provides me already with an example that all simple actions of reason can be enumerated completely and systematically. The argument from comparative modesty is striking: the dogmatist who claims to prove the soul's simplicity claims more than Kant does, because he claims knowledge of objects beyond experience, while Kant claims only knowledge of the faculty itself. The faculty is *internal* (*finde ich bei mir selbst*) — a methodological point with deep epistemological consequences. The critique of reason is possible because reason is, in a sense Kant will later refine, *self-transparent* with respect to its own a priori operations, even when it is opaque with respect to objects beyond experience. The appeal to *gemeine Logik* (general logic) as already providing an example of complete enumeration of mental acts (judgment-forms) prefigures the metaphysical deduction of the categories from the table of judgments.

Only let us be asked here, what one may hope of carrying out from such a work, with respect to its content, and with what certainty? It is, with respect to the first, the determination of all possibility, of all use of reason a priori; with respect to the second, certainty — and what is meant by it. Such a certainty Kant promises to deliver in the work itself. This brief paragraph sets up the dual criterion that will govern the remainder of the preface: scope of solutions and degree of certainty. Both will be addressed in the preface and discharged through the Critique as a whole, but Kant signals here that he wishes to specify the *quality* of the cognition the Critique offers, not merely its quantity. The promise of certainty — *Gewißheit* — is the promise of an apodictic, not merely probable, science of reason's a priori use; it commits Kant to the demand that transcendental philosophy be as rigorous as mathematics in its evidence, though by a different method.

This work, Kant concludes, can never be made popular, but also has no need to be popular; for as little as ear-pleasing wisdom can serve the very subtle web of the speculative use of reason, even so, the proper friends of such a science will, all the more willingly because metaphysics requires for its existence a quite secured foundation, sacrifice the demands of taste, if this work makes good its promise. The closing distinction — *Geschmack* (taste) versus *Wissenschaft* (science) — is the standard 18th-century distinction between popular and learned philosophy, but Kant gives it a specific twist: the Critique is *necessarily* unpopular because the structural depth of its inquiry cannot be presented in the rhetorical flourishes of *Popularphilosophie* (Mendelssohn, Garve, Feder). The promise that compensation for popular inaccessibility lies in *gesicherter Grund* — secured foundation — is a promise of permanence: the work will not need to be rewritten, because its results, once correctly established, are final. This claim of finality, more than any other, both motivated the writing of the Critique and irritated its first readers, and it directly provoked the misunderstandings that the B preface and the *Prolegomena* attempt to correct.

So much, Kant says, for the *completeness* in attaining each end and the *exhaustiveness* in attaining all ends together, which not some arbitrary intention but the nature of cognition itself imposes on us as the *matter* of our critical investigation. Still there are *certainty* (*Gewißheit*) and *clarity* (*Deutlichkeit*), two further pieces concerning the form of the investigation, which are to be regarded as essential demands that one may rightly make of the author who ventures upon so slippery an undertaking. This brief transitional paragraph reorganizes the criteria: completeness and exhaustiveness pertain to the *matter* (what is investigated and how exhaustively); certainty and clarity pertain to the *form* (how the results are presented and grounded). Kant's adjective *schlüpfrich* — slippery — is significant: it acknowledges that transcendental philosophy operates on terrain where it is easy to slip, where commonsense criteria are not available, and where the demand for rigor is therefore *higher* than in empirical disciplines, not lower.

As for certainty, Kant goes on, I have passed sentence on myself: in this kind of consideration it is in no way permitted to *opine* (*zu meinen*), and everything that even looks like a hypothesis is contraband, which may not be offered for sale at the lowest price but must be confiscated as soon as it is discovered. The reason is that every cognition that is to stand a priori announces of itself that it will be held *absolutely necessary*; and a determination of all pure a priori cognitions, which is to be the standard and even the example of all apodictic (philosophical) certainty, requires this even more. Whether Kant has discharged this promise is left to the reader; the author may only present grounds, not pass judgment on their effect upon his judges. This is one of the most uncompromising methodological commitments in the preface. Hypothesis is *contraband*: not a useful preliminary, not a heuristic step, but illegal merchandise. The reasoning is internal: a priori cognition claims necessity, and the science of a priori cognition must therefore itself be apodictic — not because Kant chooses to be strict but because the *content* of the science forbids a less strict mode. To allow hypothesis in transcendental philosophy would be to undermine the very status of a priori cognition. This claim has profound consequences: it forbids, in principle, any inductive or probabilistic move within the Critique itself, even though such moves are appropriate in empirical investigation. Kant adds a small qualification: that he may at least flag passages giving occasion for *Mißtrauen* (mistrust) so that small worries about side-purposes do not infect judgment of the main aim.

He continues by identifying the most important investigation of the whole work: those undertaken in the second chapter of the Transcendental Analytic, under the title of the *Deduction of the pure concepts of the understanding*. They have cost him the most labor — though not, he hopes, unrewarded labor — and this investigation, which is laid out somewhat deeply, has two sides. The first relates to the objects of the pure understanding and is meant to demonstrate and make comprehensible the *objective validity* of its a priori concepts. This side is essential to his aims. The other side aims to consider the pure understanding itself, with respect to its possibility and the cognitive powers on which it itself rests — that is, to consider it in *subjective relation*. Although this discussion is of great importance with respect to his main aim, it does not belong essentially to it; for the main question always remains *what and how much can understanding and reason, free from all experience, cognize* — not *how is the very faculty of thinking possible?* The latter is, as it were, a search for the cause of a given effect and so far has something resembling a hypothesis about it (although, as he says he will show on another occasion, it does not in fact have this character). This subdivision of the Deduction into objective and subjective sides is one of the most important methodological signals of the entire preface, and the editorial note in the Meiner edition rightly emphasizes its weight. The *objective deduction* establishes the right (*quaestio iuris*) of the categories to apply to objects; the *subjective deduction* explains the *psychological-architectonic* fact that the understanding has these concepts and not others. Kant explicitly weakens his commitment to the subjective deduction here: it is important, but not essential. The reason given is decisive: explaining the *Vermögen zu denken* itself by tracing its causes resembles a hypothesis (it identifies the cause of a given effect), and hypothesis is contraband. So Kant flags that if the reader does not find the subjective deduction fully convincing, the objective deduction stands on its own — and a sufficient ground for the objective conclusion can be found in pages 92–93 of the A edition (which the editorial note identifies as the "Transition to the Transcendental Deduction of the Categories," A 92–94/B 124–127). This is the famous Kantian retreat from the boldness of the A-edition Deduction's threefold synthesis to the more strictly transcendental argument of the B-edition Deduction, and the preface already signals the direction of that retreat.

Finally, on clarity, the reader has a right first to discursive (logical) clarity through concepts, and then to intuitive (aesthetic) clarity through intuitions, i.e., examples or other illustrations *in concreto*. For the first, Kant has provided sufficiently; this concerned the essence of his undertaking. But this very provision was the accidental cause of his being unable to satisfy the second, although less strict, but nevertheless fair demand. He has been almost constantly undecided in the course of his work how to handle this. The distinction between discursive and intuitive clarity is a methodologically important Kantian doctrine. Discursive clarity is clarity *by concepts*: a definition that places a concept under its genus and specifies its differentia. Intuitive clarity is clarity *by example*: an *instantia* that shows the concept *in concreto*. Both contribute to *Deutlichkeit*, but they do so in different ways: discursive clarity makes the concept's *content* clear; intuitive clarity makes its *application* clear. The dialectic between them shapes every philosophical exposition — and Kant signals here that the Critique privileges the former at the cost of the latter, but with a justification.

Examples and illustrations, Kant continues, always seemed to him necessary and accordingly flowed properly into their places in the first draft. But he soon saw the size of his task and the multitude of objects he would have to deal with, and, since he became aware that even by themselves, in dry, merely *scholastic* presentation, these would already swell the work enough, he found it inadvisable to swell it still more with examples and illustrations, which are necessary only in a *popular* aim — particularly since this work could in no way be made suitable for popular use, and the proper *connoisseurs* of the science do not need this relief (although it is always pleasing), and it could even bring with it something contrary to the purpose. The argument here interlocks with the earlier point about popularity. Examples are not optional ornaments; they are necessary for a popular use. But this work is not popular and cannot be made so. For the connoisseur — the *Kenner* — examples are pleasant but not necessary, and indeed they may be *zweckwidrig* (contrary to purpose) because they distract attention from the systematic unity. The economy of presentation thus forces Kant into the dry scholastic mode, which is the form proper to a science that claims apodictic certainty.

Abbé Terrasson said, Kant continues, that if one measures the size of a book not by the number of its leaves but by the time one needs to understand it, one could say of many a book that *it would be much shorter were it not so short*. On the other hand, if one's aim is the comprehensibility of an extensive but in one principle coherent whole of speculative cognition, one could with equal right say that *many a book would have become much clearer if it had not been made to be so very clear*. For the means of clarity help indeed in *parts*, but often disperse in the *whole*, since they do not let the reader come quickly enough to a survey of the whole and through all their bright colors still glue over and make unrecognizable the *articulation*, or members, of the system, upon which it depends most for judging its unity and soundness. The Terrasson reference (Jean Terrasson, French Enlightenment writer) introduces a witty paradox: brevity that wins compression by sacrificing intelligibility is false brevity. Kant inverts it: aids to clarity that sacrifice systematic articulation are false clarity. The metaphor of *Gliederbau* — articulation, members — is decisive: a system has the structure of a living body, with members that must be visible *qua members* in order for the unity to be assessable. The colors of intuitive clarity, by attracting attention to particulars, can *unkenntlich machen* — make unrecognizable — the very joints of the system. This is one of the most striking methodological declarations in the preface: it commits Kant to a mode of presentation in which the system as a whole is the bearer of intelligibility, and individual passages must be understood *through* the system, not despite it.

It can serve, as Kant thinks, not as little inducement to the reader to unite his effort with that of the author if he has the prospect of completing, according to the outline laid down, a great and important work entirely and yet durably. Now metaphysics, according to the concepts that we shall here give of it, is the only of all the sciences that may promise itself such completion, indeed in short time and with only little, but united, effort, so that nothing remains for posterity except to arrange everything in *didactic* manner according to its purposes, without being able to increase the content in the slightest. For it is nothing but the *inventory* of all our possessions through *pure reason*, systematically ordered. Nothing can escape us here, because what reason wholly brings forth out of itself cannot hide but is itself brought to light by reason as soon as one has discovered its common principle. The perfect unity of this kind of cognitions, and indeed from purely pure concepts, without anything from experience or even *particular* intuition leading to determinate experience having any influence on them to expand or augment them — this makes this unconditional completeness not only feasible but also necessary. *Tecum habita et noris, quam sit tibi curta supellex.* (Persius, *Sat.* IV 52: "Live with yourself, and you will know how meager your household goods are.") The metaphor of the *Inventarium* is one of Kant's most powerful: metaphysics is an inventory of pure reason's own possessions, and an inventory is exhaustive in principle precisely because it lists what is internal and finite. Reason cannot conceal its own contents from itself; the discovery of the common principle (the categories with their unifying source, transcendental apperception) brings all the contents to light at once. This makes complete metaphysics possible — and indeed *necessary*, because reason's nature is unitary. The Persius quotation has a deflating, almost rueful tone: live with yourself, and you will find how meager your furnishings are. The inventory of pure reason will not yield the riches the dogmatists promised, but exactly its own *curta supellex*: a precisely delimited stock.

Such a system of pure (speculative) reason, Kant hopes, he will himself deliver under the title *Metaphysics of Nature*, which, at not yet half the prolixity, will nevertheless have an incomparably richer content than the Critique here, which had to first lay open the sources and conditions of its possibility and had to clean and level a completely overgrown ground. Here he expects of his reader the patience and impartiality of a *judge*, but there the *complaisance* and *assistance* of a *fellow worker*; for although all the principles of the system are presented completely in the Critique, the comprehensiveness of the system itself further requires that no derived concepts be missing — concepts which one cannot a priori survey but which must be sought out gradually — and likewise, since there the entire synthesis of concepts was exhausted, here it is further demanded that the same be done with respect to *analysis*, which is easy and more entertainment than work. The plan thus announced is for a two-stage project: the Critique establishes the principles; the *Metaphysik der Natur* (which Kant partly delivered in the 1786 *Metaphysische Anfangsgründe der Naturwissenschaft*, though the full work never appeared) will deploy them. The metaphors of judge and fellow worker mark a distinction in the reader's mode of engagement: the Critique demands judicial impartiality because its claims are exposed to forensic scrutiny; the *Metaphysik* will demand collaboration because the derivation of specific consequences is a cooperative labor. The remark that *Analysis* will be needed (alongside the *Synthesis* of the Critique) signals Kant's awareness that the doctrinal metaphysics will also analyze the specific concepts of the natural sciences (matter, motion, force, etc.) into their components from the categories — a task whose execution he never fully completed.

There remain only a few remarks on the printing. Since its beginning was somewhat delayed, Kant says, he was able to see only about half the proof sheets, in which he did indeed find some printing errors that did not, however, confuse the sense, except for the one occurring on p. 379, line 4 from the bottom, where *spezifisch* is to be read instead of *skeptisch*. The Antinomy of Pure Reason, from page 425 to 461, is laid out so, in the manner of a table, that everything belonging to the Thesis runs always on the left side, and what belongs to the Antithesis runs on the right side, which he arranged so that statement and counter-statement could the more easily be compared with each other. This closing paragraph is technical but informative. The page numbers refer to the A edition. The fact that Kant draws attention to the tabular layout of the Antinomies — and explicitly justifies it as facilitating comparison of thesis and antithesis — is significant: the visual presentation embodies the systematic claim that thesis and antithesis are *equally well demonstrated* on dogmatic grounds and that the Antinomy must be resolved by a higher critique, not by victory of one side. The tabular form is therefore not merely a typographical convenience but a philosophical statement, and the preface's drawing attention to it is consistent with the rest of the preface's insistence on the *form* of presentation as integral to the content of the argument. With this practical note, the A preface ends — a curiously prosaic ending for so ambitious a preface, but one that is consistent with Kant's refusal of rhetorical flourish.

## **Preface to the Second Edition**

Whether the working of those cognitions which belong to the business of reason takes the secure path of a science or not, Kant begins, can soon be judged from the result. If, after many preparations and arrangements, as soon as it comes to the purpose it falls into a stop (*Stecken*), or if, in order to attain that purpose, it must often go back and take another path; likewise if it is not possible to bring the various co-workers into unanimity about the manner in which the common aim shall be reached: then one can always be convinced that such a study has by no means yet entered upon the secure path of a science but is a mere groping about (*bloßes Herumtappen*), and it is already a service to reason to find this path if possible, even if much that was contained in the previously, unreflectingly adopted purpose must be given up as in vain. The opening paragraph of the B preface sets out the criterion that will govern the entire methodological argument: the *secure path of a science* (*sicherer Gang einer Wissenschaft*) is recognized by three negative marks — no breakdowns at the moment of execution, no need to retrace and start over, and unanimity of practitioners. Each of these marks corresponds to a known feature of dogmatic metaphysics' failure: it always breaks down on its central questions (the antinomies, the paralogisms); it must always be begun again under a new system; and its practitioners cannot agree. The diagnostic schema is empirical (it appeals to the historical *Erfolg*, success or failure) but the conclusion is normative: a science not on the secure path is no science. The word *Herumtappen* — groping about, blind tactile searching — is the negative pole against which Kant defines scientific method. It anticipates the famous Copernican turn: the alternative to groping is method.

That *logic* has gone this secure path since the earliest times, Kant continues, can be seen from the fact that since Aristotle it has not had to take a step backwards, if one does not count the removal of some dispensable subtleties or clearer determination of what is presented as improvements — which, however, pertain more to the elegance than to the security of the science. What is still remarkable about it is that up to now it has been unable to take a step forward, and so to all appearances seems to be closed and complete. The thesis that logic is *complete* (*geschlossen und vollendet*) is one of the most criticized claims of the entire preface — modern readers have noted that Kant could not have foreseen Fregean and post-Fregean developments. But the philosophical force of the claim lies not in the empirical history of formal logic but in Kant's *concept* of logic: it is the science of the formal rules of thinking as such, and as such has a determinate scope that, once fully canvassed, is closed. The reason for this closure is given in the next sentences.

For when some moderns thought to expand logic, Kant continues, by inserting (1) *psychological* chapters about the various cognitive powers (imagination, wit), (2) *metaphysical* ones about the origin of cognition or about the different kinds of certainty according to different objects (idealism, skepticism, etc.), or (3) *anthropological* ones about prejudices (their causes and remedies), this comes from their ignorance of the proper nature of this science. It is not augmentation but disfigurement of the sciences when one lets their boundaries run into each other. The boundary of logic, however, is precisely determined by the fact that it is a science which exhaustively expounds and strictly proves nothing but the *formal rules of all thinking* (whether it be a priori or empirical, whatever origin or object it may have, encountering accidental or natural hindrances in our mind). This argument is the methodologically central one in the paragraph: logic's completeness is grounded in its *abstraction* from all material content (psychological, metaphysical, anthropological). Because it abstracts from objects, it cannot fail to enumerate the formal rules of thought; because it abstracts from origin, it cannot be invaded by genetic-psychological considerations; because it abstracts from objects, it has no business with idealism, skepticism, or any other doctrine about the relation of thought to its objects. To "expand" logic by importing such material is not augmentation but *Verunstaltung* — disfigurement. The argument has a clear consequence for Kant's project: logic in this strict sense provides the table of judgments, the formal armature from which the table of categories will be derived by the metaphysical deduction. The general logic is the *Propädeutik* — the vestibule — of the sciences, but it cannot be science of objects.

That logic has succeeded so well, Kant continues, it owes solely to its *restrictedness* (*Eingeschränktheit*), by which it is entitled, indeed bound, to abstract from all objects of cognition and their differences, and so in it the understanding has to do with nothing more than itself and its form. It must be far more difficult, naturally, for reason to take the secure path of science when it has to deal not merely with itself but also with objects; therefore logic, as propaedeutic, constitutes as it were only the vestibule of the sciences, and when one speaks of *Kenntnisse* (cognitions of objects), one indeed presupposes a logic to judge them, but one must seek their acquisition in the sciences properly and objectively so called. The asymmetry between logic and the object-sciences is the entry into Kant's central question: how can a science of objects (mathematics, physics, metaphysics) be set on the secure path? Logic cannot serve as model in any strong sense: its security comes from *not* having objects. The sciences that have objects need a different kind of self-grounding — and the next section will show that mathematics and physics have already attained it, while metaphysics has not.

So far as reason is to be in these sciences, then in them something must be cognized a priori, and their cognition can be related to its object in two ways: either merely to *determine* the object and its concept (which must be given elsewhere), or also actually to *make* it. The first is the theoretical, the other the practical cognition of reason. Of both, the pure part, however much or little it contains, namely that part in which reason wholly a priori determines its object, must be presented in advance, and that which comes from other sources must not be mixed with it; for it is bad economy when one blindly spends what comes in, without being able afterwards, when the income falls into stop, to distinguish which part of the receipts can bear the expense and from which one must cut it. The distinction between *theoretical* and *practical* cognition of reason is here introduced for the first time in the preface, and it is the architectonic premise of Kant's entire critical system: there are *two* uses of reason, distinguished by whether reason merely determines a given object or *makes* its object (in moral action). The methodological precept — separate the pure from the empirical part, isolate them, and present the pure part first — is the precept that governs the structure of the Critique itself. The household-economic metaphor (*üble Wirtschaft*) is informal but precise: one cannot evaluate the legitimacy of an income source if one cannot separate it from other sources at the point of expenditure.

Mathematics and physics, Kant continues, are the two theoretical cognitions of reason which are to determine their objects a priori; the first wholly purely, the second at least in part purely, but then also according to the standard of other sources of cognition than that of reason. The bifurcation is clean: mathematics is *wholly* a priori (it constructs its objects in pure intuition); physics is *partly* a priori (its principles are a priori but its applications require empirical observation). Metaphysics will turn out to be a third case: it claims to be wholly a priori like mathematics, but unlike mathematics it cannot construct its objects in intuition. This is what makes its situation singular and what will require the Critique.

Mathematics, Kant goes on, has gone the secure path of a science from the earliest times to which the history of human reason reaches, among the admirable people of the Greeks. But one must not think that it was as easy for it as for logic — where reason has to do only with itself — to find that royal path, or rather to pave it for itself; rather I believe that it long remained (chiefly among the Egyptians) at the groping stage, and that this transformation is to be ascribed to a *revolution* that the happy idea of a single man brought about in a single attempt, after which the path one had to take could no longer be missed, and the secure path of a science for all times and into infinite distances was entered upon and traced out. The history of this *revolution of the mode of thinking* (*Revolution der Denkart*), which was much more important than the discovery of the way around the famous Cape (of Good Hope), and of the fortunate man who brought it about, has not been preserved for us. The *Revolution der Denkart* is the central methodological term of the B preface, and Kant deploys it with strict consistency throughout. The contrast with the discovery of the route around the Cape is not casual: it places mathematics' revolution on the historical level of the great geographic discoveries of the early modern period, while subordinating that material progress to the spiritual one. The Egyptians' *Herumtappen* is the counter-example: empirical mathematical practice without method.

Yet the legend (*Sage*) handed down by Diogenes Laertius, Kant continues, naming the supposed inventor of the smallest and, in common judgment, not at all proof-requiring elements of geometrical demonstrations, proves that the memory of the change brought about by the first trace of this new path's discovery must have seemed extremely important to the mathematicians, and thereby became unforgettable. To the first one who demonstrated the *isosceles* triangle (whether his name be Thales or whatever one wills), a *light dawned* (*ging ein Licht auf*); for he found that he must not pursue what he saw in the figure, or even the mere concept of it, and as it were learn from it its properties, but produce them through what he himself a priori thought into it and presented according to concepts (through *construction*); and that, in order to know something a priori with certainty, he must attribute to the thing nothing but what necessarily follows from what he himself laid into it according to his concept. The image of the *Licht* that dawns on the first demonstrator is a recurrent figure in Kant's preface; it marks the moment when blind groping is replaced by method. The methodological content of the moment is precisely Kantian: a priori cognition is not extracted from the figure as a passive object given to sight (*was er in der Figur sahe*) or even from the mere concept of it; it is produced (*hervorbringen*) by what reason itself lays into the figure through construction. The figure is the medium of demonstration; reason is its agent. This is the proto-form of the doctrine of the synthetic a priori in mathematics — that mathematical truth depends on the constructive presentation of concepts in pure intuition — which the Critique itself will develop in the Aesthetic and the Doctrine of Method.

With *natural science*, Kant continues, things went much more slowly before it found the highway of science; for it is only about a century and a half since the proposal of the ingenious Bacon of Verulam partly occasioned this discovery, partly enlivened it, since one was already on its track — a discovery which is likewise to be explained only by a swiftly arrived-at *Revolution der Denkart*. Here he will consider natural science only so far as it is grounded on *empirical* principles. The dating (Bacon, *Novum Organum*, 1620; thus about 1620-1780) is roughly accurate, and the qualification — *so fern sie auf empirische Prinzipien gegründet ist* — is important: Kant is here speaking of natural science as an empirical-experimental enterprise, not yet of the metaphysical principles that underlie its possibility (which the *Metaphysische Anfangsgründe der Naturwissenschaft* of 1786 will address).

When Galileo, Kant continues, had his balls roll down the inclined plane with a weight chosen by himself, or Torricelli let the air bear a weight that he had thought in advance equal to that of a known column of water, or in still later time Stahl converted metals into lime and this back into metal by withdrawing something from them and giving it back: a light dawned upon all natural inquirers. They grasped (*begriffen*) that reason has insight only into what it itself produces according to its design (*Entwurf*), that reason must go ahead with principles of its judgments according to constant laws and *compel* (*nötigen*) nature to answer its questions, but must not let itself be led, as it were, on a leash by nature alone; for else accidental observations, made according to no previously drawn-up plan, do not connect in a necessary law, which reason however seeks and needs. Reason must approach nature with its principles in one hand — according to which alone agreeing appearances can hold as laws — and with the experiment, which it thought out according to those principles, in the other; not, indeed, in the quality of a *pupil* who lets the teacher tell him whatever he wishes, but of an *appointed judge* who compels the witnesses to answer the questions he puts to them. This is one of the most decisive passages of the entire preface, and it states the central methodological insight: scientific cognition of nature is not passive reception of what nature gives but active interrogation according to a prior design. The threefold examples (Galileo's inclined plane and chosen weight, Torricelli's air-pressure experiment, Stahl's calcination-reduction cycle) are paradigmatic instances of *Experiment* in the strict sense: an artificially produced situation in which a specific question, conceptually formulated in advance, is put to nature in order that a specific kind of answer can be obtained. The image of the *bestallter Richter* — the appointed judge who *compels* the witnesses — is again the juridical metaphor that runs through the preface, and the contrast with the *Schüler* (pupil) is the contrast with the Aristotelian-empiricist conception of cognition as the soul's reception of forms from nature. Reason actively interrogates; nature does not freely speak.

And so, Kant continues, even physics owes its so advantageous revolution of thought entirely to the *idea* of seeking in nature, in accordance with what reason itself puts into it, that which it must learn from nature and of which it would know nothing of itself — not attributing it to nature. By this, natural science was first brought onto the secure path of a science, after having been for so many centuries nothing but a mere groping about. The footnote attached here ("I do not strictly follow the thread of the history of the experimental method, whose first beginnings are also not well known") is itself significant: Kant is not writing history of science in the empirical sense but identifying the *methodological* turning-point that constitutes a science. The phrase *dasjenige in ihr zu suchen, was sie von dieser lernen muß, und wovon sie für sich selbst nichts wissen würde* makes the precise distinction between what reason puts in (the principles, the experimental design) and what nature gives (the response, the empirical content). This is the proleptic statement of the Copernican thesis, which will be applied to metaphysics in the next paragraphs.

To *metaphysics*, Kant continues — a wholly isolated speculative cognition of reason that elevates itself entirely above the instruction of experience, and indeed through mere concepts (not, like mathematics, through their application to intuition), where reason is to be its own pupil — fate has hitherto not been so favorable that it might have been able to enter on the secure path of a science. And yet it is older than all the others and would remain even if all the others were entirely swallowed up in the abyss of an all-destroying barbarism. For in it reason continually falls into stop, even when it wants to have insight a priori (as it presumes) into those laws that the most common experience confirms. In it one must take the way back countless times because one finds that it does not lead where one wants to go; and as for the unanimity of its adherents in their assertions, it is still so far removed therefrom that metaphysics is rather a *battlefield* (*Kampfplatz*), which seems quite particularly destined to exercise its forces in a sham fight, on which no fighter has ever yet been able to gain even the smallest place and to ground a lasting possession upon his victory. The threefold negative marks of unscientific status — falling into stop, having to retrace, lack of unanimity — are here predicated of metaphysics in detail. The phrase *Vernunft selbst ihr eigener Schüler* highlights the peculiar predicament: in metaphysics, reason has only itself for an object and yet cannot find its way. The reason is that, unlike mathematics, metaphysics cannot construct its objects in intuition, and unlike physics, it cannot interrogate nature for an answer.

There is therefore no doubt, Kant continues, that its procedure has hitherto been a mere groping about, and — what is the worst — among *mere concepts*. Now wherein lies it that no secure path of science has yet been found here? Is it perhaps impossible? Whence then has nature visited our reason with the restless striving to track this path as one of its most important affairs? Even more, how little reason do we have to place trust in our reason, when in one of the most important pieces of our desire for knowledge it not merely abandons us but holds us back with deceptive appearances (*Vorspiegelungen*) and in the end deceives us\! Or has it only thus far been missed — what indication can we use, on renewed inquiry, to hope that we shall be more fortunate than others before us have been? The cluster of rhetorical questions performs the transition from diagnosis to remedy. The possibility that metaphysics is *impossible* is canvassed and rejected because of the very fact that reason cannot help inquiring; nature has imposed the inquiry on reason. The alternative — that the path has only been missed — opens the space for Kant's own attempted *Revolution*. The mention of *Vorspiegelungen* and *betrügt* anticipates the doctrine of *transzendentaler Schein* in the Dialectic: reason's natural illusion of overstepping its bounds is the very source of the deception that is to be diagnosed.

I should think, Kant continues, the examples of mathematics and natural science, which have become what they are through a revolution brought about at one stroke, are striking enough to reflect on the essential piece of the *change of thinking* that was so advantageous to them, and to imitate it here at least by way of attempt, as far as their analogy as cognitions of reason with metaphysics permits. *Hitherto one assumed that all our cognition must conform to the objects*; but all attempts to make out anything about them a priori through concepts, by which our cognition would be expanded, came to nothing on this presupposition. Let us therefore try, for once, whether we may not make better progress in the tasks of metaphysics by assuming that *the objects must conform to our cognition*, which agrees better with the demanded possibility of an a priori cognition of them that is to make out something about objects before they are given to us. This is matters here just as with the first thoughts of *Copernicus*, who, when the explanation of the movements of the heavens did not go well if he assumed that the whole heavenly host turned round the spectator, attempted whether it might not succeed better if he let the spectator turn and left the stars at rest. This is the famous Copernican passage — the single most quoted statement of the Critical method. The thesis is that the presupposition of conformity of cognition to objects (which underlies both empiricism and dogmatic rationalism) makes a priori cognition of objects unintelligible, and that the inverse presupposition — that objects conform to our cognition, at least with respect to those features that are a priori cognizable — makes a priori cognition intelligible. The Copernican analogy is precise: Copernicus did not deny the appearances; he reinterpreted them by locating the source of the apparent motion in the observer. Kant does not deny that there are objects given to cognition; he reinterprets them by locating the source of certain features of objects (those that are a priori cognizable) in the cognitive faculty. The analogy is not perfect — as much commentary has noted, in the Copernican view the observer is *part of nature* and his motion is a real motion in physical space, while in Kant's view the cognitive faculty is on the *transcendental* side, a contrast Kant himself will draw in the next paragraphs. But the methodological point — reversal of the presupposed direction of dependence — is rigorously analogous.

In metaphysics, Kant continues, one can try it in a similar way with respect to the *intuition* of objects. If intuition had to conform to the constitution of the objects, then I do not see how one could know anything a priori about it; if, however, the object (as object of the senses) conforms to the constitution of our faculty of intuition, then I can quite well represent this possibility to myself. Because, however, I cannot remain at these intuitions if they are to become cognitions, but must refer them as representations to something as object and determine this through them, I can either assume that the *concepts* by which I bring about this determination also conform to the object — and then I am again in the same embarrassment about how I could know anything a priori about it — or I assume that the objects, or, what is the same, the *experience* in which alone they (as given objects) are cognized, conforms to these concepts; then I see at once an easier way out, because experience itself is a kind of cognition that requires understanding, whose rule I must presuppose in myself before objects are given to me, hence a priori, which is expressed in concepts a priori, to which therefore all objects of experience necessarily conform and with which they must agree. The Copernican thesis is here applied at two levels: the level of intuition (giving the doctrine of transcendental aesthetic — space and time as a priori forms of sensibility) and the level of concept (giving the doctrine of transcendental analytic — the categories as a priori forms of understanding). Both levels exhibit the same structure: if the receptive or thinking faculty had to conform to the object, no a priori cognition is possible; if the object (as it is given to us in experience) conforms to the faculty, a priori cognition becomes intelligible because the faculty provides the form of the object. The reasoning is *transcendental* in the strict sense: it argues from the actuality of a priori cognition (which is presupposed) to the conditions of its possibility (the conformity thesis). The phrase *Erfahrung selbst eine Erkenntnisart ist, die Verstand erfordert* is the seed of the entire Transcendental Analytic: experience is not raw given but cognition, and as cognition it requires the legislative activity of the understanding.

As for objects, Kant continues, so far as they are thought merely through reason and necessarily — but yet (at least as reason thinks them) cannot be given in any experience at all — the attempts to think them (for they must let themselves be thought) will hereafter provide a splendid *touchstone* of what we accept as the altered method of the mode of thinking: namely, that we cognize a priori of things only what we ourselves lay into them. The footnote attached here gives the metaphor of the experimental method applied to pure reason: the elements of reason are to be sought in what can be confirmed or refuted by an experiment; since with objects beyond the bounds of experience no experiment is possible (as it is in natural science), the experiment must be made with the *concepts* and *principles* that we accept a priori, by arranging matters so that the same objects can be considered on the one side as objects of the senses and of the understanding for experience, on the other side as objects which one thinks merely for the isolated reason striving beyond the bounds of experience, hence from two different sides; if it now turns out that when one considers things from this twofold point of view, agreement with the principle of pure reason takes place, but with a single point of view an unavoidable conflict of reason with itself arises, then the experiment decides for the correctness of that distinction. The methodological structure here outlined is one of the deepest in the preface. The Critical method is *experimental* in a strict and surprising sense: the transcendental distinction between things as appearances and things in themselves is treated as a hypothesis whose validity is to be confirmed by an experimental test — namely, whether under the distinction the principles of pure reason are consistent (with respect to the demands of speculative cognition and of practical reason both), while under the absence of the distinction they fall into contradiction (the antinomies). The experiment is *internal*: it is conducted in the laboratory of reason itself, by reflection on whether the system of principles can be coherently developed under each presupposition. The footnote thus sketches the entire structure of the Critique as a vast transcendental experiment.

This attempt, Kant continues, succeeds as one wishes and promises the secure path of a science to metaphysics in its first part — namely, where it deals with concepts a priori of which the corresponding objects can be given in experience adequate to them. For after this *change of thinking*, one can quite well explain the possibility of a cognition a priori; and, what is still more, provide the laws that a priori underlie nature as the *complex* (*Inbegriff*) of objects of experience with their satisfactory proofs — both of which were impossible by the previous procedure. But there results from this deduction of our faculty of cognizing a priori, in the first part of metaphysics, a strange and apparently very disadvantageous result with respect to its whole purpose, which occupies the second part: namely, that with this faculty we can never go beyond the boundary of possible experience, which is yet precisely the most essential concern of this science. But herein lies the experiment of a *counter-proof* (*Gegenprobe*) of the truth of the result of that first appraisal of our a priori cognition of reason: namely, that it goes only on appearances, while leaving the thing in itself indeed as for itself actual but uncognized by us. The reasoning here is intricate. The Copernican turn delivers a positive result for the first part of metaphysics (the science of nature's a priori principles, i.e., the system of the Critique's Analytic) but a negative result for the second part (theoretical metaphysics of the supersensible). This negative result is *not* a defect of the new method but its very confirmation: precisely because the method has determined that we can a priori cognize only what we lay into objects through our forms of intuition and our categories, it must follow that we cannot a priori cognize objects beyond the bounds of these forms. The *Gegenprobe* — counter-proof — is the proof from the contrary side: the same method that gives the positive result also entails the negative one, and this internal coherence is the test of the method's correctness.

For what necessarily drives us beyond the boundary of experience and of all appearances is the *unconditioned*, which reason necessarily and with all right demands in things in themselves for everything conditioned, and thereby demands the series of conditions as completed. If, however, it is found, when one assumes that our cognition of experience conforms to the objects as things in themselves, that the unconditioned *cannot be thought without contradiction*; while, on the contrary, when one assumes that our representation of things as they are given to us does not conform to them as things in themselves, but rather these objects, as appearances, conform to our way of representing them, the *contradiction falls away*; and that consequently the unconditioned must not be encountered in things insofar as we know them (they are given to us) but in them insofar as we do not know them, as things in themselves — then there is shown that what we initially accepted only by way of attempt is *grounded*. The argument is a transcendental indirect proof: the postulated distinction (appearances/things in themselves) is confirmed by its capacity to resolve a contradiction (the antinomies) that the contrary postulate generates. The reasoning is given in compressed form here; its full development is in the Transcendental Dialectic, particularly in the Antinomy of Pure Reason. The footnote attached here gives the chemical analogy: the *Analysis des Metaphysikers* separates pure cognition a priori into the heterogeneous elements of things as appearances and things in themselves; the *Dialektik* combines both again to unanimity (*Einhelligkeit*) with the necessary idea of the unconditioned; this unanimity comes about only through the distinction, which is therefore the true one.

There still remains for us, Kant continues, after every progress in this field of the supersensible has been refused to speculative reason, to attempt whether *data* are not to be found in its *practical* cognition to determine that transcendent rational concept of the unconditioned, and in this way, in accordance with the wish of metaphysics, to attain to a priori cognition that goes beyond the bounds of all possible experience — though only in practical intention. And in such a procedure, speculative reason has at least *cleared a place* for such an extension, even though it had to leave it empty; and it therefore remains open to us, indeed we are even *called upon* by it, to fill it through practical *data* of it (a footnote at this point compares this clearance to the way Copernican astronomy, by treating heliocentrism as a hypothesis, opened the way for Newton's certainty that came from the central laws of celestial bodies and proved the otherwise invisible binding force of universal attraction). The architectonic relation between the Critique of Pure Reason and the Critique of Practical Reason is here adumbrated. The first Critique, by establishing the bounds of speculative reason, *clears a place* (*Platz verschafft*) for practical reason — without thereby filling that place. The filling will be the business of the second Critique, which will establish that practical reason has its own a priori data (the moral law) by which the rational concept of the unconditioned (freedom, God, immortality) acquires not theoretical but practical reality. The relation between the two Critiques is therefore not merely sequential but architectonic: the first opens by closing, and the second fills by opening on a different front.

In this attempt to alter the previous procedure of metaphysics, Kant continues, and by carrying out a complete revolution with it after the example of the geometers and natural scientists, consists the business of this *Critique of Pure Speculative Reason*. It is a treatise on method, not a system of the science itself; but it nevertheless sketches its whole outline, both with respect to its boundaries and to the entire inner articulation (*Gliederbau*). For pure speculative reason has this peculiarity, that it can measure out its own faculty according to the differences of the manner in which it chooses objects for thinking, and that it can also exhaustively enumerate the various ways of setting itself tasks, and so sketch the whole outline of a system of metaphysics — *can* and *shall*; because, as regards the first, in cognition a priori nothing can be ascribed to objects but what the thinking subject takes out of itself, and, as regards the second, with respect to cognitive principles it is a completely separated unity standing for itself, in which each member, as in an organized body, exists for the sake of all the others, and all for the sake of each, and no principle can with security be taken in *one* relation without at the same time having been investigated in its *thoroughgoing* relation to the whole pure use of reason. The Critique, Kant insists here, is a *Traktat von der Methode*, not a system in its own right. Yet because the method itself, when correctly executed, determines the whole outline of the future system, the Critique is in a strong sense the *negative* but exhaustive presentation of the system. The metaphor of the *organisierte Körper* — the organized body — is one of Kant's most important: pure reason is *organic*, not merely systematic in the sense of an external arrangement of parts but in the sense that each member exists for the sake of the whole and the whole for the sake of each. This means that the security of a principle cannot be assessed in isolation; it must be tested in its *durchgängige Beziehung* (thoroughgoing relation) to the whole. This organicism is the deepest justification of Kant's commitment to completeness as both possible and necessary.

For this reason, Kant continues, metaphysics has the rare good fortune — which can fall to no other rational science that has to do with objects (for *logic* concerns itself only with the form of thinking in general) — that, when through this Critique it has been brought onto the secure path of a science, it can fully comprise the entire field of cognitions belonging to it and thus complete its work and lay it down for posterity as a *capital* that can never be augmented (*nie zu vermehrender Hauptstuhl*), since it has to do merely with principles and with the limitations of their use, which are determined by those principles themselves. To this completeness, therefore, it is also bound as a foundational science, and of it must one be able to say: *nil actum reputans, si quid superesset agendum* (Lucan, *Phars.* 2.657, roughly: "considering nothing done if there is anything left to do"). The Lucan quotation has the heroic-Stoic tone appropriate to the claim: metaphysics is not satisfied with partial accomplishment; the partial is *nothing* as long as anything remains to do. This is a strong claim — that metaphysics is in principle finite and completable in a way that no other object-science is. The reason is that metaphysics' objects are *internal*: the principles of pure reason and their limitations are wholly within reason, and reason can therefore in principle inventory them completely.

But what then, one will ask, is that *treasure* (*Schatz*) which we intend to bequeath to posterity through such a critique-cleansed metaphysics, brought thereby into a lasting state? At a cursory survey of this work one will think to perceive that its use is yet only *negative*, namely, never to venture beyond the boundary of experience with speculative reason; and this is indeed its first use. But this immediately becomes *positive* as soon as one becomes aware that the principles by which speculative reason ventures beyond its boundary have in fact, when one considers them more closely, *not* expansion, but, considered carefully, *contraction* of our use of reason as their unavoidable consequence — since they really threaten to extend over everything the boundaries of *sensibility*, to which they properly belong, and so to displace altogether the pure (practical) use of reason. The argument that the apparently negative result of the Critique (the restriction of speculative reason to the bounds of experience) is in fact positive turns on a fine distinction. The dogmatic principles, if granted as legitimate transcendent uses, would extend the categories (substance, causality, etc., which properly apply only within sensibility) to the supersensible, and thereby threaten to subsume the supersensible (including the moral) under the natural laws of the categories. This would *displace* the pure practical use of reason — would, e.g., make it impossible to think freedom because everything would fall under natural causality. By restricting speculative reason, the Critique secures the room for practical reason — and this securing is the positive use.

Therefore a critique which restricts the former (speculative reason) is, so far, indeed *negative*; but, inasmuch as it thereby at the same time *removes* a *hindrance* which restricts, or even threatens to destroy, the latter (practical) use, it is in fact of positive and very important utility, as soon as one is convinced that there is an absolutely necessary practical use of pure reason (the moral) in which it inevitably extends itself beyond the boundaries of sensibility — for which it indeed needs no help from the speculative, yet must be secured against its counteraction in order not to fall into contradiction with itself. To deny this service of the Critique its positive use would be just as much as to say that the *police* render no positive use because their chief business is only to *bar* the violence which citizens have to fear from citizens, so that each may pursue his affairs quietly and securely. The police analogy is one of Kant's most ironic: the negative function of the police (preventing violence) is the very condition of the positive activity of citizens (their *Angelegenheiten*). So too with the Critique: its negative function (preventing the transcendent use of speculative reason) is the very condition of the positive activity of practical reason (acting under the moral law in a way that is not contradicted by speculative claims to know everything as natural cause and effect).

That space and time are only forms of sensible intuition, and so only conditions of the existence of things as appearances; that further we have no concepts of understanding, and consequently no elements at all for the cognition of things, except so far as an intuition can be given corresponding to these concepts; consequently we can have cognition of no object as a thing in itself but only insofar as it is an object of sensible intuition, i.e., as appearance — all this is proved in the analytic part of the Critique. From this there does indeed follow the restriction of all possible speculative cognition of reason to mere objects of experience. Yet, what must be carefully noted, there is always reserved that we must be able to *think* the same objects also as things in themselves, even if we cannot *cognize* them. The footnote attached here distinguishes the two concepts: to *cognize* an object, I must be able to prove its possibility (either after the testimony of experience from its actuality, or a priori through reason); but I can *think* whatever I want, so long as I do not contradict myself — i.e., so long as my concept is a possible thought, even though I cannot vouch for whether in the totality of all possibilities an object corresponds to it. To give such a concept objective validity (real possibility, the first having been merely logical), something more is required. This more, however, need not be sought in theoretical sources of cognition; it can lie also in practical ones. The distinction *Erkennen* vs. *Denken* is one of the most consequential distinctions of the Critique. *Erkennen* (cognition) requires that intuition (sensible, in our case) supply an object to which the concept refers; without intuition, there is no cognition but only thought. *Denken* (thinking) requires only logical consistency. This means that concepts of the supersensible (God, freedom, immortality) can be *thought* without contradiction even though they cannot be *cognized*. The footnote also introduces the practical sources of cognition that will fill out the second Critique.

For otherwise, Kant continues, the absurd proposition would follow that appearance exists without something that appears. Now let us assume that the distinction, made necessary by our Critique, of things as objects of experience from the very same as things in themselves, were not made at all: then the principle of causality, and with it the mechanism of nature in its determination, would absolutely have to hold of all things in general as efficient causes. Of one and the same being, e.g., the human soul, I would not be able to say that its will is free and yet at the same time subject to natural necessity, i.e., not free, without falling into an evident contradiction, because I have taken the soul in both propositions in *one and the same meaning*, namely as a thing in general (a thing in itself), and could not take it otherwise without prior critique. But if the Critique has not erred when it teaches one to take the object in *twofold* meaning, namely as appearance or as thing in itself; if the deduction of its concepts of understanding is correct, and consequently the principle of causality applies only to things taken in the first sense (so far as they are objects of experience) and the same are not subject to it in the second meaning: then the same will is thought as in appearance (in visible actions) necessarily conforming to natural law and so far *not* free, and on the other side, as belonging to a thing in itself, not subject to that law, hence as *free*, without any contradiction occurring here. This is the central application of the appearances/things in themselves distinction to the problem of freedom and natural necessity — the third Antinomy in proleptic form. The argument is that without the distinction, an evident contradiction arises (the soul as cause is both free and not free in *the same sense*); with the distinction, the contradiction dissolves because the soul is thought in two senses (as appearance, subject to natural law; as thing in itself, not so subject). This is the positive use of the Critique applied to the deepest practical question.

Although I cannot, Kant continues, cognize my soul considered from the latter side by any speculative reason (much less by empirical observation), and consequently cannot cognize freedom as a property of a being to which I ascribe effects in the world of sense — since I would have to cognize such a being as determined according to its existence and yet not in time, which is impossible because I cannot lay any intuition under my concept — yet I can *think* freedom for myself: i.e., the representation of it contains at least no contradiction in itself, when our critical distinction of both (the sensible and the intellectual) modes of representation, and the consequent restriction of the pure concepts of the understanding, and hence also of the principles flowing from them, holds. The argument is precisely calibrated: freedom cannot be *cognized* because cognition requires intuition, and the only intuition we have is sensible (i.e., temporal), but freedom would require an a-temporal determination. Freedom can however be *thought* without contradiction because the categories (especially causality), being restricted to appearances, do not apply to the supersensible thing in itself, and so the supersensible thing in itself can be thought as free without thereby contradicting the natural causal law that governs appearances.

Suppose now, Kant continues, that morality necessarily presupposes freedom (in the strictest sense) as a property of our will, in that it adduces practical original principles lying in our reason as data of the same a priori, which would be absolutely impossible without the presupposition of freedom; suppose speculative reason had proved that freedom cannot at all be thought: then necessarily that presupposition — namely, the moral one — would have to yield to that whose opposite contains an evident contradiction; consequently *freedom* and with it *morality* (for the opposite of morality contains no contradiction unless freedom is already presupposed) would have to give way to the *mechanism of nature*. But as it is, since for morality I need nothing more than that freedom should not contradict itself and so at least be able to be *thought*, without needing to have further insight into it — that it should put no obstacle whatever in the way of the mechanism of nature in the very same action (taken in another respect) — the doctrine of morality holds its place, and the doctrine of nature also holds its own, which however would not have taken place if the Critique had not previously instructed us about our unavoidable ignorance with respect to things in themselves and restricted everything which we can *cognize* theoretically to mere appearances. This is the famous structural argument for the compatibility of natural necessity and moral freedom. The argument has the shape: morality requires that freedom be thinkable; the Critique, by distinguishing appearances and things in themselves, shows that freedom is thinkable; therefore morality can hold its ground without being contradicted by natural science. The argument does not *prove* freedom; it shows that freedom is not impossible (because not contradictory). The proof of freedom (as practical reality) is the business of the second Critique. The argument is a model of negative philosophical service to a positive practical end.

The same exposition of the positive use of critical principles of pure reason, Kant continues, can be shown with respect to the concept of *God* and of the *simple nature* of our *soul*; which, however, for brevity's sake I pass over. So I cannot even *assume* God, freedom, and immortality on behalf of the necessary practical use of my reason if I do not at the same time *deprive* speculative reason of its pretension to transcendent insights, because to attain to these it must make use of such principles which, since they in fact reach only to objects of possible experience, when nevertheless applied to what cannot be an object of experience, in fact always change it into appearance and so declare every *practical extension* of pure reason impossible. *I had therefore to suspend knowledge (Wissen aufheben) in order to make room for faith (Glauben).* And the dogmatism of metaphysics — i.e., the prejudice of getting on in it without a critique of pure reason — is the true source of all the unbelief, contrary to morality, which is always very dogmatic. This is the most famous sentence of the entire preface: *Ich mußte also das Wissen aufheben, um zum Glauben Platz zu bekommen.* The word *aufheben* has the full Hegelian valence of suspending, sublating, lifting up — knowledge is not destroyed but *suspended at its claim to transcendent extension*, in order that practical faith (in the post-metaphysical sense, not in the confessional sense) may have room. The sentence is also a polemical reversal: dogmatic *unbelief* (the materialism, fatalism, atheism of the Enlightenment) is itself *dogmatic*, and it is therefore the very target of the Critique just as much as religious dogmatism. The Critique is therefore not anti-religion (though it dismantles the speculative proofs of God's existence) but anti-dogmatism, on both fronts.

If, therefore, with a systematic metaphysics drafted in accordance with the Critique of pure reason, it cannot be very difficult to bequeath to posterity a legacy, then this is no gift to be lightly esteemed; one may regard merely the cultivation of reason through the secure path of a science in general, in comparison with the groundless tapping and frivolous straying of the same without critique, or also the better employment of time of a youth eager for knowledge — who, in the ordinary dogmatism, so early and so much receives encouragement to argue easily about things of which it understands nothing and in which it, as no one in the world, will also never have insight into anything, or even to set out to invent new thoughts and opinions, and so to neglect the learning of solid sciences — most of all, however, when one reckons the inestimable advantage of putting an end, for all future time, to all objections against morality and religion in the *Socratic* manner, namely by the clearest proof of the *ignorance* of the opponents. For some metaphysics has always been in the world, and will hereafter also be found, and with it a *dialectic of pure reason*, since it is natural to reason. It is therefore the first and most important business of philosophy, once and for all, to deprive it of all detrimental influence by stopping up the source of the errors. The argument extends the practical utility of the Critique into educational and cultural domains: the youth misled by dogmatic confidence is freed by the Critique to *learn solid sciences* rather than parade in speculative pretensions. The Socratic strategy of refutation by demonstrating opponents' ignorance is the methodological pattern: the Critique does not prove the speculative supersensible but shows that no one can prove anything about it, and this very demonstration is the most secure protection of morality and religion against speculative attack. The *Dialektik der reinen Vernunft* is *natural* to reason — Kant insists on this throughout — and therefore the dialectic cannot be eliminated but only disciplined.

In this important change in the field of the sciences, Kant continues, and in the *loss* (*Verlust*) that speculative reason must suffer with respect to its hitherto imagined possessions, everything still remains, with regard to the universal human concern and the use that the world has hitherto drawn from the doctrines of pure reason, in the same advantageous state as it ever was, and the loss touches only the *monopoly of the schools*, by no means the *interest of mankind*. The distinction between the *Schulen* (academic schools) and the *Menschheit* (humanity in its concrete moral existence) is one of the most polemically effective in the preface. The Critique deprives the schools of their dogmatic claims to prove the immortality of the soul, the freedom of the will, and the existence of God by speculative argument — but these dogmatic proofs never *reached the public* and never *grounded the public's conviction* on these matters. The public's confidence in immortality, freedom, and God rests on quite other grounds — natural disposition, the clear presentation of duty, the order and beauty of nature — which the Critique does not touch.

I ask the most inflexible dogmatist, Kant continues, whether the proof of the perduration of our soul after death from the simplicity of substance, or the proof of the freedom of the will against the universal mechanism through the subtle, although powerless, distinctions of subjective and objective practical necessity, or the proof of the existence of God from the concept of an *ens realissimum* (the contingency of the changeable and the necessity of a first mover) — whether these proofs, after they went forth from the schools, ever could have reached the public and could have had the slightest influence on its conviction. If this has not happened, and cannot also, on account of the unsuitability of common human understanding for such subtle speculation, ever be expected: if rather, as for the first, the disposition noticeable in every human being of his nature, that he can never be satisfied by the temporal (as insufficient for the dispositions of his entire vocation), must alone have produced the hope of a *future life*; with respect to the second, the mere clear presentation of duties in opposition to all claims of inclinations the *consciousness of freedom*; and finally, as concerns the third, the glorious order, beauty, and providence that shine forth everywhere in nature, the belief in a *wise and great Author of the world* — the conviction spreading to the public, so far as it rests on rational grounds, must alone have brought about — then not only does this possession remain undisturbed but it gains in respect through the schools' being now instructed not to assume any higher and more extensive insight on a point that concerns the universal human concern than that to which the great (for us most worthy of esteem) multitude can also so easily attain, and so to restrict themselves merely to the cultivation of these universally accessible and morally sufficient grounds of proof. The argument is a defense of common sense against speculative pretension: the actual sources of the public's conviction on the three great questions of metaphysics are non-speculative (natural disposition, the moral consciousness, the order of nature), and these sources are not touched by the Critique. The Critique only deprives the *schools* of their *monopoly* on what they pretended to prove but in fact never proved to the public's satisfaction.

The change, therefore, concerns merely the arrogant pretensions of the schools, who in this matter (as elsewhere rightly in many other matters) would like to be regarded as the sole connoisseurs and preservers of such truths, of which they share with the public only the use, but the key thereof they retain for themselves (*quod mecum nescit, solus vult scire videri*: "what he, like me, does not know, he alone wants to appear to know") (Horace, *Epist.* II.i.87). Yet there is still provision for a fairer claim of the speculative philosopher. He remains always exclusively the *depositary* of a science useful to the public without its knowledge, namely the Critique of reason. For the latter can never become popular, nor has it any need to be popular; because as little as the people are receptive to the finely spun arguments for useful truths, just as little do the equally subtle objections against them ever come into their minds. On the other hand, since the school, like every man who raises himself to speculation, unavoidably falls into both, the school is obligated, through thorough investigation of the rights of speculative reason, once and for all to forestall the *scandal* which sooner or later must occur even to the people from the disputes into which metaphysicians (and as such ultimately even clergymen) without critique inevitably entangle themselves, and which afterwards even falsify their own teachings. Through this alone can the root be finally cut off from *materialism*, *fatalism*, *atheism*, *free-thinking unbelief*, *enthusiasm* and *superstition*, which can be generally harmful — and even, finally, from *idealism* and *skepticism*, which are more dangerous to the schools and can hardly pass over to the public. The role of the philosopher is here defined with precision: not as popular educator (the Critique is too technical), but as the *depositary* (Latin *depositarius* — one who holds something in trust for another) of a science that protects the public without the public's awareness. The philosopher's task is to prevent the *Skandal* that arises when academic disputes filter down into religious and moral practice and corrupt them. The catalog of dangerous ideologies — materialism, fatalism, atheism, *Schwärmerei* (enthusiasm), superstition, idealism, skepticism — covers the major intellectual threats of Kant's time, and the claim is that all are rooted in some form of dogmatism (either dogmatic affirmation or dogmatic denial of transcendent claims), and that the Critique cuts off this root at its source.

If governments, Kant continues, find it good to occupy themselves with the affairs of the learned, then it would be far more consistent with their wise provision for the sciences as well as for human beings, to favor the *freedom of such a critique*, by which alone the labors of reason can be brought onto a firm footing, than to support the ridiculous *despotism of the schools*, which raise a loud cry of public danger whenever one tears their cobwebs — of which the public yet has never taken notice and whose loss it can therefore never feel. The political point is sharp: governments should protect critical philosophy because critical philosophy protects them and the public from the genuine sources of harm (which are not the destruction of dogmatic cobwebs but the dogmatism itself). The metaphor of *Spinnenweben* — cobwebs — is dismissive: the schools' dogmatic structures are not solid edifices but webs that catch only those who venture into them.

The Critique, Kant continues, is not opposed to the *dogmatic* procedure of reason in its pure cognition as a science (for this must always be dogmatic, i.e., strictly demonstrative from secure a priori principles), but to *dogmatism*, i.e., the presumption of getting on with a pure cognition from concepts (philosophical), according to principles which reason has long had in use, without inquiry into the manner and right by which it has come to them. *Dogmatism* is therefore the dogmatic procedure of pure reason *without a preceding critique of its own faculty*. This opposition is not therefore to speak for the talkative shallowness, under the assumed name of *popularity*, or even for *skepticism*, which makes short shrift of all metaphysics; rather, the Critique is the necessary preliminary arrangement for the promotion of a thorough metaphysics as science, which must necessarily be carried out dogmatically and, according to the strictest demand, systematically, hence school-correctly (not popularly) — for this demand on it, since it is committed to carrying out its business wholly a priori, hence to the full satisfaction of speculative reason, is inexcusable. The crucial distinction between *dogmatisches Verfahren* (dogmatic procedure) and *Dogmatismus* (dogmatism) is here drawn with precision. The dogmatic procedure is the strict demonstration from a priori principles — the proper method of any rational science. Dogmatism is dogmatic procedure *without prior critique of the faculty*. The Critique is therefore not against dogmatic procedure but only against its premature, uncritical employment. The future metaphysics will itself be dogmatic in procedure, but only after the Critique has secured the right to it.

In the execution, therefore, of the plan which the Critique prescribes — i.e., in the future system of metaphysics — we must one day follow the strict method of the famous *Wolff*, the greatest among all dogmatic philosophers, who first gave the example (and through this example became the originator of the spirit of thoroughness in Germany, which has not yet been extinguished), how through lawful establishment of principles, distinct determination of concepts, attempted strictness of proofs, prevention of bold leaps in inferences, the secure path of a science is to be taken — who therefore was preeminently fitted to set such a science as metaphysics in this state, if it had occurred to him through critique of the *organ*, namely of pure reason itself, to prepare the field for himself in advance: a defect which is not so much to be ascribed to him as rather to the dogmatic mode of thinking of his age, and on this account the philosophers, both of his time and of all previous times, have nothing to reproach each other with. Those who reject his method of teaching and at the same time the procedure of the Critique of pure reason can have nothing else in mind than to cast off the *fetters* of *science* altogether, to transform work into play, certainty into opinion, and philosophy into *philodoxy*. The acknowledgement of Wolff is double-edged: Wolff is the *greatest* of dogmatic philosophers and the originator of the German spirit of thoroughness, but his defect is precisely that he proceeded dogmatically without prior critique of the organ (reason itself). The defect is attributed not to Wolff personally but to the dogmatic mode of thinking of his age — a polite but pointed historical relativization. The opposition to those who would reject both Wolff *and* the Critique is the opposition to the *Popularphilosophen*, who would dissolve philosophy into popular discourse and turn *Philosophie* (love of wisdom) into *Philodoxie* (love of opinion). Kant's allegiance is therefore to *both* the dogmatic procedure (when properly grounded) and the Critique, against the popular dissolution of both.

As far as this *second edition* is concerned, Kant continues, I have, as is proper, not wanted to let this occasion pass without remedying so far as possible the difficulties and obscurities from which various misinterpretations may have arisen, which acute men, perhaps not without my fault, have encountered in judging this book. In the *propositions* themselves and their grounds of proof, as also in the form and completeness of the plan, I have found nothing to change; which is to be attributed partly to the long examination to which I subjected them before I presented them to the public, partly to the constitution of the matter itself, namely the nature of a pure speculative reason, which contains a true *articulation* (*Gliederbau*), in which everything is organ — namely all for the sake of one, and each individual for the sake of all — so that every infirmity, however small, whether it be an error (mistake) or a defect, must inevitably betray itself in use. This *immutability* (*Unveränderlichkeit*), as I hope, my system will further maintain. Not self-conceit, but only the evidence which the experiment of the equality of the result effects in the course out from the smallest elements to the whole of pure reason, and in the return from the whole (for this is also given for itself through the final aim of the same in the practical) to each part — since the attempt to alter even the smallest part immediately brings forth contradictions, not merely of the system but of universal human reason — justifies me in this confidence. The B preface's claim of immutability is even stronger than the A preface's claim of completeness: the system, once correctly established, cannot be altered without producing contradictions both in itself and in universal human reason. This is again the organic metaphor (every part is *organ*, every part for the sake of all and all for each), and it grounds the methodological precept that revisions in the second edition can only concern *presentation*, not *substance*.

Still in the *presentation*, Kant continues, there is much to be done; and herein I have, with this edition, attempted improvements, which were intended partly to remedy the misunderstanding of the *Aesthetic*, especially that in the concept of time; partly the obscurity of the *Deduction of the Concepts of the Understanding*; partly the supposed lack of sufficient evidence in the *proofs of the Principles of the Pure Understanding*; partly, finally, the misinterpretation of the *Paralogisms* directed against rational psychology. To this point (namely only to the end of the first chapter of the transcendental Dialectic) and no further does my alteration of the manner of presentation extend, because the time was too short and with respect to the rest no misunderstanding of competent and impartial examiners came to my notice — examiners whom, even though I cannot name them with the praise that is due to them, the consideration I have given to their reminders will already, by itself, encounter them in their places. The catalog of revisions corresponds exactly to the major modifications between the A and B editions: the revised Aesthetic (especially the General Observations on the Aesthetic, B66-73), the new Deduction (B129-169), the new Principles section (with the new arguments for the analogies of experience), and the new chapter on the Paralogisms of Pure Reason (B399-432), including the new Refutation of Idealism. The scrupulous specification of where the revisions stop ("only to the end of the first chapter of the transcendental Dialectic") protects Kant against the charge of having silently changed his doctrine throughout.

With this improvement, however, a small loss for the reader is bound up, which could not be prevented without making the book too voluminous — namely, that various things, which do not indeed belong essentially to the completeness of the whole, but which many a reader might miss with regret since they could in another respect be useful, had to be omitted or treated more briefly, in order to make room for my, as I hope, now more comprehensible presentation, which fundamentally with respect to the propositions and even their grounds of proof alters absolutely nothing, but which in the method of the lecture sometimes departs so much from the previous one that it could not be accomplished by interpolations. This careful disclaimer is to forestall the objection that the second edition substantively differs from the first. Kant insists: nothing in substance has changed; only the manner of presentation has been altered. This claim was disputed even in Kant's lifetime (especially regarding the new Deduction and the Refutation of Idealism, where the substantive content does appear to shift). The footnote attached here gives the new Refutation of Idealism in detail — an extraordinary intervention in the preface itself, where Kant rewrites in the footnote a passage of the body of the text. The Refutation argues that the consciousness of my own existence in time is identically bound up with the consciousness of something persistent outside me, so that the external sense is not derivative from but co-original with the inner sense, and the existence of things outside us is therefore not merely a matter of faith but of experience.

This small loss, Kant continues, which can in any case, at each one's pleasure, be made up by comparison with the first edition, will, as I hope, be outweighed by the greater comprehensibility. I have noticed with grateful pleasure in various public writings (partly in the review of various books, partly in special treatises) that the spirit of thoroughness in Germany has not died out but has only been shouted down for a short time by the fashionable tone of a *genius-like freedom of thinking*, and that the thorny paths of the Critique, which lead to a school-correct but as such alone lasting and therefore most necessary science of pure reason, have not hindered courageous and clear-headed men from mastering it. To these meritorious men, who unite with the thoroughness of insight also the talent of a luminous presentation (of which I am precisely not aware in myself), I leave to perfect my work, here and there still defective with respect to the latter; for being refuted is in this case not the danger, but being misunderstood is. On my side, I cannot enter into controversies from now on, although I shall carefully heed all hints, whether from friends or opponents, in order to use them in the future execution of the system in accordance with this propaedeutic. The reference to *geniemäßige Freiheit im Denken* is a polemic against the *Sturm und Drang* and the early German romanticism, which Kant always regarded as a counter-current to the spirit of *Gründlichkeit*. The renunciation of controversy — Kant will not engage in disputes from now on — is a methodological decision: the system speaks for itself or not at all, and Kant will not be distracted from his architectonic project by polemical defense.

Since during these labors, Kant continues, I have already advanced rather deep into age (in this month I enter my sixty-fourth year), so I must, if I wish to carry out my plan to deliver the metaphysics of nature as well as of morals, as a confirmation of the correctness of the Critique of speculative as well as of practical reason, deal sparingly with the time, and await the elucidation of the obscurities scarcely avoidable at first in this work, as well as the defense of the whole, from those meritorious men who have made it their own. At individual passages, every philosophical lecture can be pinched (for it cannot appear as armored as the mathematical), nevertheless, the *articulation* of the system, considered as unity, does not run the slightest danger thereby — for an overview of which, when it is new, only few have the agility of mind, and yet fewer have the desire, because any innovation comes inconveniently to them. *Apparent* contradictions also let themselves be ferreted out in every, especially loose, freely flowing writing, when one compares individual passages, torn from their context, against each other — which, in the eyes of one who relies on foreign judgments, throw a disadvantageous light on it, but for one who has mastered the idea as a whole, are very easily resolved. The plea for the system's organic unity is repeated here as a defensive maneuver against piecemeal criticism. Philosophical exposition is *not as armored* as mathematical exposition (because it depends on words, not symbols, and words are always polysemous), but the systematic unity of the work is the standard against which individual passages should be judged, not the reverse. The closing methodological invocation of *die Idee im Ganzen* — the idea of the whole — is the controlling hermeneutic principle of the entire Critical philosophy.

Yet, Kant concludes, if a theory has consistency in itself, action and reaction, which at first threatened it with great danger, serve only with time to smooth out its unevennesses; and when men of impartiality, insight, and true popularity occupy themselves with it, they procure for it in short time also the requisite elegance. *Königsberg, in the month of April 1787\.* The closing is sober but optimistic: the test of a theory is its internal consistency, and the historical process of action and reaction will serve to remove not the substance but the superficial roughness. The reference to *wahrer Popularität* — true popularity — is significant: not the false popularity of the *Popularphilosophen* (which is shallow chatter), but a true popularity which retains the system's rigor while presenting it accessibly. The signature, with date, marks the formal close of the second preface. With it, the entire prefatory apparatus of the Critique is complete, and the work proper — the Introduction and the body — can begin.

## **Introduction \[Edition A\]**

### **I. Idea of Transcendental Philosophy**

*Experience* is without doubt, Kant begins, the first product that our understanding brings forth in working up the raw material of sensible sensations. It is therefore the first instruction, and in its progress so inexhaustible in new teaching, that the connected life of all future generations will never have a lack of new cognitions that can be gathered on this ground. Yet experience is by no means the only field in which our understanding lets itself be restricted. It tells us, indeed, what is, but not that it must necessarily be so and not otherwise. Just on that account, it gives us no true universality; and reason, which is so eager for this kind of cognition, is more excited by it than satisfied. Such universal cognitions, however, which at the same time have the character of inner necessity, must be — independently of experience — for themselves clear and certain; one therefore calls them *cognitions a priori*: while, on the contrary, that which is borrowed merely from experience is, as one expresses it, cognized only *a posteriori*, or empirically. This opening of the A introduction is more empiricist in tone than the B version. Kant grants experience the role of the *first product* (*erstes Produkt*) of the understanding — which is to say, experience is itself an achievement, not a raw given, but the *first* such achievement of the understanding's working over of *roher Stoff*. The distinction between experience and a priori cognition is then introduced by way of two marks of experience: it tells what is but not what must be, and it offers no true universality. By contrast, a priori cognition has *inner necessity* and *true universality* — marks that experience alone cannot supply. The editorial note records that this paragraph and the next were *struck through* (*gestrichen*) in Kant's Handexemplar (Kh), confirming Kant's later decision to replace this introduction with the more rigorous B version.

It now appears, Kant continues — and this is exceedingly remarkable — that cognitions are mixed even into our experiences which must have their origin a priori, and which perhaps serve only to provide our representations of the senses with connection. For if one removes from the former everything that belongs to the senses, certain *original concepts* and the *judgments* generated from them still remain over, which must have arisen entirely a priori, independently of experience, because they make it possible to say of objects appearing to the senses more than mere experience would teach — or at least one believes that one can say more — and because assertions thereby contain *true universality* and *strict necessity*, which mere empirical cognition cannot supply. The seeming paradox — that even in experience itself there are non-experiential ingredients — is the entry into the transcendental question. The argument's structure is regressive: from the *factum* that we make claims about objects of the senses that go beyond what mere experience could teach (claims with strict necessity and true universality), we are led to conclude that there must be *ursprüngliche Begriffe* and *Urteile* originating a priori. This regressive argument from the actuality of synthetic a priori cognition to the conditions of its possibility is the basic shape of all transcendental arguments in the Critique. The criterion of true universality (*wahre Allgemeinheit*) and strict necessity (*strenge Notwendigkeit*) is the *Merkmal* (mark) by which a priori cognition is distinguished from empirical, and the next sections of the introduction (both A and B) will refine this criterion.

## **Introduction \[Edition B\]**

### **I. On the Distinction between Pure and Empirical Cognition**

*That* all our cognition begins with experience, Kant opens, there is no doubt at all; for through what should the cognitive faculty otherwise be awakened to exercise, if it did not occur through objects which stir our senses and partly themselves produce representations, partly set our understanding's activity in motion to compare these, to combine or separate them, and so to work up the raw material of sensible impressions into a cognition of objects which is called *experience*? According to time, then, no cognition precedes experience in us, and with experience all begins. The opening sentence of the B introduction is one of the most famous in the Critique, and one of the most carefully calibrated. The thesis is that *all* our cognition *begins* with experience — a concession to empiricism (against the Cartesian doctrine of innate ideas) that grants experience the temporal priority over any cognition we have. The reason given is psychological: the cognitive faculty (*Erkenntnisvermögen*) requires an *Erweckung* (awakening) to exercise, and this awakening occurs only through objects stirring the senses. The verbs are carefully chosen: objects *rühren* the senses (touch, affect), *bewirken* representations (effect, bring about), and set the understanding into the motion of *vergleichen, verknüpfen, trennen* (compare, combine, separate). All this issues in *Erfahrung* — experience, in the strict Kantian sense of cognition of objects (not mere sensory awareness). The phrase *der Zeit nach* (according to time) qualifies the claim precisely: this priority is temporal, not logical or transcendental. The next paragraph will draw the consequence of this qualification.

But although all our cognition *begins* with experience, Kant continues, it does not on that account all *arise from* experience. For it may well be that even our experiential cognition is a composite of what we receive through impressions, and what our own cognitive faculty (merely occasioned by sensible impressions) yields from itself — an addition (*Zusatz*) which we do not distinguish from that basic material until long practice has made us attentive to it and skilled in separating it. The distinction between *anfangen* (begin) and *entspringen* (arise) is the philosophically decisive distinction of the opening paragraphs. Temporally, every cognition begins with experience; but the *source* (*Ursprung*) of all the components of a cognition is not therefore experience. The composite may include contributions from the cognitive faculty itself — contributions which are *occasioned* by sensible impressions (so that they would not appear without those impressions) but are not *derived* from them (so that their content is independent of what the impressions contribute). The reason for the difficulty of recognizing this addition is psychological-genetic: we encounter cognition as a finished whole, and only long practice in *Absonderung* (separation, abstraction) makes us aware of the *Zusatz* contributed by the faculty itself. The methodological consequence is decisive: a critique of pure reason is precisely the disciplined exercise of this separation, by which the contributions of the faculty are isolated from the contributions of impressions.

It is therefore, Kant continues, at least a question still requiring closer investigation and not to be dismissed at first appearance: *whether there is any such cognition independent of experience and even of all impressions of the senses*. Such cognitions one calls *a priori*, and one distinguishes them from the *empirical*, which have their sources *a posteriori*, namely in experience. The question is now precisely formulated. The negative formulation — *unabhängig* (independent) — is important: the question is not whether there are cognitions *prior in time* to experience (the answer is no, by the previous paragraph) but whether there are cognitions *independent* of experience in respect of their origin and validity. The terminology — *a priori* (from what is prior) and *a posteriori* (from what is posterior) — is taken over from the Scholastic tradition, but Kant gives it a new and precise sense by tying it to the *Unabhängigkeit von der Erfahrung* (independence of experience) rather than to the order of explanation from cause to effect (as in Scholastic usage).

That expression, Kant continues — *a priori* — is however not yet determined enough to designate the entire sense, appropriate to the proposed question. For one is wont to say of many a cognition derived from sources of experience that we are *capable* of it or *participate* in it *a priori*, because we derive it not immediately from experience but from a general rule which we have nevertheless derived from experience. So one says of one who undermined the foundation of his house: he could know *a priori* that it would fall in — that is, he did not need to wait for the experience that it actually did fall in. But entirely *a priori* he yet could not know this. For *that bodies are heavy and therefore fall when their support is taken away*, he had nevertheless to be informed of this beforehand through experience. The argument refines the concept of a priori cognition. In ordinary usage, *a priori* is sometimes used for cognitions that are derived not immediately from experience but from general rules themselves derived from experience. Such cognitions Kant will exclude from his strict sense of *a priori*. The example of the foundation-undermining illustrates the distinction: predicting that the house will fall *appears* to be a priori (we do not have to wait to see), but it is in fact derived from an empirical generalization (bodies are heavy, supports are needed, etc.), and so is at most *comparatively* a priori. The Kantian sense of strict a priori requires independence from experience *altogether*, not merely from this or that experience.

We shall therefore, Kant continues, in what follows, understand by *a priori cognitions* not such as occur independently of this or that experience, but rather such as occur *absolutely* (*schlechterdings*) independently of all experience. To them are opposed *empirical cognitions*, or those which are possible only a posteriori, i.e., through experience. Among a priori cognitions, however, those are called *pure* in which nothing empirical is mixed. So, for example, the proposition *every alteration has its cause* is a proposition a priori, but not pure, because *alteration* is a concept that can be drawn only from experience. The distinction *a priori / pure a priori* is sharp and methodologically important. A cognition is *a priori* if it is absolutely independent of experience in its validity; it is *pure a priori* if no component of it is empirical. The example *jede Veränderung hat ihre Ursache* (every alteration has its cause) is a a priori proposition (because the connection of alteration and cause is universal and necessary), but not pure because *Veränderung* (alteration) is itself an empirical concept. By contrast, a proposition like *every event has a cause* (with *Ereignis* taken without reference to time) might be pure. This distinction will become important in the section on synthetic a priori judgments, where Kant will argue that the most important kinds of philosophical cognition (and the foundational propositions of mathematics and physics) are pure synthetic a priori, not impure.

### **II. We Are in Possession of Certain a priori Cognitions, and Even the Common Understanding Is Never without Such**

It comes here, Kant begins, to a *mark* (*Merkmal*) by which we can securely distinguish a pure cognition from empirical ones. Experience teaches us, indeed, that something is constituted thus or thus, but not that it cannot be otherwise. *First*, then, if a proposition is thought together with its *necessity*, it is a judgment a priori; if it is moreover not derived from any other than such as is itself again valid as a necessary proposition, it is absolutely a priori. This first paragraph of section II announces a methodological move from the *definition* of a priori cognition (sections I) to its *identification* by criteria (*Merkmale*). The first criterion is *Notwendigkeit* (necessity): if a proposition is thought *with* its necessity, it is a priori. The reason is that experience can only ever teach us what *is* (the *Faktum*), not what *must be*. Necessity therefore cannot come from experience and must come from a non-empirical source — namely, from the faculty itself. The qualifier *schlechterdings a priori* (absolutely a priori) is added when the proposition is not derived from another a priori proposition that might itself be open to empirical contamination.

Let me continue the criteria. *Secondly*: experience never gives its judgments true or strict, but only assumed and comparative universality (through induction), so that it must properly mean: as far as we have hitherto perceived, no exception to this or that rule is found. If, then, a judgment is thought in strict universality — i.e., so that no exception at all is allowed as possible — then it is not derived from experience, but is valid absolutely a priori. Empirical universality is therefore only an arbitrary intensification of validity, from that which holds in most cases to that which holds in all, as, e.g., in the proposition *all bodies are heavy*; whereas, conversely, where strict universality belongs essentially to a judgment, this indicates a particular source of cognition for it, namely a faculty of cognition a priori. Necessity and strict universality are therefore secure marks of a cognition a priori, and also belong together inseparably. Yet since it is sometimes easier in their use to show their empirical limitation than the contingency in the judgments, or it is sometimes more evident the unrestricted universality which we ascribe to a judgment than its necessity, it is advisable to use the said criteria separately, each of which is for itself infallible. The second criterion is *strict universality* (*strenge Allgemeinheit*), as opposed to *comparative universality* (*komparative Allgemeinheit*) which is what induction yields. Strict universality admits *no possible exception*, while comparative universality only summarizes what has been observed so far. Strict universality, like necessity, cannot come from experience and therefore points to a non-empirical source. The two criteria — necessity and strict universality — are mutually entailing in their full strength but methodologically separable in their application, since necessity is sometimes more visible and universality at other times more evident.

Now, Kant continues, it is easy to show that there really *are* such necessary and in the strictest sense universal, hence pure judgments a priori, in human cognition. If one wishes an example from the sciences, one need only look at all the propositions of mathematics; if one wishes one from the most common use of the understanding, the proposition that every alteration must have a cause can serve — indeed in the latter the very concept of a cause so obviously contains the concept of a necessity of connection with an effect and a strict universality of the rule, that it would be entirely lost if one wished to derive it, as Hume did, from a frequent association of what happens with what precedes, and a custom arising therefrom (hence a merely subjective necessity) of connecting representations. One can also, even without needing such examples in proof of the actuality of pure a priori principles in our cognition, prove their indispensability for the possibility of experience itself, hence prove them a priori. For where would experience itself get its certainty if all the rules according to which it proceeds were always in turn empirical, hence contingent — rules which one could therefore hardly hold as first principles. The argument here is twofold. First, examples confirm that we *do* have synthetic a priori cognitions: mathematics provides them in abundance, and the principle of causality is an instance from common understanding. Second — and more powerfully — even if we did not have such examples, we could prove the existence of a priori principles by their indispensability for the *possibility of experience itself*: experience as such requires certain rules, and these rules cannot themselves be merely empirical (else they would be contingent and could not be first principles). The mention of Hume is sharp: Hume's derivation of causality from custom yields a *subjektive Notwendigkeit*, but the concept of cause carries an *objective* necessity, which custom cannot supply. This is the proleptic articulation of the response to Hume that the Analytic of Principles will develop.

We can be content here, Kant continues, with having presented the pure use of our cognitive faculty as a fact, together with its marks. But not merely in *judgments*, but even in *concepts* themselves, an a priori origin of some of them shows itself. Take from your experiential concept of *body* all that is empirical in it, one piece at a time: the color, the hardness or softness, the weight, even the impenetrability; there still remains the *space* that it occupied (which has now entirely vanished), and you cannot leave this aside. Likewise, if you take from your empirical concept of any corporeal or incorporeal object all the properties that experience teaches you, you nevertheless cannot deprive it of that whereby you think it as a *substance*, or as adhering to a substance (although this concept contains more determination than that of an object in general). You must therefore, compelled by the necessity with which this concept presses upon you, admit that it has its seat a priori in your cognitive faculty. The argument now extends from judgments to concepts. The method is *Absonderung* (separation) by progressive abstraction: from the empirical concept of body, one removes all empirical features (color, hardness, weight, impenetrability), and what remains is *space*; from the concept of any object, one removes empirical features, and what remains is *substance*. These remainders cannot be removed without destroying the very possibility of thinking the object as object; therefore they belong to the a priori structure of the cognitive faculty. The procedure is what Kant will later call *transzendentale Erörterung* (transcendental exposition) — the demonstration that a concept's content is partly a priori by showing that no further empirical removal is possible.

### **III. Philosophy Needs a Science Which Determines the Possibility, the Principles, and the Extent of All Cognitions a priori**

What is far more significant, Kant continues — far more than what has been shown so far — is that certain cognitions even leave the field of all possible experiences, and through concepts to which no corresponding object can be given anywhere in experience, *appear* to enlarge the extent of our judgments beyond all the limits of experience. The decisive escalation occurs here. Section II established that a priori cognitions exist and are intermingled with experience; section III now points to a far more remarkable kind of cognition — those that not only operate within experience but pretend to leave its field entirely. The careful verb *zu erweitern den Anschein haben* ("to have the appearance of extending") is crucial: Kant does not yet concede that such judgments actually do extend our cognition; he says only that they have this appearance (*Anschein*). The whole question of whether this appearance is reality or illusion is the central question of the entire Critique. The objects in question are those that exceed the field of any possible experience — paradigmatically, the objects of traditional metaphysics: God, the soul, the world as totality. The concepts by which we attempt to reach them have no corresponding intuition (*kein entsprechender Gegenstand in der Erfahrung*), and yet they are forwarded as if they extended cognition.

And precisely in these latter cognitions, Kant continues, which go beyond the world of sense, where experience can give neither guidance nor correction, lie the investigations of our reason which we hold, by their importance, far more excellent and their final aim far more sublime than everything that the understanding can learn in the field of appearances; in which we — even at the risk of error — rather venture everything than that we should abandon such pressing investigations on any ground of misgiving, or out of disdain and indifference. The paragraph valorizes the metaphysical impulse precisely while exhibiting its precariousness. The investigations that go *beyond the world of sense* (*über die Sinnenwelt hinausgehen*) lack any *Leitfaden* (guideline) and any *Berichtigung* (correction) from experience. The standard scientific apparatus of confirmation and refutation is absent. Yet these investigations are not therefore dismissed — on the contrary, reason holds them as *vorzüglicher* (more excellent) and their *Endabsicht* (final aim) as more *erhaben* (sublime) than anything achievable in the field of appearances. The rhetoric — *alles wagen* (venture everything), *angelegene Untersuchungen* (pressing investigations) — points to reason's existential investment in these questions. The motivation for the critical project is therefore not skeptical dismissal but the protection and defense of an inquiry to which reason cannot but commit itself. The implicit polemic is twofold: against the dogmatist who pursues these inquiries without regard for the risk of error, and against the indifferentist who abandons them out of *Bedenklichkeit* (misgiving), *Geringschätzung* (disdain), or *Gleichgültigkeit* (indifference). The first three sins — misgiving, disdain, indifference — characterize the empiricist temperament; Kant aligns himself, against them, with the metaphysical impulse, but as one who will conduct the inquiry critically.

These unavoidable problems of pure reason itself, Kant continues, are *God, freedom, and immortality*. The science, however, whose final aim, with all its preparations, is properly directed only to the solution of these, is called *metaphysics*, whose procedure at the beginning is *dogmatic* — that is, without prior examination of the capacity or incapacity of reason for so great an undertaking, it confidently undertakes the execution. The three names are spoken: *Gott, Freiheit, Unsterblichkeit*. These are the *unvermeidliche Aufgaben* (unavoidable tasks) of pure reason — unavoidable because reason poses them to itself by its own nature, not as optional research projects but as questions that arise of necessity from the structure of rational inquiry itself. They will reappear under different names throughout the Critique: in the Dialectic as the three transcendental Ideas (psychological, cosmological, theological), in the Canon of Pure Reason as the three objects of the highest interest of reason, in the second Critique as the postulates of practical reason. The definition of *Metaphysik* is then given functionally: the science whose final aim, with all its preparations (*Zurüstungen* — a striking word, suggesting armaments or scaffolding), is *only* the solution of these problems. The diagnosis of metaphysics' *dogmatic* procedure — undertaking execution without prior examination of the capacity (*Vermögen*) or incapacity (*Unvermögen*) of reason — is the diagnosis the Critique is designed to remedy. The Critique's procedure will be precisely the *prior examination* of capacity and incapacity, before any execution is attempted.

Now it appears natural, Kant continues, that as soon as one has left the ground of experience, one should not immediately, with cognitions one possesses without knowing whence, and on the credit of principles whose origin one does not know, erect a building — without having first assured oneself of its foundation through careful investigations; so that one would rather have raised the question long beforehand: how does the understanding come to all these cognitions a priori, and what extent, validity, and worth may they have. In fact, nothing is more natural, if one understands by the word *natural* what *should* happen in a fair and rational way; but if one understands by it what *usually* happens, then in turn nothing is more natural and comprehensible than that this investigation has long had to be left undone. The structure of the paragraph is a careful play on the word *natürlich*. In a normative sense, it would be natural — i.e., rational, fitting, what one would expect of a careful inquirer — to investigate the foundation before erecting the building; the architectural metaphor will recur throughout the Critique. But in a descriptive sense — *what usually happens* — the opposite is what is natural: namely, that the inquiry is omitted. The duality of *natürlich* maps onto the duality between what reason *should* do (the demand of critique) and what it *actually has been doing* (the history of dogmatic metaphysics). The retrospective question — *how does the understanding come to all these cognitions a priori, and what extent, validity, and worth may they have* — is in fact the question of the entire Critique. Kant identifies the failure of metaphysics with the failure to pose this question in advance.

For one part of these cognitions, Kant continues — the mathematical — is in the old possession of reliability, and thereby gives a favorable expectation also for the others, although they may be of an entirely different nature. Besides, if one is beyond the circle of experience, then one is sure not to be contradicted by experience. The charm of expanding one's cognitions is so great that one can be stopped in one's progress only by a clear contradiction one runs into. This, however, can be avoided if one makes one's *fictions* (*Erdichtungen*) only carefully, without their being less fictions for that. The diagnosis of why the critical question was not posed sooner is now historical-psychological. Mathematics' undisputed reliability was misleadingly extended as a favorable presumption to other a priori cognitions, even though their *nature* was wholly different. And once one has left the *Kreis der Erfahrung*, one is also free from the corrective force of experiential refutation — a freedom which, paradoxically, encourages rather than discourages further advance. The only remaining check on dogmatic metaphysics is internal consistency (the avoidance of *clear contradiction*), and this check can be evaded by careful construction. The result is a body of supposed cognition whose elements remain *Erdichtungen* — fictions — however carefully constructed. The word *Erdichtung* will reappear later as part of Kant's diagnosis of speculative metaphysics: the dialectical illusions of pure reason are not random errors but careful constructions which simulate the form of knowledge while being void of objective content.

Mathematics, Kant continues, gives us a brilliant example of how far we can carry it, independently of experience, in cognition a priori. Now it is occupied with objects and cognitions only in so far as such can be presented in intuition. But this circumstance is easily overlooked, because the said intuition itself can be given a priori, hence is hardly distinguished from a mere pure concept. Excited by such a proof of the power of reason, the drive to extension sees no bounds. The fact-pattern of mathematics is one of the recurring puzzles of the Critique. On the one hand, mathematics achieves genuine a priori cognition (the synthetic a priori judgments of arithmetic and geometry); on the other hand, this achievement is parasitic on a feature that escapes notice — namely, that mathematical objects are presented in a pure intuition (*Anschauung a priori*), which is therefore not a mere concept. The reason mathematics is misleading as a precedent for metaphysics is that one assumes mathematical reason proceeds purely from concepts; in fact, it proceeds from concepts *only with the aid of pure intuition*. The transcendental Aesthetic will later argue that space and time are precisely this *pure intuition* — forms of sensibility, given a priori, without which mathematics would be impossible. The diagnosis here is proleptic: the false generalization from mathematics to all a priori cognition rests on a misunderstanding of mathematics itself.

The light dove, Kant continues, cleaving the air in free flight and feeling its resistance, might form the representation that in airless space it would do far better. Likewise Plato left the world of sense, because it sets the understanding such narrow bounds, and ventured beyond it on the wings of the ideas, into the empty space of the pure understanding. He did not notice that with his efforts he gained no way forward — for he had no *foothold* (*Widerhalt*), as it were, to serve as ground, on which he could brace himself and apply his forces to set the understanding into motion. This famous image is one of the most cited in the Critique. The dove takes the resistance of air as a hindrance, when in fact that resistance is what makes flight possible; remove the resistance, and the dove cannot fly at all. By analogy, reason takes the limitations of sensible experience as a hindrance to metaphysical insight, when in fact those very limitations are what give cognition its purchase. Plato is named as the paradigm of this error — the philosopher who fled the sensible world for the supposed *empty space of pure understanding* and discovered there no traction, no *Widerhalt*. The word *Widerhalt* (foothold, counter-hold) is precise: cognition requires not only an active faculty (the *Verstand*) but also something against which it can press, something that *resists* and thereby provides a reference-point. Without such resistance, the understanding spins in the void without advancing. The image will be picked up again in the Transcendental Dialectic, where the absence of *Widerhalt* in the supersensible domain will be the structural source of dialectical illusion.

It is, however, the usual fate of human reason in speculation, Kant continues, to finish its building as early as possible, and only afterwards to investigate whether the ground was well laid for it. Then, however, all kinds of palliations (*Beschönigungen*) are sought out, to console us about its soundness, or rather to reject altogether such a late and dangerous examination. But what holds us free from all anxiety and suspicion during the building, and flatters us with apparent thoroughness, is this. A great part — and perhaps the greatest — of the business of our reason consists in *analyses* (*Zergliederungen*) of the concepts that we already have of objects. This delivers us a quantity of cognitions which, though they are nothing more than *clarifications* or *elucidations* (*Aufklärungen* or *Erläuterungen*) of what was already thought (though in a confused way) in our concepts, are nevertheless esteemed as new insights *at least with respect to form*, although materially or as regards content they do not enlarge but only *set apart* the concepts we have. The architectural metaphor returns: the dogmatic builder lays the foundation last, if at all. The *Beschönigungen* (palliations, glossings-over) are the rhetorical strategies of school-metaphysics by which the foundational question is deferred or evaded. The key analytical move is now made: a great part of the business of reason consists in *Zergliederungen* — analyses, in the strict sense of decomposition of concepts into their components. Such analyses yield *Erläuterungen* (clarifications) of what was already thought in the concept, however confusedly. They look like extensions of cognition (they have the *form* of new insight) but materially they extend nothing — they only *set apart* (*aus einander setzen*) the components of an already-possessed concept. This is the proleptic articulation of the analytic-synthetic distinction which the next section will develop systematically.

Since this procedure now yields an actual cognition a priori, Kant continues, which has a secure and useful progress, reason — without itself noticing — under this pretense (*Vorspiegelung*) smuggles in (*erschleicht*) assertions of an entirely different kind, in which reason adds to given concepts entirely foreign ones and, indeed, a priori, without our knowing how it comes to do so and without our even letting such a question come into our thoughts. I will therefore deal at once with the *distinction of this twofold kind of cognition*. The argument now identifies the precise mechanism of dogmatic metaphysics' self-deception. Analytic cognition (which decomposes concepts) is indeed legitimately a priori; but on the back of this legitimate procedure, reason *smuggles in* (*erschleicht* — a word with juridical overtones, suggesting illicit acquisition) a quite different kind of cognition, one in which entirely foreign concepts are added to given ones. The verb *erschleichen* will reappear at decisive points in the Critique, especially in the Dialectic, where the dialectical illusions are diagnosed as *Erschleichungen* — illicit transitions from one kind of cognition to another. The methodological move announced here — *to deal at once with the distinction of this twofold kind of cognition* — is the transition to the next section. The argument has now reached the point where the analytic/synthetic distinction becomes thematic, and the question of synthetic a priori cognition (the central question of the Critique) can be precisely formulated.

### **IV. On the Distinction between Analytic and Synthetic Judgments**

In all judgments, Kant begins, in which the relation of a subject to a predicate is thought — if I consider only the affirmative, since the application to the negative is afterwards easy — this relation is possible in two ways. Either the predicate B belongs to the subject A as something that is (in a covert way) *contained* in this concept A, or B lies entirely *outside* the concept A, although it stands in connection with it. In the first case I call the judgment *analytic*, in the other, *synthetic*. Analytic judgments (the affirmative) are therefore those in which the connection of the predicate with the subject is thought *through identity*; those in which this connection is thought *without identity* shall be called synthetic judgments. The methodological scope of the distinction is set first: affirmative subject-predicate judgments. The negative case is set aside because its application is *afterwards easy* — though this restriction has provoked considerable scholarly debate, since not all judgments are subject-predicate in form (existential, relational, hypothetical judgments are problem cases). The two-way distinction is then introduced through the in/out metaphor: B is *contained* in A (covertly, *versteckter Weise*), or B lies *entirely outside* A. The first case is the analytic judgment, identified as one in which the predicate-subject connection is *through identity* (*durch Identität*); the second is the synthetic judgment, in which the connection is *without identity*. The criterion of identity is significant: an analytic judgment is one in which what is asserted of the subject is in some sense the subject itself (or part of it), so that the predicate is already implicit in the subject. A synthetic judgment, by contrast, joins to the subject something that is not it.

The former, Kant continues, could also be called *judgments of clarification* (*Erläuterungsurteile*), and the latter *judgments of extension* (*Erweiterungsurteile*); because the former add nothing through the predicate to the concept of the subject, but only *decompose* it through analysis into its *partial concepts* (*Teilbegriffe*) which were already thought in it (though confusedly); whereas the latter add to the concept of the subject a predicate which was not thought in it at all, and which could not have been drawn out of it by any analysis. The alternative terminology — *Erläuterung* / *Erweiterung* — captures the functional difference. Analytic judgments *clarify* what we already have; synthetic judgments *extend* what we have. The analysis of concepts (*Zergliederung*) into *Teilbegriffe* (partial concepts) is the analytic operation; the addition of foreign predicates is the synthetic operation. The reference to confused thinking (*verworren*) is important: the partial concepts contained in the subject are already there, but only confusedly. The analytic operation makes them *distinct* (*deutlich*) without adding to them. This Leibnizian/Wolffian vocabulary of *verworren* / *deutlich* is here put to a new use: it does not name a progress within a single kind of cognition, but distinguishes the very nature of analytic cognition (which is the move from confused to distinct possession of the same content) from synthetic cognition (which adds new content).

For example, Kant continues, when I say: *all bodies are extended*, this is an analytic judgment. For I need not go outside the concept that I connect with the word *body* in order to find extension as connected with it; I only need to *decompose* that concept — i.e., to become conscious of the manifold which I always think in it — in order to find this predicate in it; it is therefore an analytic judgment. On the contrary, when I say: *all bodies are heavy*, then the predicate is something quite different from what I think in the mere concept of a body in general. The addition of such a predicate therefore yields a synthetic judgment. The two canonical examples are set in opposition. *Alle Körper sind ausgedehnt* is analytic because *Ausdehnung* (extension) is part of what one thinks in the concept of body — one does not need to go outside the concept to find it. *Alle Körper sind schwer* is synthetic because *Schwere* (heaviness, weight) is not part of what one thinks in the concept of body — one needs to go outside the concept (to experience, in fact) to find it. The contrast is doubly significant. First, it is a contrast between definition and substantive content: that bodies are extended belongs to the very definition of body in Kant's framework, while that they are heavy is an empirically discovered fact. Second, the example of weight is not arbitrarily chosen: Newtonian physics had recently shown that gravity is a property of matter, but the connection of gravity with matter is contingent and empirical (one could imagine a body in a gravity-free field). Kant uses the example to drive home that synthetic predicates *extend* what we knew about the subject in a way that analytic predicates do not.

*Empirical judgments, as such, are altogether synthetic.* For it would be incongruous to ground an analytic judgment on experience, since I need not go outside my concept to formulate the judgment, and I therefore need no testimony of experience for it. That a body is extended is a proposition which stands fast a priori, and not an empirical judgment. For before I go to experience, I have all the conditions of my judgment already in the concept, from which I need only draw out the predicate according to the principle of contradiction, and thereby at the same time can become conscious of the necessity of the judgment, which experience would never even teach me. This is the \[B\]-edition addition (drawn from the *Prolegomena*) which makes a sharper point than the \[A\] text. *Erfahrungsurteile sind insgesamt synthetisch.* All experiential judgments are without exception synthetic. The reason is structural: experience teaches us only what is added to a concept by way of supplementation; what is already in the concept is independent of experience. The point will be important in section V, where the synthetic a priori judgments of mathematics and physics will be shown to be neither analytic (which would make them not extend cognition) nor empirical (which would make them not strictly necessary), but rather a third class — synthetic and yet a priori. The example *that a body is extended* is here used to make the point that this judgment is analytic and therefore a priori (in fact, all analytic judgments are a priori, since they appeal only to the principle of contradiction). The contrast with empirical judgments will license, in the next section, the third case — synthetic *and* a priori, which is the central problem of the Critique.

On the other hand, although I do not include the predicate of heaviness in the concept of a body in general, the concept yet designates an object of experience through a *part* of this experience, to which I can add other parts of the same experience as belonging to the first. I can cognize the concept of body beforehand analytically through the marks of extension, impenetrability, shape, etc., which are all thought in this concept. Now I extend my cognition, however, and, looking back to the experience from which I had abstracted this concept of body, I find heaviness also always connected with the above marks; and so I add this, *as a predicate*, *synthetically* to that concept. It is therefore experience on which the possibility of the synthesis of the predicate of heaviness with the concept of body is grounded, because both concepts — although the one is not contained in the other — nevertheless belong to one another *as parts of a whole*, namely of experience, which is itself a synthetic connection of intuitions, *though only contingently*. The argument now articulates the logical structure of empirical synthesis. The concept of body, considered analytically, has its component marks (extension, impenetrability, shape). To add heaviness to it requires looking *back* to experience, in which body is encountered together with heaviness. The phrase *als Teile eines Ganzen* (as parts of a whole) names experience as the totality in which the two concepts are connected. Experience itself is described as a *synthetische Verbindung der Anschauungen* (synthetic connection of intuitions) — a description that anticipates the central thesis of the Transcendental Analytic, namely that experience itself is the work of synthesis. The qualifier *zufälliger Weise* (contingently) is crucial: empirical syntheses are always contingent, because experience itself does not necessitate the connection of any two particular concepts. The contrast with synthetic a priori judgments, which are *necessary* connections, will be drawn immediately.

But in the case of synthetic judgments a priori, Kant continues, this *means of help* (*Hülfsmittel*) is wholly missing. If I am to go beyond the concept A in order to recognize another B as connected with it, what is it that I rely on, and through which the synthesis becomes possible — since I do not have here the advantage of looking around for it in the field of experience? Take the proposition: *Everything that happens has its cause*. In the concept of *something that happens*, I do indeed think an existence before which a time precedes, etc., and from this analytic judgments can be drawn. But the concept of a *cause* lies entirely outside that concept, and indicates something different from that which happens, and is therefore not at all contained in this latter representation. How then do I come to say of *what happens in general* something quite different from it, and to recognize the concept of cause as belonging to it, although not contained in it? The central question is now precisely posed. In the empirical case, the *Hülfsmittel* of experience supplies the X — the third term that allows synthesis. In the a priori case, this aid is missing, and yet synthetic a priori judgments are made. The example chosen is the principle of causality — *Alles, was geschieht, hat seine Ursache* — which will be the central case throughout the Critique (and the centerpiece of the Second Analogy in the Transcendental Analytic). The analytic component of the concept *what happens* is identified: an existence preceded by a time. But the concept of a cause is not part of what is thought in *what happens* — to say that what happens has a cause is to add to the concept something *quite different* (*ganz Verschiedenes*). And yet we make this judgment with strict necessity (every event has a cause, not just most events). What licenses this transition? The question is now formulated; the rest of section IV begins to articulate the depth of the puzzle.

What is here the unknown \= X, Kant continues, on which the understanding relies when it believes itself to discover, outside the concept A, a foreign predicate B which it nonetheless takes to be connected with it? *Experience cannot be it*, because the principle adduced adds this second representation to the first not merely with greater universality, but also with the *expression of necessity*, hence wholly a priori and out of mere concepts. Now the entire final aim of our speculative cognition a priori rests on such synthetic, i.e., *extension-principles* (*Erweiterungs-Grundsätze*); for the analytic ones are indeed highly important and necessary, but only in order to attain that *distinctness* (*Deutlichkeit*) of concepts which is requisite for a secure and extended synthesis, as for a really new acquisition. The X is named: the *unknown* on which the understanding relies for synthetic a priori judgments. The thesis is that this X cannot be experience, because the principles in question carry strict necessity and strict universality, which experience cannot supply. Therefore some non-empirical X must be at work — and the identification of this X is the central task of the Critique. The architectonic claim is then made explicit: the entire final aim of speculative cognition a priori rests on synthetic *Erweiterungs-Grundsätze* — extension-principles. Analytic principles are necessary as preparation (they make our concepts *deutlich*) but they do not extend cognition; only synthetic principles do. The reorientation of metaphysical inquiry from the analytic to the synthetic, and from the conceptual to the conditions of synthesis, is the methodological revolution of the Critique.

Here, then, lies a certain *secret* (*Geheimnis*), Kant continues — and the \[A\] text adds: whose clarification alone can make secure and reliable the progress in the boundless field of pure cognition of understanding — namely, to uncover, with appropriate universality, the *ground of the possibility of synthetic judgments a priori*, to insight into the conditions which make each kind of them possible, and to determine this whole cognition (which constitutes its own genus) in a system, according to its original sources, divisions, extent, and limits — not by a fleeting circumscription but completely and sufficiently for every use. So much by way of preliminary on what is *peculiar* (*Eigentümliches*) to synthetic judgments. The \[A\] text's footnote here adds a striking polemical observation: had any of the ancients posed this question, the consideration would alone have powerfully resisted all systems of pure reason up to our time, and would have spared so many vain attempts, blindly undertaken without knowing what one was properly dealing with. The word *Geheimnis* (secret) is emphatic. The ground of the possibility of synthetic a priori judgments is the central mystery whose disclosure the entire Critique is to provide. Note the careful articulation: the task is *not merely* to demonstrate that such judgments exist (which section V will do by examples), but to *uncover the ground of their possibility* with *gehörige Allgemeinheit* (appropriate universality) — to see the conditions which make each kind possible, and to determine the whole in a system. The mention of *original sources, divisions, extent, and limits* (*Quellen, Abteilungen, Umfang und Grenzen*) is the architectonic vocabulary of the Critique. The \[A\]-edition footnote is among the sharpest historical judgments in the prefaces: had any ancient philosopher posed this question, all the systems of pure reason up to Kant's time would have been resisted by it. The vain attempts of dogmatic metaphysics, *blindlings unternommen* (blindly undertaken), arose precisely from ignorance of what they were *properly dealing with*. The Critical revolution begins by getting the question right.

### **V. In All Theoretical Sciences of Reason Synthetic a priori Judgments Are Contained as Principles**

*Mathematical judgments are altogether synthetic.* This proposition, Kant begins, seems to have escaped hitherto the observations of the analysts of human reason — indeed, to be directly opposed to all their conjectures — although it is incontrovertibly certain and in the sequel very important. For because one found that the inferences of the mathematicians all proceed according to the principle of contradiction (which the nature of every apodictic certainty requires), one persuaded oneself that the principles also were cognized from the principle of contradiction; in which one was deceived. For a synthetic proposition can indeed be seen according to the principle of contradiction, but only in such a way that *another synthetic proposition is presupposed*, from which it can be inferred; never, however, in itself. The thesis is announced in italics: *Mathematische Urteile sind insgesamt synthetisch*. The thesis runs contra the entire rationalist tradition (Wolff, Leibniz, and the school-metaphysics) which held mathematical judgments to be analytic — derivable from the principle of contradiction alone. The diagnosis of how this error arose is careful: the *inferences* of the mathematicians do proceed according to the principle of contradiction (deductive inference is everywhere governed by it), and the confusion was to suppose that the *first principles* (*Grundsätze*) from which the inferences proceed are also so derivable. They are not. A synthetic proposition can be derived from another synthetic proposition by the principle of contradiction (this is what happens within a mathematical deduction), but it cannot be derived *an sich selbst* — from itself, by mere conceptual analysis. The point is foundational: the whole edifice of mathematics rests on synthetic principles, and these principles cannot be derived from logic alone.

Before all, Kant continues, it must be remarked: that proper mathematical propositions are always judgments a priori and not empirical, because they carry necessity with them, which cannot be taken from experience. If one is not willing to grant this — well then, I will restrict my proposition to *pure mathematics*, whose concept already entails that it contains not empirical but only pure cognition a priori. The first point is preserved against any empiricist resistance: mathematical propositions are a priori (because they bear necessity), and if anyone disputes this, the thesis is restricted to *reine Mathematik*, whose very concept (as opposed to *applied* mathematics) requires that its cognition be pure a priori. The careful retreat to *reine Mathematik* is methodologically important: even if some uses of mathematics involve empirical content (as in applied mechanics), pure mathematics as such (arithmetic, geometry in their pure form) cannot but be a priori. The general thesis — that mathematics is a priori — is therefore secured at the very least for pure mathematics; and pure mathematics is what is at issue when the question is whether mathematical *Grundsätze* are analytic or synthetic.

One might initially think, Kant continues, that the proposition 7 \+ 5 \= 12 is a merely analytic proposition, which follows from the concept of a sum of seven and five according to the principle of contradiction. But if one considers it more closely, one finds that the concept of the sum of 7 and 5 contains nothing more than the *union* of the two numbers into a single one — by which it is not at all thought *which* this single number is that comprises the two. The concept of *twelve* is by no means already thought when I merely think that union of seven and five; and however long I may analyze my concept of such a possible sum, I will still not find the twelve in it. One must go *beyond* these concepts, taking *intuition* to one's aid — which corresponds to one of the two, say one's five fingers, or (as Segner does in his arithmetic) five points — and so successively add the units of the five given in intuition to the concept of seven. The famous example is now developed. The concept of *seven plus five* does not contain *twelve*. What it contains is the abstract idea of *uniting* two numbers into one, but the determinate *which* of the resulting number is not given by the analysis of the concept alone. To find that 7 \+ 5 \= 12, one must *go beyond* the concept — and the going-beyond is accomplished by intuition: by counting, fingers, points, or any other concrete intuitive presentation. The reference to *Segner* (Johann Andreas Segner, the eighteenth-century mathematician) is to a contemporary arithmetic textbook which used dots for this purpose. The thesis is that arithmetic, even at its most elementary, requires intuition for synthesis — concept alone is insufficient. The example is not chosen at random: numerical equations are the most plausible candidates for analytic status (one might think they are just identities expressed in different notation), and Kant must show that even these are synthetic.

For I take first the number 7, Kant continues, and, taking the fingers of my hand as intuition for the concept of 5, I add the units, which I previously gathered together to make the number 5, in this image of mine successively to the number 7, and so I see the number 12 *arise* (*entspringen*). That 5 was to be added to 7 — this I did indeed think in the concept of a sum \= 7 \+ 5; but not that this sum is equal to the number 12\. The arithmetical proposition is therefore always synthetic; which one becomes the more clearly aware of when one takes somewhat larger numbers, since it then becomes clear that — turn and twist our concepts as we will — without taking intuition to our aid, by the mere analysis of our concepts, we could never find the sum. The verb *entspringen* (to arise, to spring forth) is significant: the number 12 does not lie in the concept of *7 \+ 5*, but arises through the intuitive act of counting-on. The role of intuition (fingers, points, or any other concrete presentation) is to provide the *third term* X mentioned in section IV — the something outside the concept on which synthesis relies. The test of larger numbers is rhetorically effective: it is easy enough to imagine that one might just "see" 7+5=12 in the concept (because we know the answer well), but with 1739 \+ 6452 \= 8191, no amount of conceptual analysis will yield the answer; intuitive (algorithmic, calculation-based) procedures are required. Hence: arithmetic is synthetic.

Just as little, Kant continues, is any principle of pure geometry analytic. That *the straight line between two points is the shortest* is a synthetic proposition. For my concept of *straight* contains nothing of magnitude, but only a quality. The concept of *shortest* is therefore added entirely from outside, and cannot be drawn from the concept of a straight line by any analysis. Intuition must therefore be taken to one's aid here, by means of which alone the synthesis is possible. The same demonstration is now extended to geometry. The proposition *the straight line is the shortest between two points* is a paradigm of geometric truth. The analysis of the concept *straight*: what is in it? A quality (*Qualität*), not a magnitude (*Größe*). The concept of *shortest*, however, is a comparative magnitude — a quantitative concept. Quality and quantity are not derivable from each other; therefore the predicate *shortest* is not contained in the subject *straight* but is added from outside. Intuition — in this case, the pure spatial intuition that grounds geometry — is what makes this synthesis possible. The example is among the most famous in the Critique and was a frequent target of subsequent criticism (one can ask whether *straight* really contains nothing of magnitude, or whether modern definitions of straightness via geodesics make the proposition definitionally analytic). But Kant's claim is faithful to the Euclidean conception: straightness is a primitive geometric quality, and the connection with shortness of distance is a substantive geometric truth that requires intuitive presentation.

A few principles which the geometers presuppose, Kant continues, are indeed actually analytic and rest on the principle of contradiction; but they serve also only, like identical propositions, for the *chain of the method* (*zur Kette der Methode*) and not as *principles* — e.g., *a \= a*, *the whole is equal to itself*, or *(a \+ b) \> a*, *the whole is greater than its part*. And yet even these, although they hold by mere concepts, are admitted in mathematics only because they *can be presented in intuition*. What ordinarily makes us believe that the predicate of such apodictic judgments is already in our concept, and that the judgment is therefore analytic, is merely the *ambiguity of the expression*. We are supposed to add in thought a certain predicate to a given concept, and this necessity already attaches to the concepts. But the question is not what we are to *think* (*hinzudenken*) in addition to the given concept, but what we *actually think* in it, though only obscurely; and there it shows itself that the predicate does indeed inhere in those concepts necessarily, but not as thought in the concept itself, but rather by means of an intuition which must be added to the concept. The careful concession is made: *some* mathematical principles are analytic — namely, identities like *a \= a* and instances of the part-whole axiom like *(a \+ b) \> a*. But these analytic principles do not function as substantive *Prinzipien* of mathematics; they belong only to the *Kette der Methode*, the chain of the method — i.e., to the logical structure of inference. And even these analytic propositions, Kant adds, are admitted in mathematics *only because they can be presented in intuition*. This is a striking concession: even mathematical analytic propositions are intuitively grounded, not just conceptually. The diagnosis of the analytic-judgments illusion is then given with precision: the *Zweideutigkeit des Ausdrucks* (ambiguity of expression) confuses what we *should think* (the normative requirement of completing the concept) with what we *actually think* (the empirical content of our actual representation). The predicate necessarily attaches to the concept — yes — but not as part of the concept itself, but via an intuition that must be added. The point is methodologically decisive: the necessity of mathematical truths does not entail their analyticity; necessity can come from intuition just as well as from concepts.

*Natural science* (*Naturwissenschaft, Physica*) *contains synthetic judgments a priori in itself as principles.* I will only adduce a few propositions as examples, Kant continues, like the proposition: *that in all alterations of the corporeal world the quantity of matter remains unaltered*; or that, *in all communication of motion, action and reaction must always be equal to one another*. In both, not only the necessity, and hence their a priori origin, but also that they are synthetic propositions, is clear. For in the concept of matter I do not think *permanence* (*Beharrlichkeit*), but only its presence in space through filling it. I therefore actually go beyond the concept of matter, in order to add to it a priori in thought something that I did not think *in it*. The proposition is therefore not analytic but synthetic, and yet thought a priori; and so in the rest of the propositions of the pure part of natural science. The same thesis is now extended to physics. The two examples are the conservation of matter (*Beharrlichkeit der Materie*, the principle of the First Analogy in the Transcendental Analytic) and the equality of action and reaction (Newton's third law, the principle of the Third Analogy). Both are necessary (their *Notwendigkeit* shows that they are a priori) and both are synthetic (their content is not derivable from the concepts of matter or motion alone). The analysis of the concept of matter is given: matter is presence in space through filling space — but permanence is not part of this concept. To assert that matter is *permanent* (i.e., conserved through all alterations) is therefore to go beyond the concept synthetically. The example shows that the pure part of natural science (*reiner Teil der Naturwissenschaft*) — that is, the rational mechanics that grounds empirical physics — consists of synthetic a priori propositions. This positions the Critique as a foundational inquiry not only for metaphysics but for theoretical natural science itself.

*In metaphysics*, Kant continues, even if one regards it as only a so far merely attempted science, yet through the nature of human reason an *indispensable* one, *synthetic cognitions a priori shall be contained*. It is not at all the business of metaphysics merely to analyze concepts which we make for ourselves a priori of things, and thereby to elucidate them analytically; rather, we want to *extend* our cognition a priori, for which we must employ such principles as add something to the given concept which was not contained in it, and through synthetic judgments a priori go so far indeed that experience itself cannot follow us so far, e.g., in the proposition *the world must have a first beginning*, and others. And so metaphysics, at least *with respect to its end* (*ihrem Zwecke nach*), consists of nothing but synthetic propositions a priori. The third domain — metaphysics — is now treated. The carefully qualified description — *bisher bloß versuchte, dennoch aber durch die Natur der menschlichen Vernunft unentbehrliche Wissenschaft* (hitherto merely attempted, but yet through the nature of human reason an indispensable science) — captures the precarious position of metaphysics: it has not yet succeeded as a science, but the impulse to it is part of the nature of human reason. The proper aim of metaphysics is not analytic clarification but synthetic extension. The example *the world must have a first beginning* (one of the antinomic theses to be treated in the Dialectic) shows the kind of synthetic a priori judgment metaphysics aims at — judgments that go so far beyond experience that experience cannot follow. The qualifier *ihrem Zwecke nach* (with respect to its end) is significant: metaphysics consists of synthetic a priori propositions *insofar as it aims at extending cognition*. Whether this aim is achievable, and within what limits, is precisely what the Critique will determine. The section thus completes the survey of the three fields in which synthetic a priori judgments occur: mathematics, natural science, and metaphysics. The stage is set for the general formulation of the central problem.

### **VI. The General Problem of Pure Reason**

One already gains very much, Kant begins, when one can bring a quantity of investigations under the formula of a single problem. For thereby one not only lightens for oneself one's own business, in that one determines it exactly, but also for everyone else who wishes to test it the judgment whether we have done justice to our undertaking or not. The proper problem of pure reason is now contained in the question: *How are synthetic judgments a priori possible?* The methodological remark on formulation precedes the formulation itself. The reduction of a multitude of investigations to a single *Aufgabe* (problem, task) is a major methodological gain — both for the inquirer (who can now define the task precisely) and for any critic (who can judge whether the task has been accomplished). The formulation, when given, has the clarity of a single sentence: *Wie sind synthetische Urteile a priori möglich?* This question is the central question of the entire Critique. Everything else — the Transcendental Aesthetic, Analytic, Dialectic, and Methodenlehre — is structured around the answer to this question. The Critique is, in this sense, a single sustained argument addressed to a single question.

That metaphysics has hitherto remained in such a wavering state of uncertainty and contradictions, Kant continues, is to be ascribed solely to the cause that one did not let this problem, and perhaps even the distinction between analytic and synthetic judgments, come into one's thoughts earlier. On the solution of this problem, or on a satisfactory proof that the possibility which it demands to know explained does not in fact take place, depends now the *standing and falling* (*Stehen und Fallen*) of metaphysics. *David Hume*, who of all philosophers came closest to this problem — but did not think it nearly determinately enough nor in its universality, but stopped at the synthetic proposition of the connection of effect with its causes (the *principium causalitatis*) — believed himself to bring out that such a proposition a priori is wholly impossible; and according to his inferences, all that we call metaphysics would amount to a mere illusion of supposed insight of reason into what is in fact only borrowed from experience and has, through habit, acquired the *appearance* of necessity; an assertion destroying all pure philosophy, into which he would never have fallen if he had had our problem before his eyes in its universality — since he would then have seen that, according to his argument, there could also be no pure mathematics, because this certainly contains synthetic propositions a priori, against which assertion his good understanding would then have safeguarded him. The diagnosis of the history of metaphysics is precise: it has failed because it lacked the right question. The two preconditions of progress are (i) the analytic/synthetic distinction (section IV) and (ii) the question of synthetic a priori (section VI). Without these, no progress was possible. Hume, the closest predecessor, is now treated. Hume came nearest the central problem but missed it in two respects: he did not think it *determinately enough* (*bestimmt genug*) and not in its *universality* (*Allgemeinheit*). He restricted his attention to the principle of causality (*Principium causalitatis*) and concluded that this synthetic principle could not be a priori — hence reduced it to habit (*Gewohnheit*) and the *appearance* of necessity. This reduction destroys all pure philosophy. Had Hume seen the universality of the problem, he would have realized that his argument would also destroy pure mathematics (which is also synthetic a priori), and his *good understanding* (*guter Verstand* — a delicate compliment) would have prevented him from this. The implicit historical narrative is therefore: Hume saw the question but mislocated it; Kant has now located it correctly and can therefore answer it. The architectonic role of Hume in the Critique is established: he is the catalyst (*ihn vom dogmatischen Schlummer erwecken*, as the *Prolegomena* will say) whose partial insight provides the occasion for Kant's full insight.

In the solution of the above problem, Kant continues, is at the same time included the possibility of the pure use of reason in the grounding and execution of all sciences which contain a theoretical cognition a priori of objects — i.e., the answering of the questions: *How is pure mathematics possible? How is pure natural science possible?* Of these sciences, since they are actually given, it can well befittingly be asked *how* they are possible; for that they *must be* possible is proved through their actuality. The general problem is now decomposed into its specific instances. The central question — *how are synthetic judgments a priori possible?* — implies, when applied to specific fields, three further questions: how is pure mathematics possible? how is pure natural science possible? how is metaphysics possible? The first two of these can be asked in a relatively secure way, since the sciences in question are actually given (*wirklich gegeben*) — pure mathematics exists, pure natural science exists. The transcendental question is not whether they exist but *how* they exist — what makes their possibility intelligible. The methodological principle invoked here — *ab esse ad posse* (from being to possibility) — is the regressive method of the Critique: starting from the actuality of an a priori science, regress to the conditions that must hold for it to be possible. The footnote attached observes that someone might doubt the actuality of pure natural science; but it suffices to look at the various propositions at the beginning of empirical physics — conservation of matter, inertia, equality of action and reaction — to be convinced that they constitute a *physica pura* (or *rationalis*) which deserves to be set forth as a separate science.

But as regards *metaphysics*, Kant continues, its previous poor progress, and because one cannot say of any one hitherto put forward, with respect to its essential purpose, that it actually exists, must lead every one with reason to doubt its possibility. Yet this *kind of cognition* is in a certain sense also to be regarded as given, and metaphysics is — if not as science, yet as *natural disposition* (*Naturanlage*, *metaphysica naturalis*) — actual. For human reason proceeds unceasingly, without being moved by mere vanity of much-knowing, driven by its own need, on to such questions as cannot be answered by any experiential use of reason and principles borrowed therefrom; and so in all men, as soon as reason extends in them to speculation, there has at all times actually been some metaphysics, and there will always remain some. And now of this too the question arises: *How is metaphysics as natural disposition possible?* — i.e., how do the questions arise that pure reason poses to itself, and which it is driven by its own need to answer as best it can, out of the nature of universal human reason? The third specific question — about metaphysics — must be posed with greater care, because the alleged science is precarious. Metaphysics has had no agreed-upon successful execution; its *Wirklichkeit* (actuality) cannot be presupposed. But — and this is a crucial move — metaphysics is *actual* in another sense: as a *Naturanlage*, a *natural disposition* of human reason. The argument is that reason cannot but pose certain questions to itself (the unavoidable problems of God, freedom, immortality, named in section III); these questions arise *not* from vain curiosity but from *eigenes Bedürfnis* (reason's own need). The Latin phrase *metaphysica naturalis* preserves the schoolish terminology. The first question about metaphysics is therefore: how is it possible as a *natural disposition*? — i.e., what is the source in the structure of reason that drives it to pose these unanswerable questions? The Transcendental Dialectic will provide the answer: reason has its own transcendental Ideas (psychological, cosmological, theological) which arise from its own structure, and these Ideas, when illegitimately applied to objects, produce the dialectical illusions.

But since, in all hitherto undertaken attempts to answer these natural questions, Kant continues — e.g., whether the world has a beginning, or is from eternity, etc. — *unavoidable contradictions* have always been found, one cannot rest content with the mere natural disposition to metaphysics — i.e., with the pure faculty of reason itself, out of which some metaphysics always grows — but rather it must be possible to bring this to *certainty* (*Gewißheit*), whether in the *knowing* or *not-knowing* of the objects — i.e., either of the decision about the objects of its questions, or of the capacity or incapacity of reason to judge about them — therefore either to extend our pure reason with reliability, or to set determinate and secure limits to it. *This last question*, which flows from the above general problem, would rightfully be: *How is metaphysics as science possible?* The argument now turns from natural-disposition metaphysics to scientific metaphysics. The diagnosis of the dialectical antinomies — *unvermeidliche Widersprüche* (unavoidable contradictions) — is given: when reason attempts to answer its own self-posed questions (e.g., the cosmological antinomies: did the world have a beginning, is it limited in space, etc.), it falls into contradictions that cannot be resolved by dogmatic procedure. Therefore the natural disposition must be brought to *Gewißheit* — either by deciding the questions, or by setting limits to reason's competence. The methodological possibilities are then disjunctively given: *Erweiterung* (extension of cognition) or *Schranken setzen* (setting limits). The result of the disjunction is the question of metaphysics-as-science: *Wie ist Metaphysik als Wissenschaft möglich?* This is the fourth and final form of the central question, completing the architecture: how are synthetic judgments a priori possible? — and specifically: in mathematics? in natural science? as natural disposition? as science?

The critique of reason therefore necessarily leads at last to science, Kant continues; the dogmatic use of it without critique, on the contrary, to baseless assertions to which one can oppose equally apparent ones, hence to *skepticism*. Also, this science cannot be of any great frightening extensiveness, because it has to do not with objects of reason, whose manifoldness is infinite, but merely with itself, with problems that arise entirely out of its own bosom, and which are posed to it not through the nature of things which are distinct from it, but through its own nature — since, when it has first thoroughly come to know its own capacity in respect of objects which may come before it in experience, it must easily become possible to determine completely and securely the *extent* and the *limits* of its attempted use beyond all experiential limits. The methodological consequence: critique leads to science; dogmatism leads to skepticism. The polemic against dogmatic metaphysics is sharp: without critique, every dogmatic assertion has an equally specious counter-assertion (this is the structural source of the antinomies), and the result is *Skeptizismus* — the historical position of Hume and the Pyrrhonists. The promise of the Critique is then made: the science of critique need not be of frightening extent, because it does not deal with the inexhaustible objects of reason but only with reason itself, with its own self-generated problems. The internality of the inquiry — reason examining itself — is the source of its tractability. Reason's *Vorrat* (stock, supply) of resources is small enough to be fully surveyed.

One can therefore, and must, Kant continues, regard all hitherto made attempts to bring metaphysics about *dogmatically* as not done; for what is analytic in the one or the other — namely, mere analysis of the concepts that dwell in our reason a priori — is not yet the *purpose*, but only a *preparation* (*Veranstaltung*) for the proper metaphysics, namely synthetically to extend its cognition a priori; and is unsuited for this purpose, because it merely shows what is contained in these concepts, but not *how* we come to such concepts a priori, in order then also to determine their valid use with respect to the objects of all cognition in general. The dismissive judgment on previous metaphysics is now made: all dogmatic attempts at metaphysics are to be regarded *als ungeschehen* — as not having happened. The reason is that previous metaphysics has been *analytic* — it has analyzed concepts that reason a priori possesses, but has not asked the synthetic question. Analytic metaphysics is at most a *Veranstaltung* (preparation) for proper metaphysics, but it is not the goal itself. The strict requirement on the science of metaphysics is twofold: it must extend a priori cognition synthetically, and it must determine *how we come to* these concepts a priori (the question of their origin) so that we can also determine their valid use (the question of their objective application). Analytic metaphysics fails on both counts — it does not extend, and it does not ground.

Also, only little *self-denial* (*Selbstverleugnung*) is required to give up all these claims, Kant continues, since the undeniable contradictions of reason with itself (also unavoidable in the dogmatic procedure) have long since deprived every hitherto-existing metaphysics of its standing. More *steadfastness* will be necessary not to be deterred, by internal difficulty and external resistance, from finally promoting through another, wholly contrary to the previous, treatment a science indispensable to human reason — of which one can chop down every grown trunk, but not eradicate the root — to a flourishing and fruitful growth. The transitional remark notes that the rejection of dogmatic metaphysics requires little *Selbstverleugnung* — little self-denial, since dogmatic metaphysics has been long since discredited by its own internal contradictions. The greater virtue required is *Standhaftigkeit* (steadfastness): the resolve to pursue the new path against both internal difficulty and external opposition. The botanical metaphor — every trunk can be cut, but the root cannot be eradicated — preserves the deep claim that metaphysics is rooted in the natural disposition of reason and cannot be uprooted, only redirected. The promise of the new Critical metaphysics is *gedeihlicher und fruchtbarer Wuchs* — a flourishing and fruitful growth.

### **VII. Idea and Division of a Particular Science under the Name of a Critique of Pure Reason**

From all of this, Kant begins, the *idea* of a particular science now arises, which can be called *the Critique of Pure Reason*. For reason is the faculty which supplies the *principles* of cognition a priori. Hence pure reason is that which contains the principles to cognize something absolutely a priori. An *organon* of pure reason would be a totality (*Inbegriff*) of those principles according to which all pure cognitions a priori can be acquired and actually established. The exhaustive application of such an organon would procure a *system* of pure reason. The Critique is now defined. *Vernunft* (reason) is the faculty which supplies the *principles* of a priori cognition. *Reine Vernunft* is that branch of reason which contains the principles for knowing something *absolutely* (*schlechthin*) a priori. The technical vocabulary of the Critique's architecture is then laid out: an *Organon* would be the totality (*Inbegriff*) of those principles by which pure a priori cognitions are acquired; the application of such an Organon would yield a *System* of pure reason. The word *Inbegriff* (totality, comprehensive concept) is distinctive: it does not mean simply a collection but a unified totality that can be grasped under a single concept — the architectonic ideal of the Critique.

But since this is asking very much, Kant continues, and since it still stands open whether such an *extension* of our cognition is here possible at all, and in what cases — we can regard a science of the mere *appraisal* (*Beurteilung*) of pure reason, its sources and limits, as the *propaedeutic* to the system of pure reason. Such a science would have to be called not *doctrine*, but only *Critique of Pure Reason*, and its *use* would, with respect to speculation, be only *negative* — would serve not for the extension but only for the *purification* (*Läuterung*) of our reason, and to keep it free from errors; which is already very much gained. The Critique is now distinguished from the Organon. The Organon would extend cognition; the Critique only appraises (*beurteilt*) reason's capacity for such extension. The Critique is therefore preparatory (*Propädeutik*) to the system, not the system itself. It is *not Doktrin* (not doctrine) but *Kritik* — a verdict on the possibility and limits of doctrine. Its use is *only negative* (with respect to speculation): it does not extend cognition but rather *purifies* (*läutert*) reason by keeping it free from errors. The negative function — keeping reason free from error — is described as *schon sehr viel gewonnen* (already very much gained), echoing the formula from the \[B\] preface that to set bounds to error is also to set bounds to truth. The positive function of the Critique with respect to *practical* reason will be alluded to in the \[B\] preface's discussion of freedom, but the speculative use is, as such, only negative.

I call all cognition *transcendental*, Kant continues, which is occupied not so much with objects, but rather with our *manner of cognition of objects insofar as this is to be possible a priori*. A system of such concepts would be called *transcendental philosophy*. But this is again too much for the beginning. For because such a science would have to contain both analytic and synthetic cognition a priori completely, it is, as far as our intention is concerned, of too wide an extent — since we may carry the analysis only so far as it is indispensably necessary to insight into the principles of synthesis a priori, with which alone we are concerned, in their whole extent. This investigation, which we cannot properly call *doctrine*, but only *transcendental critique*, because it does not have as its purpose the extension of the cognitions themselves but only their *correction*, and is to provide the *touchstone* (*Probierstein*) of the worth or worthlessness of all cognitions a priori — is what we are now occupied with. The definition of *transzendental* is the central methodological definition of the Critique. In the \[A\] formulation, transcendental cognition is occupied with our *Begriffe a priori* of objects in general. In the \[B\] formulation (which is the one mostly used), it is occupied with our *Erkenntnisart* (manner of cognition) of objects insofar as this is to be possible a priori. The \[B\] formulation is more careful: it does not say that transcendental cognition concerns concepts but rather the *manner of cognition* — emphasizing that the inquiry is into the conditions of possible a priori cognition. A *system* of such transcendental concepts would be *Transzendental-Philosophie*. But the Critique is not this complete system, because the complete system would require both analytic and synthetic a priori cognition in full extent, while the Critique restricts itself to as much analysis as is necessary for insight into synthetic a priori principles. The Critique is therefore *transcendental critique*, not transcendental philosophy: it is the *Probierstein* (touchstone, recalling the same word from the \[A\] preface) of the worth of all a priori cognition, not the totality of such cognition itself.

Such a critique is therefore, Kant continues, a *preparation*, where possible, for an *Organon*; and if this should not succeed, at least for a *Canon* of pure reason — according to which, in any case, the complete system of the philosophy of pure reason, whether it consist in the extension or merely in the limitation of its cognition, could one day be presented both analytically and synthetically. For that this is possible — indeed, that such a system cannot be of too great extent, so that one may hope to complete it entirely — can be assessed in advance from the fact that here it is not the nature of *things* (which is inexhaustible) but the *understanding* which judges of the nature of things, and even this understanding only with respect to its cognition a priori, that constitutes the object — whose *stock* (*Vorrat*), since we may not seek it externally, cannot remain hidden from us, and is, according to all conjecture, small enough to be completely taken up, to be appraised according to its worth or unworth, and to be brought under correct estimation. The disjunction *Organon* / *Canon* is significant. An *Organon* would be a positive instrument for extending cognition; a *Canon* would be a set of rules for the legitimate use of cognition. The Critique aims at an Organon if possible, at least at a Canon if not. The Canon of Pure Reason will be the title of a key section of the Doctrine of Method, in which the practical (moral) use of reason will be developed as the canonical use. The methodological grounding of the Critique's tractability — its finite extent — is now made explicit: the object of investigation is not the inexhaustible nature of things, but the understanding's own a priori cognition; its *Vorrat* (stock, store) is therefore finite and surveyable. This grounds the architectonic ambition of the Critique: a complete system of finite extent.

Still less may one expect here a *critique of books and systems of pure reason*, Kant continues (in the \[B\] edition's addition), but rather a critique of the pure faculty of reason *itself*. Only when this lies at the ground does one have a secure touchstone for assessing the philosophical content of old and new works in this field; otherwise the unauthorized historian and judge appraises the baseless assertions of others through his own, which are just as baseless. The \[B\] edition adds this important methodological clarification. The Critique is not a polemical work against particular books or systems (it is not a critique *of Leibniz*, *of Wolff*, *of Locke*, *of Hume*); it is a critique of the *faculty of reason* itself. The methodological priority is foundational: without a prior critique of reason as such, any criticism of particular philosophical works is groundless (since it has no *Probierstein* to appraise them). The polemic against the *unbefugte Geschichtsschreiber und Richter* (unauthorized historian and judge) is targeted at the historians of philosophy who criticize systems without having undertaken the fundamental work of self-criticism.

*Transcendental philosophy* is the idea of a science, Kant continues, for which the Critique of Pure Reason is to draw up the entire *plan* (*Plan*) architectonically — i.e., from principles, with full guarantee of the completeness and security of all the parts that make up this building. It is the *system of all principles of pure reason*. That this Critique is not itself called transcendental philosophy rests solely on the fact that, in order to be a complete system, it would also have to contain a *thorough analysis of the whole human cognition a priori*. Now our Critique must indeed also lay before our eyes a complete enumeration of all the *stem-concepts* (*Stammbegriffe*) which constitute the said pure cognition. But it fittingly refrains from the thorough analysis of these concepts themselves, as well as from the complete review of those derived from them — partly because this analysis would not be purposive, since it does not bear the *concern* (*Bedenklichkeit*) which is met with in synthesis, for the sake of which the whole Critique exists; partly because it would be contrary to the unity of the plan to take on the responsibility for the completeness of such an analysis and derivation, of which one can well be relieved with respect to one's purpose. The distinction between Critique and Transcendental Philosophy is now precisely articulated. *Transzendental-Philosophie* is the complete system, of which the Critique provides only the *Plan*. The system would have to contain complete analytic and synthetic a priori cognition; the Critique restricts itself to a complete *enumeration* of the *Stammbegriffe* (stem-concepts, root-concepts — i.e., the categories) but does not engage in their thorough analysis or in the derivation of all subordinate concepts. The reason for this restriction is twofold: (i) analytic decomposition is not what generates *Bedenklichkeit* (concern, difficulty) — synthesis is what does, and it is for the sake of clarifying synthesis that the Critique exists; (ii) including a complete analysis would expand the Critique beyond its proper scope. The Critique is therefore the *complete idea* of Transcendental Philosophy without being Transcendental Philosophy itself.

This completeness of analysis as well as of derivation, Kant continues, is, however, easy to supplement if these only first exist as detailed principles of synthesis, and if nothing is lacking to them with respect to this essential purpose. To the Critique of Pure Reason therefore belongs everything that constitutes transcendental philosophy, and it is the complete *idea* of transcendental philosophy, but not yet this science itself, because it goes in analysis only so far as is necessary for the complete appraisal of synthetic cognition a priori. The architectonic relationship is now clarified: once the Critique has succeeded in establishing the principles of synthesis, the analytic supplementation can be added as a matter of normal extension. The Critique is therefore prior in order of inquiry to Transcendental Philosophy proper, even though it is methodologically restricted in extent.

The principal point of attention in the division of such a science, Kant continues, is: that no concepts at all must come in which contain anything empirical in themselves; or that the a priori cognition be wholly *pure* (*rein*). Hence, although the highest principles of morality and the fundamental concepts of it are cognitions a priori, they yet do not belong to transcendental philosophy, because they — although they do not put the concepts of pleasure and displeasure, of desires and inclinations, etc., which are altogether of empirical origin, at the ground of their prescriptions — must yet necessarily draw these along into the construction of the system of pure morality as *obstacle* to be overcome, or as *incentive* (*Anreiz*) which should not be made into the motive (*Bewegungsgrund*). Hence transcendental philosophy is a *worldly wisdom* (*Weltweisheit*) of *pure merely speculative reason*. For everything practical, insofar as it contains *motives* (*Triebfedern*), refers to feelings, which belong to empirical sources of cognition. The criterion of *purity* is now made central. Transcendental philosophy admits only concepts which contain nothing empirical. By this criterion, even moral philosophy — though its highest principles are a priori — does not belong to transcendental philosophy, because the construction of the system of morality must necessarily reference concepts of pleasure, desire, inclination (as obstacles to be overcome or incentives that are *not* to be made motives), and these are empirical. Transcendental philosophy is therefore the *Weltweisheit der reinen bloß spekulativen Vernunft* — the worldly wisdom of pure, *merely speculative* reason. The phrase *bloß spekulativ* (merely speculative) is restrictive: the Critique addresses pure reason in its theoretical, not practical, use. This sets up the parallel work in the second Critique (the *Critique of Practical Reason*), which will address pure practical reason. The argument that *Triebfedern* (motives) refer to feelings, and feelings are empirical, is the basis for excluding the practical from transcendental philosophy. The footnote at the \[A\] preface marked this distinction differently — there, *Bewegungsgründe* (motives) referred to feelings — but the \[B\]-edition has refined the vocabulary.

If one now wishes to undertake the division of this science from the general standpoint of a system, Kant continues, then the one we now present must contain, first, a *Doctrine of Elements* (*Elementar-Lehre*), and second, a *Doctrine of Method* (*Methoden-Lehre*) of pure reason. Each of these chief parts would have its sub-division, the grounds of which cannot yet be presented here. Only this much seems necessary to the introduction or preliminary reminder: that there are *two stems* of human cognition, which perhaps spring from a *common but to us unknown root*, namely *sensibility* and *understanding* (*Sinnlichkeit und Verstand*) — through the former of which objects are *given* to us, through the latter, however, they are *thought*. Insofar as sensibility should contain a priori representations which constitute the *condition* under which objects are given to us, it would belong to transcendental philosophy. The *transcendental doctrine of sense* (*transzendentale Sinnenlehre*) would have to belong to the *first* part of the elementary science, because the conditions under which alone the objects of human cognition are given precede those under which the same are thought. The architectonic division of the work is now announced. The Critique has two principal parts: *Elementar-Lehre* (Doctrine of Elements) and *Methoden-Lehre* (Doctrine of Method). Each will have further subdivisions whose grounds cannot yet be presented. The proleptic distinction is then made: there are *zwei Stämme* (two stems, trunks) of human cognition — *Sinnlichkeit* (sensibility) and *Verstand* (understanding). The famous phrase *aus einer gemeinschaftlichen, aber uns unbekannten Wurzel* (from a common but to us unknown root) suggests that these two stems may have a deeper unity, but that this unity is *uns unbekannt* (unknown to us). This phrase has generated immense interpretive controversy — Heidegger, in *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics*, will famously interpret the common root as the *transcendental imagination*; the German Idealists (Fichte, Schelling, Hegel) will all attempt their own unification of the two stems. Kant himself remains agnostic. The functional distinction is then made: sensibility *gives* objects (through receptivity); understanding *thinks* them (through spontaneity). The conditions of givenness precede the conditions of thinking, because nothing can be thought of which has not first been given. Therefore the *transzendentale Sinnenlehre* (transcendental doctrine of sense) — the Transcendental Aesthetic — must come first in the order of exposition, before the doctrine of understanding (the Transcendental Analytic). This sets up the architectonic that follows.

## **Introduction \[Edition A\]**

### **II. Division of Transcendental Philosophy**

Transcendental philosophy, Kant begins, is here only an *idea*, for which the Critique of Pure Reason is to draw up the whole *plan* architectonically — i.e., from principles — with full guarantee of the completeness and security of all the parts that make up this building. That this Critique is not itself called transcendental philosophy rests solely on the fact that, in order to be a complete system, it would also have to contain a *thorough analysis* of the whole of human cognition a priori. The \[A\]-edition's section II repeats, with slight stylistic variations, much of the content of the \[B\] edition's section VII. The opening characterization of transcendental philosophy as an *Idee* (idea) — for which the Critique provides the *Plan* — is the central architectonic concept. The Critique is to be drawn up *architektonisch* (architectonically), *aus Prinzipien* (from principles), with full guarantee of *Vollständigkeit* (completeness) and *Sicherheit* (security) of all parts. The metaphor of a *Gebäude* (building) made of *Stücke* (pieces) returns once more. The reason the Critique is not yet transcendental philosophy is the same: it does not contain the *ausführliche Analysis* (thorough analysis) of all human a priori cognition.

Now our Critique, Kant continues, must indeed also lay before our eyes a complete *enumeration* (*Herzählung*) of all the *stem-concepts* (*Stammbegriffe*) which constitute the said pure cognition. But it fittingly refrains from the thorough analysis of these concepts themselves, as well as from the complete review of those derived from them — partly because this analysis would not be purposive, since it does not bear the concern which is met with in synthesis, for the sake of which the whole Critique exists; partly because it would be contrary to the unity of the plan to take on the responsibility for the completeness of such an analysis and derivation, of which one can well be relieved with respect to one's purpose. The methodological restriction of the Critique is the same as in \[B\]: it enumerates the *Stammbegriffe* (which will turn out to be the twelve categories of the Metaphysical Deduction) but does not undertake their complete analysis. The justification is twofold: complete analysis is not *zweckmäßig* (purposive) because the concern that drives the Critique is not analytic decomposition but synthetic ground-laying; and complete analysis would burden the Critique with a responsibility — the *Verantwortung der Vollständigkeit* — that is not its proper task. The careful distinction between Critique and Transcendental Philosophy is therefore preserved across both editions, with the Critique conceived as the *complete idea* of Transcendental Philosophy without being identical to it.

This completeness of analysis as well as of derivation, Kant continues, from the concepts a priori to be delivered in the future, is, however, easy to supplement once they are first there as detailed principles of synthesis, and nothing is lacking to them with respect to this essential purpose. To the Critique of Pure Reason, then, belongs everything that constitutes transcendental philosophy, and it is the *complete idea* of transcendental philosophy, but not yet this science itself, because it goes in analysis only so far as is necessary for the complete appraisal of synthetic cognition a priori. The repetition of this point in the \[A\] edition has the same significance as in \[B\]: the Critique is the complete idea of Transcendental Philosophy because once the principles of synthesis are established, the analytic supplementation is straightforward. The Critique is the foundation and plan of the science; the science itself can be erected on it afterwards.

The principal point of attention in the division of such a science, Kant continues, is that absolutely no concepts must come in which contain anything empirical in themselves, or that the a priori cognition be entirely pure. Hence, although the highest principles of morality and the fundamental concepts of it are cognitions a priori, they yet do not belong to transcendental philosophy, because the concepts of pleasure and displeasure, of the desires and inclinations, of the will (*Willkür*) etc., which are altogether of empirical origin, would have to be presupposed in them. Hence transcendental philosophy is a *worldly wisdom* of pure merely speculative reason. For everything practical, insofar as it contains *grounds of motivation* (*Bewegungsgründe*), refers to feelings, which belong to empirical sources of cognition. The \[A\] edition's formulation differs slightly from \[B\] in vocabulary — \[A\] says *Willkür* (will, in the sense of free choice) where \[B\] does not, and \[A\] uses *Bewegungsgründe* where \[B\] uses *Triebfedern* (motives, incentives). The substance is the same: morality is a priori in its highest principles but practical philosophy as a whole involves empirical concepts (pleasure, desire, will), and therefore cannot belong to transcendental philosophy in the strict sense. The \[B\] edition's refinement — which retains the role of feelings as obstacles to be overcome or as incentives not to be made motives — is somewhat more sophisticated than \[A\]'s simpler claim that empirical concepts would have to be *vorausgesetzt* (presupposed). But the architectural conclusion is identical: transcendental philosophy is the worldly wisdom of *pure, merely speculative* reason.

If one now wishes to undertake the division of this science from the general standpoint of a system, Kant continues, then the one we now present must contain, first, a *Doctrine of Elements*, and second, a *Doctrine of Method* of pure reason. Each of these chief parts would have its sub-division, the grounds of which cannot yet be presented here. Only this much seems necessary to the introduction or preliminary reminder: that there are *two stems of human cognition* which perhaps spring from a common but to us unknown root, namely *sensibility* and *understanding* — through the first of which objects are given to us, but through the second are thought. Insofar as sensibility should contain a priori representations which constitute the conditions under which objects are given to us, it would belong to transcendental philosophy. The *transcendental doctrine of sense* would have to belong to the *first* part of the elementary science, because the conditions under which alone the objects of human cognition are given precede those under which the same are thought. The closing paragraph of the \[A\]-edition introduction is identical in substance to its \[B\]-edition counterpart and announces the same architectonic division. *Elementar-Lehre* (Doctrine of Elements) and *Methoden-Lehre* (Doctrine of Method) are the two chief parts; the Doctrine of Elements has its further subdivisions which will only be presented when the order of exposition requires it. The proleptic announcement of the two stems — *Sinnlichkeit* and *Verstand*, sensibility and understanding — and of their *gemeinschaftliche, aber uns unbekannte Wurzel* (common but to us unknown root) is preserved. The architectonic priority of sensibility over understanding in the order of exposition (since objects must first be *given* before they can be *thought*) is the justification for placing the Transcendental Aesthetic before the Transcendental Analytic in the body of the work. With this, the introductory apparatus of the Critique is complete, and the body of the work — the Transcendental Doctrine of Elements, beginning with the Transcendental Aesthetic — can commence.

## **Transcendental Doctrine of Elements**

The work proper opens with this announcement, given at A17/B31 as a divisional heading. The *Transzendentale Elementarlehre* (Transcendental Doctrine of Elements) is the first of the two principal parts of the Critique announced in section VII of the Introduction; the *Transzendentale Methodenlehre* (Doctrine of Method) will be the second. The Elements are those a priori conditions under which alone cognition is possible; the Method will treat of the discipline, canon, architectonic, and history of pure reason. The Elements have their own internal articulation: a *First Part* (the Transcendental Aesthetic, on sensibility) and a *Second Part* (the Transcendental Logic, on understanding and reason). The architectonic priority of sensibility — that objects must first be *given* before they can be *thought* — determines that the Aesthetic precedes the Logic. The numbering "I. Transzendentale Elementarlehre" at the head is one of the rare divisional headings in the work; the absence of an introductory paragraph here is appropriate, since the Introduction has already explained what the Elementarlehre is and why it has the structure it has.

## **First Part: The Transcendental Aesthetic**

### **§ 1**

In whatever way and through whatever means, Kant opens, a cognition may relate to objects, that through which it relates *immediately* to them, and at which all thinking aims as a means, is *intuition* (*Anschauung*). This, however, takes place only insofar as the object is *given* to us; but this in turn — at least for us human beings — is possible only by way of the object affecting the mind in some manner. The capacity (*receptivity*) to receive representations through the manner in which we are affected by objects is called *sensibility* (*Sinnlichkeit*). By means of sensibility, then, objects are *given* to us, and it alone provides us with *intuitions*; through the understanding, however, they are *thought*, and from it arise *concepts*. All thinking must, however, whether directly (*directe*) or indirectly (*indirecte*), by means of certain marks, ultimately relate to intuitions, hence — for us — to sensibility, because in no other way can an object be given to us. The opening paragraph of the Aesthetic does the foundational architectonic work: it distinguishes the two stems of cognition (sensibility/understanding) by their mode of relation to objects (immediate/mediate) and by their characteristic products (intuitions/concepts). *Anschauung* is defined functionally: that through which cognition relates *immediately* (*unmittelbar*) to its object. All other cognition (concepts, judgments, inferences) relates only mediately, by way of intuitions. The qualifier *uns Menschen wenigstens* (at least for us human beings) is critical: Kant does not deny that there might be other kinds of intelligence (a divine, intuitive understanding, an *intuitus originarius*) for which objects are not given but constituted by their being thought; he restricts his claim to *our* finite, sensible, receptive cognition. The Handexemplar addition — *if the representation is not itself in itself the cause of the object* — makes this restriction explicit. The crucial concepts of *Rezeptivität* and *Affizieren* are introduced: sensibility is a *Vermögen* (capacity) characterized by passivity, by being-affected (*affiziert werden*) rather than producing. The understanding by contrast is *Spontaneität* — a capacity for self-activity, generating its own products (concepts). The numbering (§ 1\) is a B-edition addition, part of the more systematic structure of the second edition.

The effect of an object upon the faculty of representation, Kant continues, so far as we are affected by it, is *sensation* (*Empfindung*). That intuition which relates to the object through sensation is called *empirical*. The undetermined object of an empirical intuition is called *appearance* (*Erscheinung*). The vocabulary of the Aesthetic is now precisely established. *Empfindung* names the subjective effect of an object upon receptivity; it is the basic content of empirical consciousness. *Empirische Anschauung* is intuition mediated by sensation. *Erscheinung* — appearance — is the *undetermined object* (*unbestimmter Gegenstand*) of empirical intuition. The qualifier "undetermined" is crucial: appearance is not yet the *determined* object that will emerge once the categories have been applied; at this stage of the inquiry (the Aesthetic) we are still considering objects only insofar as they are given to sensibility, not yet thought by understanding. The vocabulary marks out a precise descending sequence: things in themselves → affection of sensibility → sensation → empirical intuition → (undetermined) appearance. The distinction between *Erscheinung* and *Phänomen* (which will appear later as the determined empirical object) is already implicit here.

In the appearance, Kant continues, I call that which corresponds to sensation its *matter* (*Materie*); but that which makes it possible that the manifold of the appearance can be ordered in certain relations (*in gewissen Verhältnissen geordnet werden kann*), I call the *form* (*Form*) of the appearance. Since that in which alone sensations can be ordered and placed in a certain form cannot itself be sensation in turn, the matter of all appearance is given to us only a posteriori; but its *form* must lie ready for them all a priori in the mind, and therefore must be able to be considered apart from all sensation. The matter/form distinction structures the entire Aesthetic. *Materie* is what is *given* in sensation — the qualitative content of empirical consciousness (colors, sounds, hardness, etc.). *Form* is the *ordering* (*Ordnung*, *Verhältnis*) by which this material is structured into a coherent appearance. The argument for the a priori status of form is methodologically decisive: that which orders sensations cannot itself be a sensation, on pain of regress (an ordering principle which is just one further item to be ordered would never finish the work of ordering). Therefore form must be a priori — present in the mind in advance, ready to receive whatever empirical content may come. The phrase *im Gemüte a priori bereit liegen* (ready a priori in the mind) anticipates the famous result of the Aesthetic: that space and time are precisely such forms, lying ready in advance of any empirical encounter. The criterion of *abgesondert von aller Empfindung* — separable in consideration from all sensation — gives the methodological key to the Aesthetic: by abstracting from all empirical content, we isolate the pure forms.

I call all representations *pure* (in the transcendental sense), Kant continues, in which nothing belonging to sensation is encountered. Accordingly, the *pure form* of sensible intuitions in general will be encountered in the mind a priori, wherein all the manifold of appearances is intuited in certain relations. This pure form of sensibility will itself also be called *pure intuition* (*reine Anschauung*). So, if I separate from the representation of a body that which the understanding thinks in it — such as substance, force, divisibility, etc. — as well as that which belongs to sensation in it — such as impenetrability, hardness, color, etc. — then there still remains over for me from this empirical intuition something, namely *extension* and *figure* (*Ausdehnung und Gestalt*). These belong to pure intuition, which a priori — even without an actual object of the senses or of sensation — takes place in the mind as a mere form of sensibility. The Aesthetic now demonstrates its method by example. The method is one of progressive abstraction: from the representation of a body, separate (i) what the *understanding* thinks (concepts like substance, force, divisibility — these will be the categories) and (ii) what belongs to *sensation* (the qualia-like properties of impenetrability, hardness, color); what remains is *Ausdehnung und Gestalt* — extension and figure. These remainders cannot be removed without destroying the spatial form of the representation itself; therefore they belong to *pure intuition*. The reference to *substance, force, divisibility* as belonging to the understanding is proleptic — these are categories that will only be properly justified in the Analytic. The reference to *impenetrability, hardness, color* as belonging to sensation marks them as a posteriori. The remainders — *Ausdehnung und Gestalt* — are the spatial features of the body, and they belong to pure intuition because they are what the spatial form of sensibility a priori contributes to any empirical representation of body.

A science of all principles of sensibility a priori, Kant continues, I call the *transcendental aesthetic*. There must therefore be such a science, which constitutes the first part of the transcendental doctrine of elements, in contrast to that which contains the principles of pure thinking and is called *transcendental logic*. The footnote attached defends the use of the term *Ästhetik* against Baumgarten's appropriation of it for the critique of taste. The Germans, Kant notes, are the only ones who now use the word *Ästhetik* to designate what others call critique of taste. There lies at the ground here a failed hope that the excellent analyst Baumgarten entertained — to bring the critical appraisal of the beautiful under principles of reason and to raise its rules to the status of a science. But this endeavor is vain; for such rules, or criteria, are merely empirical in their principal sources, and can therefore never serve as determinate a priori laws by which our judgment of taste must be guided — rather, the latter constitutes the proper *touchstone* (*Probierstein*) of the correctness of the former. For this reason, it is advisable either to drop this designation again and to reserve it for the doctrine that is a true science (whereby one would also come closer to the language and sense of the ancients, with whom the division of cognition into αἰσθητὰ καὶ νοητὰ was very famous), or else to share the designation with speculative philosophy and to take aesthetics partly in the transcendental sense, partly in psychological meaning. The footnote performs the philological retrieval. Baumgarten — *vortrefflicher Analyst*, an excellent analyst, but with this failed hope — had given *Aesthetica* to the science of taste; Kant insists that taste cannot be a science (its rules are empirical) and reclaims *Ästhetik* for transcendental philosophy in the etymologically faithful sense: αἴσθησις, sensible perception. The reference to the Greek division between *aisthēta* and *noēta* (sensibles and intelligibles) gives the deep historical authority for Kant's usage. The third Critique will, in the end, partly relent on this terminology — the *Critique of the Power of Judgment* will treat aesthetic judgment under the name *Ästhetik* despite Kant's protest here.

In transcendental aesthetics, accordingly, Kant continues, we shall first *isolate* (*isolieren*) sensibility, by separating off everything that the understanding thinks in connection with it through its concepts, so that nothing remains but empirical intuition. Secondly, we shall further separate from the latter everything that belongs to sensation, so that nothing remains but pure intuition and the mere form of appearances, which is the only thing that sensibility can deliver a priori. In this investigation it will be found that there are two pure forms of sensible intuition as principles of cognition a priori, namely *space* and *time*, with whose consideration we shall now occupy ourselves. The methodological procedure of the Aesthetic is now made explicit. The two-step isolation: first separate off the conceptual contribution of understanding (abstract from substance, force, etc.); then separate off the sensational content (abstract from color, hardness, etc.). What remains after this double abstraction is *reine Anschauung* — pure intuition — and this is *die einzige* (the only thing) that sensibility can deliver a priori. The result is then announced in advance: there are *two* pure forms of sensible intuition — *Raum* (space) and *Zeit* (time). These are the principles of a priori cognition that the Aesthetic will identify and analyze. The order of treatment will be space first (outer sense), then time (inner sense) — an order whose rationale is partly architectonic (space yields the more public, mathematically tractable structure) and partly thematic (time will reveal itself as the more universal form, applying to all representations including those of outer sense).

### **First Section: On Space**

#### **§ 2\. Metaphysical Exposition of This Concept**

By means of *outer sense* (*äußerer Sinn*), Kant begins — a property of our mind — we represent objects as *outside us*, and these all together in space. Therein their shape, magnitude, and relation to one another is determined, or determinable. The *inner sense* (*innerer Sinn*), by means of which the mind intuits itself or its inner state, gives no intuition of the soul itself as an object; yet it is still a determinate form under which alone the intuition of its inner state is possible, so that everything that belongs to the inner determinations is represented in relations of time. Outwardly, time cannot be intuited, any more than space can be intuited as something in us. What then are space and time? Are they actual entities? Are they only determinations, or also relations of things, but such as would belong to them in themselves even if they were not intuited; or are they such as adhere only to the form of intuition, hence to the subjective constitution of our mind, without which these predicates could not be attributed to any thing at all? The opening establishes the inner/outer sense distinction. *Äußerer Sinn* presents objects *outside us* in space; *innerer Sinn* presents the mind's own inner states in time. The asymmetry between them is noted: outer sense gives a manifold (objects with shape, magnitude, relation); inner sense does not give *the soul itself as object* but only the *form* under which its inner states appear. The four possibilities for what space and time *are* are then disjunctively listed: (i) actual entities (the Newtonian view, absolute space and time as real beings); (ii) determinations or relations of things in themselves, but such as would belong to them even unintuited (a Leibnizian variant on the relational view); (iii) determinations adhering only to the form of intuition, to the subjective constitution of the mind (Kant's own view, the transcendental ideality of space and time). The disjunction frames the entire argument of the Aesthetic, which will establish (iii) by elimination.

To instruct ourselves about this, let us first *expound* the concept of space, Kant continues. By *exposition* (*expositio*) I understand the *distinct* (though not exhaustive) representation of what belongs to a concept; the exposition is *metaphysical*, however, when it contains what presents the concept *as given a priori*. The B-edition addition makes the methodological framework precise. *Erörterung* (exposition) is the distinct (*deutlich*) representation of what belongs to a concept — distinct, not exhaustive (i.e., what is essential to it, not every conceivable predicate). *Metaphysisch* the exposition becomes when its content is the concept's *a priori givenness*. This is set in opposition to *transzendentale Erörterung* (which §3 will undertake): transcendental exposition explains the concept *as a principle from which the possibility of other synthetic a priori cognitions can be seen*. Metaphysical exposition is therefore the analysis of the concept itself; transcendental exposition is the regressive demonstration of its conditions-of-possibility role.

1. Space is no empirical concept that has been abstracted from outer experiences. For in order that certain sensations be related to something *outside me* — i.e., to something in another place of space than that in which I find myself — and in order that I be able to represent them as *outside and next to one another*, hence not merely as different but as in *different places*, the representation of space must already lie at the ground. Accordingly, the representation of space cannot be borrowed through experience from the relations of outer appearance, but rather this outer experience is itself only possible at all by means of the said representation. The first argument of the Metaphysical Exposition of space is the argument from the conditions of the representation of objects *outside me*. To represent objects as outside me (i.e., as in spatial relations to my own location) presupposes the representation of space; therefore space cannot be abstracted from such representations but is rather their precondition. The argument has the form of a regressive transcendental argument: from the *factum* of outer experience, regress to its conditions; space is one of these conditions; therefore space is a priori. The phrase *außer und neben einander* (outside and next to one another) is a B-edition addition that sharpens the point: spatial juxtaposition is presupposed by any representation of distinct objects, not derivable from it.

2. Space is a *necessary representation a priori* that lies at the ground of all outer intuitions. One can never form a representation of there being no space, although one can well think that no objects are encountered in it. It is regarded therefore as the condition of the possibility of appearances, and not as a determination dependent on them, and is a representation a priori that necessarily lies at the ground of outer appearances. The second argument is the argument from the *unrepresentability of the absence of space*. We can think of space as empty (a void), but we cannot think away space itself; therefore space is a necessary representation. The asymmetry — empty space yes, no-space no — is the diagnostic mark of a priori necessity. The argument turns on the modal status of space: it is not contingent (some specific spatial arrangement) but necessary (space as such). The A-edition's variant text, preserved in the editorial apparatus, makes the same point through the inference from apodictic certainty of geometric principles back to the a priori character of the underlying representation. The B-edition replaces this with the more economical argument from imaginability.

3. Space is no *discursive* — or, as one says, *general* — concept of relations of things in general, but a *pure intuition* (*reine Anschauung*). For one can represent to oneself only a *single* space, and when one speaks of *many* spaces, one understands by this only *parts* of one and the same single space. Nor can these parts precede the single all-encompassing space as its components (from which a composition would be possible), but rather can only be thought *in* it. It is essentially single, the manifold in it — hence also the general concept of spaces in general — rests merely upon limitations. From this it follows that with regard to it an *a priori intuition* (which is not empirical) lies at the ground of all concepts of it. Thus also all geometric principles — e.g., that in a triangle two sides together are greater than the third — are derived never from general concepts of line and triangle, but from intuition, and indeed a priori, with apodictic certainty. The third argument is the *uniqueness* argument: space is essentially *singular* (one space), and the many spaces we speak of are parts of this single space. This is the contrary of how concepts behave: under a general concept (say, *triangle*) fall many individuals (*this triangle*, *that triangle*), and the concept stands above them as a higher genus. Space is not like this. The single space is prior to its parts: the parts are limitations *within* the single space, not its constituents. This is the formal mark of an intuition, not a concept. A general concept *contains under itself* (*unter sich enthalten*) many individuals; an intuition *contains in itself* (*in sich enthalten*) its parts. Space behaves like the latter: every part of space is in space, not under space. Therefore space is intuition, not concept — and since the representation is a priori, it is *pure* intuition. The example of the triangle inequality — that two sides exceed the third — illustrates: this is not derived from general concepts (the concepts of line and triangle do not contain this proposition analytically) but from intuition.

4. Space is represented as an *infinite given magnitude*. Now one must think every concept indeed as a representation that is contained in an infinite quantity of different possible representations (as their common mark), hence contains these *under itself*; but no concept, as such, can be thought as if it contained an infinite quantity of representations *in itself*. Nevertheless space is so thought (for all parts of space, to infinity, are simultaneous). Therefore the original representation of space is *intuition a priori*, and *not concept*. The fourth argument is the *infinitude* argument: space is given as an infinite magnitude, and a concept cannot be so given. The structure is: a concept contains *under* itself many possible individuals (a concept *animal* contains under it all individual animals), and this containment can in principle be infinite (the concept *number* contains all individual numbers, infinitely many). But this is containment *under* itself, not *in* itself: a concept does not *contain in* itself infinitely many representations as parts. Space, however, is given as containing in itself infinitely many parts (all parts of space are *simultaneously* given — *zugleich* — in the single space). This containment-in-itself of infinitely many parts is the formal mark of intuition, not of concept. Therefore the original representation of space is *Anschauung a priori* — pure intuition. The A-edition's variant gives a slightly different formulation centered on the geometric distinction between a foot and an ell, both subsumed under the general concept *space*; the B-edition's version is more rigorous, focused on the part-whole relation.

#### **§ 3\. Transcendental Exposition of the Concept of Space**

By a *transcendental exposition* I understand, Kant explains, the explanation of a concept as a principle from which the possibility of other synthetic a priori cognitions can be seen. To this purpose it is required: 1\) that such cognitions actually flow from the given concept; 2\) that these cognitions are possible only under the presupposition of a given mode of explanation of this concept. The B-edition adds this §3 to make explicit the methodological role of the transcendental exposition. The distinction from metaphysical exposition is now sharp: where metaphysical exposition analyzes what belongs to the concept *as given a priori*, transcendental exposition shows that the concept is the *principle* of further synthetic a priori cognitions. Two conditions: (i) such further cognitions must really flow from the concept; (ii) they must be possible only under this concept's mode of explanation. The procedure is regressive — from the *factum* of synthetic a priori cognition (here, geometry), regress to the conditions that make it possible.

Geometry, Kant continues, is a science that determines the properties of space synthetically and yet a priori. What then must the representation of space be, for such a cognition of it to be possible? It must originally be *intuition*; for from a mere concept no propositions can be drawn that go beyond the concept, which yet happens in geometry (Introduction V). But this intuition must be found in us a priori — i.e., prior to any perception of an object — hence be *pure*, not empirical intuition. For geometric propositions are altogether *apodictic* — i.e., bound up with the consciousness of their necessity — e.g., *space has only three dimensions*; but such propositions cannot be empirical or experiential judgments, nor be inferred from such (Introduction II). The transcendental exposition is here carried out. Geometry is the *factum* — it exists, it produces synthetic a priori cognition. What must space be in order to make this possible? Two conditions: (a) the representation of space must be *intuition*, because synthetic (extension-yielding) propositions can be drawn only when one goes beyond a concept, and going-beyond is intuitive; (b) the intuition must be *a priori*, *pure*, because geometric propositions are apodictic (necessarily true, with no possible exception), and necessity cannot come from experience. The example — *space has only three dimensions* — illustrates the apodicticity. The cross-references to Introduction V (synthetic a priori judgments require intuition) and II (apodicticity is the mark of a priori) tie the transcendental exposition to the general framework laid down in the Introduction.

How can there now dwell in the mind an outer intuition that precedes the objects themselves, and in which the concept of the latter can be determined a priori? Obviously not otherwise than insofar as it has its seat *merely in the subject*, as the formal constitution of the subject of being affected by objects and thereby of acquiring an immediate representation of them, i.e., intuition, hence only as the *form of outer sense in general*. The crux of the argument is now stated. The only way an outer intuition can precede its objects is by being not a feature of the objects but a feature of the subject — specifically, the *formal constitution* (*formale Beschaffenheit*) of the subject's capacity to be affected. If space were a feature of objects, the representation of it would have to come *after* the affection by them; but the representation of space must come *before* (since it is the precondition for representing the objects as outside us). Therefore space is the *form of outer sense* — the a priori formal structure of the subject's receptive capacity.

Hence our explanation alone makes comprehensible the possibility of geometry as a synthetic a priori cognition, Kant concludes. Any mode of explanation that does not deliver this, even if it should bear some similarity in appearance with our own, can most securely be distinguished from it by these marks. The transcendental exposition therefore serves a double function. It demonstrates the truth of the transcendental ideality of space (since only this explanation makes geometry possible), and it provides a *Probierstein* — touchstone — for distinguishing the Kantian view from rival views (Newtonian absolute space, Leibnizian relational space). Any view that fails to ground synthetic a priori geometry is excluded.

#### **Conclusions from the Above Concepts**

a) Space represents no property whatsoever of any things in themselves, nor any of them in their relation to one another — i.e., no determination of them that adheres to objects themselves, and that would remain if one were to abstract from all subjective conditions of intuition. For neither absolute nor relative determinations can be intuited prior to the existence of the things to which they belong, hence not a priori. The first conclusion negates Newton (absolute determinations) and Leibniz (relative determinations) in a single stroke. Both views hold space (or its relational substrate) to be a determination of things in themselves; both views are excluded because *no* determination of things in themselves can be intuited a priori. The argument is regressive: a priori intuition is possible only if its content is not features of things in themselves but features of the subject; therefore the content of a priori intuition cannot be such determinations. This is the *Gegenprobe* (counter-test) prepared in the B preface.

b) Space is nothing other than the form of all appearances of outer sense, i.e., the *subjective condition of sensibility* under which alone outer intuition is possible for us. The second conclusion is positive: space *is* the form of outer sense, the subjective condition of sensibility. This is *transcendental ideality* in its positive formulation: space has its origin in the subject, not in objects. The conjunction of the negative conclusion (a) and the positive conclusion (b) gives Kant's full doctrine: space is *empirically real* (it pertains universally to all objects of outer experience) and *transcendentally ideal* (it is a feature of the subject's mode of receptivity, not of things in themselves).

Now, since the receptivity of the subject — to be affected by objects — necessarily precedes all intuitions of these objects, Kant continues, it can be understood how the form of all appearances can be given in the mind prior to all actual perceptions, hence a priori; and how, as a pure intuition in which all objects must be determined, it can contain principles of their relations prior to all experience. The constructive argument is given: receptivity must precede affection, and therefore the form of receptivity must be a priori. Once this is granted, the puzzle of how a priori intuition is possible dissolves: pure intuition is just the formal structure of receptivity itself, present in advance of any empirical content. Geometry's possibility is thereby explained: geometric propositions are about the formal structure of outer intuition, which is the subjective form of receptivity, hence available to a priori analysis.

We can therefore speak of space and of extended beings, etc., only from the standpoint of a human being, Kant continues. If we depart from the subjective condition under which alone we can acquire outer intuition — namely, the manner in which we may be affected by objects — then the representation of space signifies nothing at all. This predicate is attributed to things only insofar as they appear to us — i.e., are objects of sensibility. The permanent form of this receptivity, which we call sensibility, is a necessary condition of all relations in which objects are intuited as outside us; and when one abstracts from these objects, it is a pure intuition that bears the name space. Because we cannot make the particular conditions of sensibility into conditions of the possibility of *things* but only of their *appearances*, we can well say that space comprises all things that may appear to us externally, but not all things in themselves, however they may be intuited or by whatever subject. For we cannot judge at all of the intuitions of other thinking beings, whether they may be bound to the same conditions which restrict our intuition and are universally valid for us. The crucial restriction of perspective is now made explicit. The validity of the doctrine of space is restricted to the *standpoint of a human being* (*Standpunkt eines Menschen*). The Handexemplar addition notes the puzzle why Mendelssohn could assert this so apodictically *while still giving space objective reality* — a sharp diagnostic remark indicating that Kant's distinctive position is the conjunction of subjective ground and objective validity. The argument for why space pertains to all things that *appear to us* but not to all *things in themselves* is the central distinction of the Aesthetic. The judgment-form refinement is made: a judgment becomes unconditional if the restricting condition is built into the subject concept. *All things are next to one another in space* — restricted to *as objects of our sensible intuition*. *All things, as outer appearances, are next to one another in space* — unrestricted, universally valid a priori.

Our expositions accordingly teach the *reality* (i.e., objective validity) of space with respect to everything that can come before us externally as object, but at the same time the *ideality* of space with respect to things when they are considered by reason in themselves — i.e., without taking account of the constitution of our sensibility. We assert therefore the *empirical reality* of space (with respect to all possible outer experience), although also the *transcendental ideality* of the same — i.e., that it is nothing as soon as we leave aside the condition of the possibility of all experience and assume it as something underlying things in themselves. The double doctrine is now formulated in its canonical form: *empirische Realität* \+ *transzendentale Idealität*. The doctrine has two aspects which must be held together. Space is *empirically real* — i.e., universally and objectively valid for all possible outer experience. This protects the objectivity of physics and geometry against subjectivistic reduction. Space is *transcendentally ideal* — i.e., it does not pertain to things in themselves. This protects against the dogmatic claim that we have a priori knowledge of things in themselves through space.

But there is also outside of space no other subjective representation related to something *outer* that could be called objective a priori. Kant continues that one cannot derive synthetic a priori propositions from any of them, as one can from the intuition in space (§ 3). Therefore, strictly speaking, no *ideality* (*Idealität* — note the editorial correction from *Realität* — Akademie reading) belongs to them, although they agree with the representation of space in that they belong merely to the subjective constitution of the manner of sense — e.g., of sight, hearing, feeling — through the sensations of colors, tones, and warmth, which, since they are mere sensations and not intuitions, in themselves let no object be cognized, least of all a priori. The B-edition revision sharpens what the A-edition had only loosely connected. The point is to distinguish space (a *form of intuition*, a priori, universally valid, ground of synthetic a priori cognition) from sensible qualities (colors, tones, warmth — *sensations*, a posteriori, varying among subjects, not ground of a priori cognition). Both belong to the subjective constitution of sensibility, but they belong differently: space is the *Form* of receptivity, colors and tones are *Empfindungen* within receptivity. The methodological note explains why one cannot illustrate the ideality of space by analogy to colors: the latter are not a priori representations but a posteriori sensations.

The intent of this remark, Kant continues, is only to prevent one from undertaking to illustrate the alleged ideality of space by entirely inadequate examples, since colors, taste, etc., are with right considered not as constitutions of things but merely as alterations of our subject — alterations which may even differ among different human beings. For in this case that which originally is itself only appearance — e.g., a rose — is taken in the empirical understanding for a thing in itself, which can nevertheless appear different to every eye with respect to color. By contrast, the transcendental concept of appearances in space is a *critical reminder* that nothing intuited in space is a thing in itself, nor is space a form of things that might belong to them in themselves; rather, that objects in themselves are not known to us at all, and that what we call outer objects are nothing other than mere representations of our sensibility, whose form is space, but whose true correlate — i.e., the thing in itself — is thereby not cognized at all, nor can be, after which moreover in experience nothing is ever inquired. The two levels of distinction are now precisely articulated. At the *empirical* level, one distinguishes the rose (which has its color as a function of empirical conditions, including the perceiver's visual system) from the color as appearance. This is an empirical distinction within the sphere of empirical objects. At the *transcendental* level, one further distinguishes the rose (as empirical object, an appearance) from the *thing in itself* (whatever it may be that underlies the appearance). This is a critical-philosophical distinction which neither colors nor any other secondary qualities can illustrate, because they are empirical phenomena and not transcendental forms. The phrase *wahres Correlatum* (true correlate) for the thing in itself is significant — it preserves the realist insight that there is *something* corresponding to our appearances, while denying that we have any cognitive access to it.

### **Second Section: On Time**

#### **§ 4\. Metaphysical Exposition of the Concept of Time**

Time, Kant begins, 1\) is no empirical concept that has been abstracted from any experience. For *simultaneity* (*Zugleichsein*) or *succession* (*Aufeinanderfolge*) would themselves not enter into perception if the representation of time did not lie at the ground a priori. Only under its presupposition can one represent: that something be at one and the same time (simultaneously) or in different times (successively). The argument is parallel to the first space argument: the very representation of temporal relations (simultaneity, succession) presupposes time, so time cannot be derived from experience but is its precondition.

2. Time is a necessary representation that lies at the ground of all intuitions. With respect to appearances in general, one cannot annul time itself, although one can quite well take the appearances away from time. Time is therefore given a priori. In it alone is all actuality of appearances possible. The latter can all disappear, but time itself (as the universal condition of their possibility) cannot be annulled. The argument from unrepresentability-of-absence is now made for time. We can think away all events in time, but not time itself. The asymmetry parallels the case of space: empty space yes, no-space no; empty time yes, no-time no. Time is therefore necessary, hence a priori.

3. Upon this a priori necessity is also grounded the possibility of apodictic principles concerning the relations of time, or of *axioms of time in general*. It has only *one* dimension: different times are not simultaneous but successive (just as different spaces are not successive but simultaneous). These principles cannot be drawn from experience, for experience would give neither strict universality nor apodictic certainty. We could only say: so common perception teaches; but not: so must it be. These principles hold as rules under which experience in general is possible, and they instruct us *prior to* experience, not *through* it. The argument from apodictic principles is now made for time. Time has one dimension — a structural axiom. Times are successive, not simultaneous (the contrary holds of space). These axioms have strict universality and apodictic certainty; experience cannot supply this; therefore the axioms must be grounded in the a priori structure of time itself. The phrase *vor derselben, und nicht durch dieselbe* (prior to experience, not through it) captures the transcendental relation: the axioms of time are conditions of experience, not products of it.

4. Time is no discursive — or, as one calls it, *general* — concept, but a pure form of sensible intuition. Different times are only *parts* of one and the same time. The representation that can be given only through a single object, however, is *intuition*. Also, the proposition that different times cannot be simultaneous cannot be derived from a general concept. The proposition is synthetic and cannot arise from concepts alone. It is therefore *immediately* contained in the intuition and representation of time. The uniqueness argument is now made for time: there is one time, and the many times are parts of it (not individuals subsumed under a higher concept). The criterion of *gegeben durch einen einzigen Gegenstand* (given through a single object) is the structural mark of intuition. The proposition *different times cannot be simultaneous* is synthetic (not derivable by concept analysis) and a priori (apodictic), and is therefore *unmittelbar enthalten* (immediately contained) in the intuition of time.

5. The infinitude of time signifies nothing more than that every determinate magnitude of time is possible only through *limitations* of a single time lying at the ground. Therefore the original representation *time* must be given as unlimited. But where the parts themselves, and every magnitude of an object, can be determinately represented only through limitation, there the whole representation must not be given through concepts (for concepts contain only partial representations) but rather an immediate intuition must lie at the ground. The infinitude argument is now made for time: every determinate time is a limitation within a single, unlimited time. As with space, this is the formal mark of intuition (not concept), since limitation as a way of generating parts requires the prior given whole. The B-edition refines the formulation; the A-edition's form was slightly less precise. The argument concludes the Metaphysical Exposition of time: time is a pure a priori intuition, parallel to space.

#### **§ 5\. Transcendental Exposition of the Concept of Time**

I can therefore refer back to no. 3, Kant says, where for brevity I have placed what is properly transcendental under the article of the metaphysical exposition. Here I will add only that the concept of *alteration* (*Veränderung*) and, with it, the concept of *motion* (*Bewegung*, as alteration of place) is possible only through and in the representation of time: that, if this representation were not (inner) intuition a priori, no concept, whichever it may be, could make comprehensible the possibility of an alteration — i.e., of a combination of contradictorily opposed predicates (e.g., the being at a place and the not-being of the same thing at the same place) in one and the same object. Only in time can both contradictorily opposed determinations be encountered in one thing, namely *successively*. Our concept of time therefore explains the possibility of as much synthetic a priori cognition as the general doctrine of motion exhibits — which is no small fertility. The B-edition adds §5 as a separate transcendental exposition for time. The transcendental exposition uses *Veränderung* and *Bewegung* as the *factum* to be explained. Alteration involves the conjoining of contradictorily opposed predicates (a thing is here, then not here) — a conjunction that would be flatly contradictory if the predicates were taken simultaneously, but is intelligible if taken successively. Only time allows for *Sukzession*; therefore alteration presupposes time. And the general *Bewegungslehre* (doctrine of motion) is a body of synthetic a priori cognition whose possibility is grounded in time's being a pure intuition. The proleptic note: the question of how contradictorily opposed predicates can coexist successively in one thing will be the central question of the First Analogy and of the chapter on phenomena and noumena.

#### **§ 6\. Conclusions from These Concepts**

a) Time is not something that subsists for itself, or attaches to things as an objective determination, hence would remain over if one were to abstract from all subjective conditions of the intuition of it: for in the first case it would be something that would be actual without an actual object. As regards the second case, it could not, as a determination or order attaching to things themselves, *precede the objects* as their condition and be cognized and intuited a priori by synthetic propositions. This latter, on the contrary, takes place very well if time is nothing but the subjective condition under which all intuitions can take place in us. For then this form of inner intuition can be represented prior to the objects, hence a priori. The disjunction here mirrors the one for space. (i) Time is a self-subsistent entity (Newtonian absolute time, an *Unding* — non-thing — that exists without there being anything else); rejected because nothing can be actual without an actual object. (ii) Time is an objective determination or order attaching to things in themselves; rejected because such determinations cannot be intuited a priori. (iii) Time is the subjective form of intuition; this alone makes synthetic a priori cognition of time possible.

b) Time is nothing other than the form of *inner sense* — i.e., of the intuition of ourselves and of our inner state. For time can be no determination of outer appearances; it belongs neither to a shape nor a position, etc.; it on the contrary determines the relation of representations in our inner state. And, just because this inner intuition gives no shape, we seek to make up for this lack by analogies, and represent the temporal sequence through a line extending into infinity, in which the manifold constitutes a series that is of only one dimension; and we infer from the properties of this line to all properties of time, except the one thing that the parts of the former are simultaneous, those of the latter however always successive. From this it is also evident that the representation of time itself is *intuition*, since all its relations can be expressed in an outer intuition. Time is identified as the form of *innerer Sinn* — inner sense, the mind's intuition of itself. The asymmetry between outer and inner intuition is noted: outer intuition has the rich structural feature of *Gestalt* (shape), inner intuition does not — time has only the linear structure of succession. To compensate, we represent time spatially, by a line. This spatial representation of time is illuminating about the structural properties of time (one dimension, etc.) but cannot capture the distinguishing feature: the parts of a line are simultaneous, the parts of time are successive. The methodological point: the very fact that we use spatial representation to grasp temporal structure shows that time, like space, is an intuition (not a concept) — because the structural analogies are presented in outer intuition (a line) just as time itself is presented in inner intuition.

c) Time is the formal a priori condition of all appearances in general. Space, as the pure form of all outer intuition, is as a priori condition limited merely to outer appearances. By contrast, since all representations — whether or not they have outer things as their object — yet in themselves, as determinations of the mind, belong to the inner state; and since this inner state stands under the formal condition of inner intuition, hence of time: time is an a priori condition of *all appearance in general*, indeed the *immediate* condition of inner appearances (of our soul) and thereby *mediately* also of outer appearances. If I can say a priori: all outer appearances are in space and are determined a priori according to the relations of space — then from the principle of inner sense I can quite universally say: all appearances in general — i.e., all objects of the senses — are *in time* and stand necessarily in relations of time. The architectonic place of time is now made explicit. Space is the form of outer intuition only; time is the form of *all* appearance. The reason is that all representations whatsoever — including those of outer things — are themselves *Bestimmungen des Gemüts* (determinations of the mind), hence inner states, hence under the form of inner sense, which is time. Time is the *immediate* condition of inner appearances and the *mediate* condition of outer appearances (which are first inner representations, only then representing outer things). This generality of time — its applying to everything, not just to outer things — will be crucial when, in the Analytic, time becomes the medium in which the categories acquire their schematic application to all objects of experience.

If we abstract from our manner of intuiting ourselves inwardly, Kant continues, and from comprehending by means of this intuition also all outer intuitions in the faculty of representation, and hence take objects as they may be in themselves, then time is *nothing*. It is of objective validity only with respect to *appearances*, because these are already things that we take as *objects of our senses*; but it is no longer objective when one abstracts from the sensibility of our intuition, hence from that manner of representation peculiar to us, and speaks of *things in general* (*Dinge überhaupt*). Time is therefore solely a subjective condition of our (human) intuition — which is always sensible, i.e., insofar as we are affected by objects — and in itself, outside the subject, is nothing. Nevertheless, with respect to all appearances, hence to all things that can come before us in experience, it is necessarily objective. The structure of the doctrine is now parallel to that for space. *Empirical reality* of time (with respect to all objects of experience) \+ *transcendental ideality* of time (it is nothing outside the conditions of experience). The argument that *Time is nothing* outside its function as form of intuition is sharper for time than for space, because of time's deeper grasp into the structure of the subject: time is the form under which the subject appears to itself, hence its denial as feature of things-in-themselves is the denial of the subject as it is in itself. The implications for the doctrine of the self will be drawn out in the Paralogisms and the Refutation of Idealism.

We cannot say *all things are in time*, Kant continues, because in the concept of things in general one abstracts from every manner of intuiting them, which manner is however the proper condition under which time belongs in the representation of objects. If the condition is added to the concept, and it reads: *all things, as appearances (objects of sensible intuition), are in time*, then the principle has its good objective correctness and universality a priori. The judgment-form refinement parallel to that made for space is repeated: the qualified judgment *all things, as appearances, are in time* has universal a priori validity; the unqualified judgment *all things are in time* fails because *things in general* includes also things in themselves, which are not in time.

Our assertions therefore teach *empirical reality* of time — i.e., objective validity with respect to all objects that may ever be given to our senses. And since our intuition is always sensible, no object can ever be given to us in experience that does not stand under the condition of time. By contrast, we contest all claim of time to *absolute reality*, by which it would attach to things absolutely, without reference to the form of our sensible intuition, as a condition or property. Such properties belonging to things in themselves can never be given to us through the senses. *Therein consists therefore the* transcendental ideality of time, according to which it is nothing at all if one abstracts from the subjective conditions of sensible intuition, and can be reckoned to things in themselves (apart from their relation to our intuition) neither as subsisting nor inhering. Yet this ideality, like that of space, is not to be compared with the subreptions of sensation, because one yet presupposes, in the case of the appearance itself to which these predicates inhere, that it has objective reality, which here entirely falls away, except insofar as it is merely empirical, i.e., regards the object itself merely as appearance: on which the above remark of the first section is to be consulted. The doctrine is now stated in its full canonical form: empirical reality \+ transcendental ideality of time, with the warning (as before with space) not to confuse this transcendental claim with the empirical-psychological reduction of secondary qualities (the *Subreptionen der Empfindung*).

#### **§ 7\. Elucidation**

Against this theory, which concedes empirical reality to time but contests the absolute and transcendental, Kant continues, I have heard from insightful men an objection so unanimously that I infer from it that it must naturally occur to every reader to whom these considerations are unfamiliar. It runs thus: *Alterations are actual* (this is proved by the change of our own representations, even if one were to deny all outer appearances along with their alterations). Now alterations are possible only in time, consequently time is something actual. The answer has no difficulty. I grant the whole argument. Time is indeed something actual — namely, the actual form of inner intuition. It has therefore subjective reality with respect to inner experience — i.e., I really have the representation of time and of my determinations in it. It is therefore really to be regarded not as object but as the manner of representation of myself as object. If, however, I myself, or another being, could intuit me without this condition of sensibility, then these very same determinations which we now represent to ourselves as alterations would yield a cognition in which the representation of time, and hence of alteration, would not occur at all. Its empirical reality therefore remains as condition of all our experiences. Only absolute reality cannot be granted to it, according to the above. It is nothing but the form of our inner intuition. The footnote: *I can indeed say: my representations follow one another; but this only means that we are conscious of them as in a temporal sequence, i.e., according to the form of inner sense. Time is not therefore something in itself, nor any determination objectively attaching to things.* The objection imagined here is the natural reply: alterations are real; alterations require time; so time is real. Kant grants the premises but rejects the inference's conclusion as overstated. *Time is real* — yes, as the form of inner intuition; this is its *subjective Realität*. But *time is real in itself, as a feature of things-in-themselves* — no, this would be *absolute Realität* which Kant denies. The careful disambiguation of *Realität* recovers the doctrine: empirical reality, transcendental ideality, against absolute reality.

The cause, however, why this objection is so unanimously made — and indeed by those who can yet bring forward nothing illuminating against the doctrine of the ideality of space — is this. They hoped not to be able to prove apodictically the absolute reality of space, because idealism stood in their way, according to which the actuality of outer objects is not capable of any strict proof. By contrast, the actuality of the object of our inner senses (of myself and my state) is *immediately clear through consciousness*. The former could be a mere illusion (*Schein*); the latter, in their opinion, is undeniably something actual. They did not consider, however, that both — without our being permitted to contest their actuality as representations — yet belong only to appearance, which always has two sides: the one, that the object is considered in itself (apart from the manner of intuiting it, whose constitution however remains for that very reason always problematic); the other, that the form of the intuition of this object is considered — which is not to be sought in the object in itself but in the subject to which it appears, but yet really and necessarily attaches to the appearance of this object. The asymmetry between space and time in popular philosophical sentiment is now diagnosed. Outer reality is felt to be precarious (idealist worries about the bridge from inner to outer); inner reality is felt to be immediate and indubitable (the Cartesian *cogito*). Hence ideality of space is more easily granted than ideality of time. But the diagnosis is that both belong to appearance, and both have two sides: the object considered apart from the manner of intuition (the *transcendental object*), and the form of intuition (located in the subject). This dual-aspect analysis is the structural insight of transcendental idealism — appearance always has these two sides, neither reducible to the other.

Time and space are accordingly two sources of cognition from which a priori various synthetic cognitions can be drawn — as pure mathematics with regard to cognitions of space and its relations gives a brilliant example. Both, namely, taken together, are pure forms of all sensible intuition, and therefore make synthetic a priori propositions possible. But these a priori sources of cognition determine themselves through this very condition (that they are merely conditions of sensibility) — namely, their limits: that they relate only to objects insofar as these are considered as appearances, but do not present things in themselves. The latter alone are the field of their validity; once one goes beyond this, no further objective use of them takes place. This reality of space and time leaves moreover the security of experiential knowledge untouched: for we are equally certain of it, whether these forms necessarily attach to things in themselves or only to our intuition of these things. By contrast, those who assert the absolute reality of space and time — whether they assume it as subsistent or only as inherent — must be at odds with the principles of experience themselves. The architectonic balance of the Aesthetic is now stated. Space and time are *Erkenntnisquellen* (sources of cognition) for synthetic a priori cognition (paradigmatically, pure mathematics). At the same time, they are sources only within a limited field — the field of appearances — and not beyond. The reality of space and time as forms of intuition does not threaten empirical knowledge (since they apply universally to all appearances), but it forecloses any extension of mathematical cognition to things in themselves.

For if they decide for the first — which is generally the party of the mathematical investigators of nature — Kant continues, then they must assume two eternal and infinite self-subsisting non-things (space and time) that exist (without however anything actual existing) only in order to comprise all that is actual within themselves. The Newtonian view of absolute space and time is now characterized polemically as positing two *Undinge* (non-things) — eternal, infinite, self-subsistent, yet not actual things — which "exist" only in order to contain everything else. The internal incoherence of this position is highlighted by the parenthesis: things exist *ohne daß doch etwas Wirkliches ist* — without there actually being anything. If they take the second party (of which some metaphysical natural philosophers are) — and space and time count for them as relations of appearances (next to or after one another) abstracted from experience, though confusedly represented in the abstraction — then they must contest the validity, or at least the apodictic certainty, of the mathematical doctrines a priori with respect to actual things (e.g., in space); since this certainty does not take place a posteriori at all, and the a priori concepts of space and time, on this opinion, are only creatures of the imagination, whose source must really be sought in experience, from whose abstracted relations the imagination has made something that contains the general of these but cannot occur without the restrictions which nature has connected with them. The Leibnizian view of relational space and time is now characterized: it can preserve appearance-relations but at the cost of the apodicticity of mathematics. If space and time are abstractions from experience, then mathematical truths are at best inductive generalizations, lacking the strict universality and apodictic certainty they actually possess.

The first gain so much that they make the field of appearances free for mathematical assertions, Kant continues. By contrast, they become very entangled by these same conditions when the understanding wishes to go beyond this field. The second gain in regard to the latter — namely, that the representations of space and time do not come in their way when they wish to judge of objects not as appearances but merely in relation to the understanding; but they can give no ground for the possibility of mathematical cognitions a priori (since they lack a true and objectively valid intuition a priori), nor bring the propositions of experience into necessary agreement with those assertions. In our theory, of the true constitution of these two original forms of sensibility, both difficulties are remedied. The diagnostic survey is now made. The Newtonian (absolutist) view secures mathematics but stumbles on metaphysics (the *Undinge* are an unintelligible doubling). The Leibnizian (relationalist) view permits metaphysics but loses the apodictic certainty of mathematics. Kant's transcendental ideality of space and time secures both: mathematics is grounded in the pure intuitive forms of sensibility, and the limits of these forms (their applying only to appearances) leaves room for non-spatiotemporal metaphysics.

That, in conclusion, the transcendental aesthetic cannot contain more than these two elements — namely, space and time — is clear from this: that all other concepts belonging to sensibility, even that of motion which unites both, presuppose something empirical. For motion presupposes the perception of something movable. In space, however, considered in itself, there is nothing movable: hence the movable must be something that is found in space only through experience, hence an empirical datum. Likewise, the transcendental aesthetic cannot include the concept of *alteration* among its a priori data, for time itself does not alter, but rather something that is in time. To that, then, the perception of some existence and the succession of its determinations is required, hence experience. The completeness argument is the final move of the Aesthetic. Why are space and time the *only* a priori forms of sensibility? Because every other candidate (motion, alteration, etc.) presupposes something empirical: motion presupposes a movable, alteration presupposes a determinable existence. Hence only the pure forms — space and time, the structural conditions of receptivity itself — can be a priori, and they are exhausting.

#### **§ 8\. General Remarks on the Transcendental Aesthetic**

I. First, it will be necessary to explain as distinctly as possible what our opinion is with regard to the fundamental constitution of sensible cognition in general, in order to forestall all misinterpretation of it. The §8 General Remarks (added in B) function as a clarification of the doctrine of the Aesthetic against various possible misreadings. They are Kant's response to the criticisms the A-edition Aesthetic had received in the years between editions.

We have therefore meant to say: that all our intuition is nothing but the representation of *appearance*; that the things we intuit are not in themselves that for which we intuit them, nor are their relations in themselves so constituted as they appear to us; and that, if we abolish our subject — or even only the subjective constitution of the senses in general — then all the constitution, all the relations of objects in space and time — indeed, space and time themselves — would disappear, and as appearances they cannot exist in themselves but only in us. What it may be that has to do with the objects in themselves and apart from all this receptivity of our sensibility remains entirely unknown to us. We know nothing but our manner of perceiving them, which is peculiar to us, which also must not necessarily belong to every being, although it must to every human being. With this alone do we have to do. Space and time are the *pure forms* of it; sensation in general the *matter*. The former we can cognize a priori, i.e., prior to all actual perception, and they are therefore called *pure intuition*; the latter, however, is that in our cognition which makes it cognition a posteriori — i.e., empirical intuition. The former adhere absolutely necessarily to our sensibility, whatever kind our sensations may be; the latter can be very different. The opening summary states the doctrine with maximum clarity: all intuition is representation of appearance; things in themselves are unknown; space, time, and the relations among them, would disappear if the subject were abolished. Space and time are pure forms; sensation is matter. The qualification *was den Menschen anhangt, nicht notwendig jedem Wesen* (peculiar to us human beings, not necessary to every being) reaffirms the restriction of the doctrine to the standpoint of finite human cognition.

If we could bring this intuition of ours even to the highest degree of distinctness, we would not thereby come any closer to the constitution of the objects in themselves. For we would in any case still cognize only our manner of intuition — i.e., our sensibility — completely, and this still only under the conditions originally attached to the subject, of space and time; what the objects may be in themselves would never become known to us through the most enlightened cognition of their appearance, which alone is given to us. The Leibnizian assumption — that sensibility is just confused understanding, so that with sufficient clarification we could break through to the things in themselves — is now refuted. Distinctness (*Deutlichkeit*) is an intra-empirical improvement; it does not transcend the conditions of empirical knowledge. The form of intuition (space and time) is a feature of the subject, not of objects; making our representations of objects more distinct does not change what they are *of*. The doctrine of the Aesthetic therefore implies that no amount of empirical perfection breaks the limit.

Therefore, the \[view\] that our whole sensibility is nothing but the confused representation of things — which contains merely what belongs to them in themselves, but only under an aggregation of marks and partial representations that we do not consciously analyze — is a *distortion* of the concept of sensibility and of appearance which makes the whole doctrine of them useless and empty. The difference between an indistinct and a distinct representation is merely logical and does not affect the content. Without doubt the concept of *right*, used by the sound understanding, contains the very same as the subtlest speculation can develop out of it, only that in common and practical use one is not conscious of these manifold representations in this thought. Therefore one cannot say that the common concept is *sensible* and contains a mere appearance; for right cannot appear at all — rather, its concept lies in the understanding, and represents a constitution (the moral) of actions which belongs to them in themselves. The Leibnizian conflation is now diagnosed and rejected. *Indistinct/distinct* is a logical distinction (about the structure of representation), not a transcendental distinction (about its source). The concept of *Recht* (right, justice) — a moral concept — is grasped indistinctly by common sense and distinctly by the philosophical analyst, but in both cases it remains a concept of the understanding, not a sensible representation. The mode of obscurity does not turn an intellectual content into a sensible one. By contrast, the representation of a body in intuition contains nothing whatsoever that could belong to an object in itself, but merely the appearance of something and the manner in which we are affected by it; and this receptivity of our faculty of cognition is called sensibility, and remains worlds apart from the cognition of the object in itself — even if one were to penetrate to the bottom of the appearance. The body in intuition is a body-as-appearance, and the appearance is constitutively a function of receptivity, not (more or less clearly) a feature of the thing-in-itself.

The Leibniz-Wolff philosophy has therefore assigned an entirely false standpoint to all investigations into the nature and origin of our cognitions, by regarding the distinction between sensibility and the intellectual as merely *logical* — whereas it is clearly *transcendental*; and concerns not merely the form of distinctness or indistinctness, but the *origin and content* of them; so that through the former we cognize the constitution of things in themselves not merely indistinctly but not at all, and as soon as we take away our subjective constitution, the represented object with the properties that sensible intuition attributed to it is nowhere to be encountered or capable of being encountered, since precisely this subjective constitution determines its form as appearance. The historical critique is now made explicit. The Leibniz-Wolff school took the sensibility/understanding distinction to be a merely logical-grade distinction (sensibility \= confused understanding, understanding \= distinct sensibility). Kant's transcendental distinction is fundamentally different: sensibility and understanding differ in their origin (receptivity vs. spontaneity) and in their content (intuitions vs. concepts), not in their degree of clarity. The transcendental distinction is what makes intelligible the limits of cognition (sensibility's limitation to appearances) and at the same time the possibility of synthetic a priori cognition.

We otherwise well distinguish among appearances that which essentially attaches to the intuition of them — and is valid for every human sense in general — from that which attaches to them only contingently — in that it is not valid for the relation of sensibility in general, but only for a particular position or organization of this or that sense. And here one calls the first cognition such as presents the *object in itself*, the second only its *appearance*. This distinction, however, is only *empirical*. If one remains with it (as one commonly does), and does not in turn regard that empirical intuition (as one should) as mere appearance, so that nothing in it concerns any *thing in itself*, then our transcendental distinction is lost, and we believe to cognize things in themselves, although we everywhere (in the sense-world) have to do with nothing but appearances, even down to the deepest investigation of their objects. So we will indeed call the rainbow a mere appearance in a rain-shower, but this rain the *thing in itself* — which is also correct, insofar as we understand the latter concept only physically, as that which, in universal experience, under all the different positions of the senses, is yet determined in intuition thus and not otherwise. The two-tier distinction is now made crystal clear. The *empirical* distinction is the ordinary, common-sense distinction between subjective phenomena (rainbow as appearance) and objective things (rain as object). This is a within-the-empirical distinction. The *transcendental* distinction is between the entire empirical realm (both rainbow *and* rain, both as appearances) and the realm of things in themselves. The danger is to confuse the empirical-objective (rain as "thing in itself" in the everyday sense) with the transcendentally real (thing in itself in Kant's strict sense). Even the rain — the drops, their shape, the space they fall through — are all transcendentally only appearances, even though empirically they count as objective things.

If, however, we take this empirical \[object\] in general and ask, without regard to its agreement with every human sense, whether it represents an object in itself (not in the raindrops, for these are already as appearances empirical objects), then the question of the relation of the representation to the object is *transcendental*; and not only are these drops mere appearances, but even their round shape — indeed, even the space in which they fall — are nothing in themselves, but merely modifications or grounds of our sensible intuition; the transcendental object, however, remains unknown to us. The full transcendental analysis is now spelled out: at the empirical level, raindrops are real objects; at the transcendental level, raindrops (and their roundness, and the space they fall in) are appearances; the *transzendentale Objekt* (transcendental object) — what corresponds to the appearances as their unknown ground — remains *unbekannt*, unknown.

The second important matter of our transcendental aesthetic is: that it acquire not merely as a *plausible hypothesis* some favor, but be as certain and indubitable as can ever be demanded from a theory that is to serve as an organon. To make this certainty fully evident, we will choose some case in which its validity may become palpable and serve for the greater clarity of what has been adduced in §3. The constructive proof of the Aesthetic is now offered. The argument: geometry's synthetic a priori propositions are actual (factum); how are they possible? Only through pure a priori intuition; only if space is a subjective form of the subject. Hence the transcendental ideality of space is not a mere hypothesis but a *necessary* condition of the *factum* of geometry. The case-study method is then deployed.

Suppose, then, that space and time were objective in themselves and conditions of the possibility of things in themselves: then it appears first that of both, apodictic and synthetic propositions a priori in great number — chiefly of space — occur, which we shall therefore preferentially investigate here as example. Since the propositions of geometry are cognized synthetically a priori and with apodictic certainty, I ask: whence do you take such propositions, and on what does our understanding rely in order to attain such absolutely necessary and universally valid truths? There is no other way than through concepts or through intuitions; but both, as such, are either given a priori or a posteriori. The latter — namely empirical concepts and likewise that on which they are based, empirical intuition — can give no synthetic proposition except such as is itself merely empirical, i.e., a proposition of experience, hence can never contain *necessity and absolute universality*, such as however is the characteristic of all propositions of geometry. The transcendental argument is now spelled out as a regressive proof. Geometric propositions are apodictic — necessary and strictly universal. They cannot be cognized through empirical means (which yields only inductive generalization, not strict necessity). Therefore they must be cognized through a priori means. There are two possibilities: through pure concepts, or through pure intuition.

But what would be the first and only means to attain such cognitions, namely through mere concepts or through intuitions a priori, it is clear that from mere concepts no synthetic but only analytic cognition can be obtained. Take only the proposition: that through two straight lines no space at all can be enclosed, hence no figure is possible; and try to derive it from the concept of straight lines and the number two; or also, that from three straight lines a figure is possible, and try the same merely from these concepts. All your effort is in vain, and you see yourself forced to take refuge in intuition, as geometry also always does. You give yourself, therefore, an object in *intuition*; but of what kind is this — is it a pure intuition a priori or an empirical one? If it were the latter, then never could a universally valid, much less an apodictic, proposition come from it; for experience can never furnish such. You must therefore give your object a priori in intuition, and on this ground your synthetic proposition. The argument now eliminates the option of synthetic-from-concepts. The example: from the concepts of *straight line* and *number two* alone, one cannot derive that two straight lines cannot enclose a space; one needs intuition. Hence geometric cognition is synthetic-from-intuition, not synthetic-from-concept. And the intuition must be a priori (not empirical), since the proposition is apodictic.

Now if there did not lie in you a faculty of intuiting a priori; if this subjective condition were not at the same time as to form the universal a priori condition under which alone the object of this (outer) intuition is itself possible; if the object (the triangle) were something in itself, without relation to your subject: how could you say that what necessarily lies in your subjective conditions for constructing a triangle must also necessarily belong to the triangle in itself? For you could surely not add anything new (the figure) to your concepts (of three lines), which therefore would have to be necessarily encountered in the object, since this is given before your cognition and not through it. If, therefore, space (and so also time) were not a *mere form of your intuition* which contains a priori conditions under which alone things can be outer objects for you, which without these subjective conditions are nothing in themselves: then you could a priori determine absolutely nothing synthetically about outer objects. It is therefore unquestionably certain, and not merely possible or even probable, that space and time, as the necessary conditions of all (outer and inner) experience, are merely subjective conditions of all our intuition; in respect to which therefore all objects are mere appearances and not things given for themselves in this manner — of which, with respect to what concerns their form, much can be said a priori, but never the least about the thing in itself which may lie at the ground of these appearances. The constructive argument is now complete. The synthetic-a-priori-from-intuition possibility requires that the intuition be subjective (a feature of the subject); else the necessary connection between what holds in the construction and what holds of the object cannot be explained. If the triangle were a thing in itself, our subjective construction of it would have no necessary bearing on it. The transcendental ideality of space (and time) is therefore not a hypothesis but a *necessary condition* of geometric (and arithmetic) cognition.

II. To the confirmation of this theory of the ideality of outer as well as inner sense — hence of all objects of the senses as mere appearances — can especially serve the observation: that everything in our cognition that belongs to intuition (with the exception of feeling of pleasure and displeasure and the will, which are not cognitions at all) contains nothing but mere *relations* — of places in an intuition (extension), of alteration of places (motion), and of laws according to which this alteration is determined (moving forces). But what is present in the place, or what (apart from change of place) operates in the things themselves, is not given through this. Through mere relations, however, a thing in itself is not cognized: therefore it is well to judge that, since through outer sense nothing but mere representations of relations is given to us, this sense can also contain in its representation only the *relation* of an object to the subject, and not the *inner* which belongs to the object in itself. The relationalism-of-intuition argument is a striking and important addition in B. What outer intuition gives us, Kant claims, is only *Verhältnisse* (relations): spatial relations (extension), motion-relations, force-relations. We never get the *inner* nature of what underlies the relations — the relata themselves apart from their relations. Hence outer sense cannot present things in themselves (which would require knowing the inner nature of the relata) but only the relations as they appear to the subject. With inner intuition, Kant continues, it is just the same. Not only that the representations of outer senses constitute the proper material with which we occupy our mind; but also the time in which we place these representations — which itself precedes the consciousness of them in experience, and as the formal condition of the manner in which we place them in the mind, lies at the ground — already contains relations of succession, simultaneity, and of that which is simultaneous with succession (the *permanent*). Now what, as representation, can precede every action of thinking something is *intuition*; and if it contains nothing but relations, it is the form of intuition, which, since it represents nothing except insofar as something is placed in the mind, can be nothing other than the manner in which the mind is affected through its own activity — namely, this placing of its representation, hence through itself — i.e., an inner sense as to its form. Everything that is represented through a sense is to that extent always appearance; and an inner sense would therefore either have to be not granted at all, or the subject which is the object of it would only be represented through it as appearance, not as it would judge of itself if its intuition were mere self-activity — i.e., intellectual. The doctrine of inner sense is now developed with great care. *Time* is the form of inner sense; it contains only relations (succession, simultaneity, permanence). What is intuited in inner sense is therefore relations of the mind's own activity to itself — the mind affecting itself by placing representations. This is *Selbstaffektion* — self-affection. The subject as represented through inner sense is therefore the subject *as appearance to itself*, not the subject as it is in itself. This conclusion is one of the most consequential of the Critique: even the soul (mind), as known to itself empirically, is appearance, not thing-in-itself. Hereby all difficulty rests only on this: how a subject can intuit itself inwardly; but this difficulty is common to every theory. Self-consciousness (apperception) is the simple representation of the I, and if through this alone all the manifold in the subject were given self-actively (*selbsttätig*), then inner intuition would be intellectual. In man, however, this consciousness requires inner *perception* of the manifold that is given in the subject beforehand; and the manner in which this is given in the mind without spontaneity must, for the sake of this distinction, be called *sensibility*. If the faculty of becoming conscious of oneself is to seek out (apprehend) what lies in the mind, it must affect the mind; and only in this way can it bring about an intuition of itself, whose form, however — which lies at the ground in the mind beforehand — determines, in the representation of time, the manner in which the manifold is together in the mind; since the mind then intuits itself, not as it would represent itself immediately self-actively, but according to the manner in which it is inwardly affected; consequently, *as it appears to itself, not as it is*. The distinction between intellectual self-intuition (which would yield the soul as it is in itself) and sensible self-intuition (which yields the soul as appearance) is one of the most important distinctions in the Critique. Human self-consciousness is *not* intellectual; it requires inner perception, which is mediated by the form of time, which involves self-affection. Therefore human self-knowledge is empirical and reaches only the soul-as-appearance, not the soul-in-itself. This conclusion will be the central point of the Paralogisms of Pure Reason.

III. When I say: in space and time intuition represents both outer objects as well as the self-intuition of the mind, both as they affect our senses — i.e., as they *appear* — this does not mean that these objects would be a mere *illusion* (*Schein*). For in appearance the objects, indeed even the constitutions which we attribute to them, are always regarded as something really given; only that, insofar as this constitution depends only on the manner of intuition of the subject in the relation of the given object to it, this object as *appearance* is distinguished from itself as object in itself. So I do not say that bodies *merely seem* to be outside me, or that my soul merely seems to be given in my self-consciousness, when I claim that the quality of space and time — in conformity with which, as a condition of their existence, I posit both — lies in my manner of intuition and not in these objects in themselves. It would be my own fault if I made into mere illusion what I should count as appearance. The footnote distinguishes: predicates of appearance can be attributed to the object in relation to our sense (red to the rose, smell), but illusion (*Schein*) can never be attributed to the object as such, because *Schein* is precisely the attribution to the object-as-such of what belongs to it only in relation to the subject — e.g., the rings (handles) once attributed to Saturn. The distinction *Erscheinung* vs *Schein* — appearance vs illusion — is critical. *Erscheinung* is the *way an object is given to us*, including its relational features (color, taste, the spatiotemporal form); these are objectively valid for our experience. *Schein* (illusion) arises when we *misattribute* features that belong only in relation to the subject to the object *in itself* (saying *the rose is red in itself*, *Saturn has rings as it is in itself*). The Critique therefore does not reduce appearances to illusions; it gives appearances their proper objective status while reserving for things-in-themselves their unknowability.

But this does not happen according to our principle of the ideality of all our sensible intuitions; rather, if one attributes *objective reality* to those forms of representation, then one cannot avoid having everything thereby converted into mere illusion. For if one regards space and time as constitutions which, as far as their possibility is concerned, must be encountered in things in themselves, and considers the absurdities (*Ungereimtheiten*) in which one then becomes entangled — in that two infinite things, which are not substances nor anything actually inhering in substances, yet still existing, indeed the necessary condition of the existence of all things, must remain over even if all existing things are abolished — then one cannot really blame the good Berkeley if he reduces bodies to mere illusion; indeed, even our own existence, which would in this manner be made dependent on the for-itself-subsisting reality of a non-thing (*Unding*) like time, would have to be converted, with this, into mere illusion — an absurdity that no one has yet wished to bear the guilt of. The polemic against the Newtonian absolute view returns: it generates *Ungereimtheiten* (absurdities) — two infinite non-things, hovering as ungrounded conditions. The reductio: under such a view, Berkeley's idealism becomes defensible (since the infinite non-things would be merely fictions), and even our own existence becomes precarious (since it would depend on these phantoms). The Kantian doctrine avoids this absurdity precisely because it locates space and time in the subject's manner of intuition, not in things-in-themselves.

IV. In natural theology, where one thinks of an object that not only cannot for us be any object of intuition, but cannot itself at all be an object of sensible intuition, one is careful to remove from all its intuition (for such must all its cognition be, and not *thinking*, which always indicates limits) the conditions of time and space. But with what right can one do this, if one has previously made both into forms of things in themselves, and indeed such as would, as conditions of the existence of things a priori, remain over even if one had abolished the things themselves: for as conditions of all existence in general, they must also be it of the existence of God. There remains nothing — if one will not make them into objective forms of all things — but to make them into subjective forms of our outer as well as inner manner of intuition; which is called *sensible* because it is not *original* (i.e., one through which the very existence of the object of intuition is given — and which, as far as we can see, can belong only to the primordial being), but rather depends on the existence of the object, hence is possible only through the faculty of representation of the subject being affected by it. The argument from natural theology is now made. Theology asserts a non-sensible knowledge of God, which means a cognition not constrained by space and time. But if space and time are conditions of things-in-themselves, they would also have to be conditions of God's existence (since God is *the* most real thing). This is absurd. Therefore space and time cannot be conditions of things-in-themselves; they must be subjective forms of our manner of intuition. The distinction between *intuitus derivativus* (derivative intuition, ours) and *intuitus originarius* (original intuition, divine) is here named. Divine intuition would not be receptive but constitutive — the object would exist only by being intuited by God. Our intuition is the opposite: receptive, dependent on objects given to us, hence sensible.

It is also not necessary that we restrict the manner of intuition in space and time to the sensibility of man; it may be that all finite thinking beings must necessarily agree with man in this (although we cannot decide this); yet for this universal validity it does not therefore cease to be *sensibility* — just because it is *derivative* (*intuitus derivativus*), not original (*intuitus originarius*), hence not intellectual intuition, which as the just-mentioned ground belongs only to the primordial being, but never to a being dependent both as regards its existence and its intuition (which determines its existence in relation to given objects); although this latter remark must be counted in our aesthetic theory only as *clarification*, not as ground of proof. The qualification is then made: even if all finite thinking beings shared space and time with us, this would not make space and time things-in-themselves. *Universality* among finite beings is not transcendental reality. The criterion is *derivativus*/*originarius* — derivative receptive intuition vs. original creative intuition. Only the latter would be non-sensible; finite intuition, however widely shared, is sensible.

#### **Conclusion of the Transcendental Aesthetic**

Here we have now one of the requisite pieces for the solution of the general problem of transcendental philosophy: *how are synthetic propositions a priori possible?* — namely, pure intuitions a priori, space and time; in which, when we wish in an a priori judgment to go beyond a given concept, we encounter that which is not in the concept but is yet a priori discoverable in the intuition corresponding to it, and can be synthetically connected with it. But such judgments can, on this ground, never reach further than to objects of the senses, and can hold only for objects of possible experience. The concluding paragraph of the Aesthetic states its place in the architectonic of the Critique. The Aesthetic has solved one piece of the central question — *Wie sind synthetische Urteile a priori möglich?* — by identifying the pure a priori intuitions (space and time) that ground the synthetic-a-priori cognition of mathematics. The crucial *limiting* clause is then added: this solution holds only for *objects of the senses*, i.e., objects of possible experience. Synthetic a priori cognition does not reach beyond the field of appearances. This restriction will be the recurring theme of the Logic, especially in the Dialectic, where the illegitimate extension of synthetic a priori cognition beyond the field of experience is diagnosed as the source of dialectical illusion.

## **Second Part: The Transcendental Logic**

### **Introduction: Idea of a Transcendental Logic**

#### **I. On Logic in General**

Our cognition, Kant begins, arises from two basic sources of the mind: the first is to receive representations (*receptivity of impressions*), the second the faculty to cognize an object through these representations (*spontaneity of concepts*); through the first an object is *given* to us, through the second the object is *thought* in relation to that representation (as a mere determination of the mind). *Intuition and concepts* therefore constitute the elements of all our cognition, so that neither concepts without intuition corresponding to them in any way, nor intuition without concepts, can yield a cognition. Both are either *pure* or *empirical*. *Empirical*, if sensation (which presupposes the actual presence of the object) is contained in them; *pure*, however, if no sensation is mixed in with the representation. The latter can be called the matter of sensible cognition. Therefore *pure intuition* contains only the *form* under which something is intuited, and *pure concept* contains only the form of the thinking of an object in general. Only pure intuitions or concepts are possible a priori; empirical only a posteriori. The opening of the Logic resumes and extends the framework of the Aesthetic. The two stems — sensibility and understanding — are now functionally characterized again: receptivity vs. spontaneity, giving vs. thinking, intuition vs. concept. The crucial systematic claim is made: *neither concepts without intuition nor intuition without concepts can yield a cognition*. This will be the leitmotif of the entire Logic. Cognition requires the conjunction of the two stems; either alone is sterile. The matter/form distinction is then applied within both stems: pure intuition (form of sensibility) / empirical intuition (form \+ sensation as matter); pure concept (form of thought) / empirical concept (form \+ empirical content).

If we will call the receptivity of our mind to receive representations insofar as it is in any way affected, *sensibility*, then by contrast the faculty to bring forth representations itself — or the *spontaneity of cognition* — is the *understanding* (*Verstand*). Our nature brings it with itself that intuition can never be other than *sensible* — i.e., it contains only the manner in which we are affected by objects. The faculty, however, to *think* the object of sensible intuition is the *understanding*. Neither of these properties is to be preferred to the other. Without sensibility no object would be given to us, and without understanding none would be thought. *Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind* (*Gedanken ohne Inhalt sind leer, Anschauungen ohne Begriffe sind blind*). Therefore it is just as necessary to make one's concepts sensible (i.e., to add to them the object in intuition) as to make one's intuitions understandable (i.e., to bring them under concepts). Both faculties or capacities also cannot exchange their functions. The understanding cannot intuit anything, and the senses cannot think anything. Only from their *unification* can cognition arise. But one must not for this reason mix their share, but has great cause to carefully *separate* and *distinguish* each from the other. Therefore we distinguish the science of the rules of sensibility in general — i.e., *aesthetic* — from the science of the rules of understanding in general — i.e., *logic*. The most famous formula of the Critique is here stated: *Gedanken ohne Inhalt sind leer, Anschauungen ohne Begriffe sind blind*. The balance is reciprocal: each faculty needs the other. Concepts without intuition lack content (the "matter" of cognition); intuitions without concepts lack structure (the "form" of cognition). The methodological consequence is twofold: (i) the cooperative thesis — cognition requires both faculties working together; (ii) the separation thesis — the faculties have distinct functions and must be kept distinct in transcendental analysis. The Critique violates neither: it analyzes sensibility (Aesthetic) and understanding (Logic) separately for the sake of clarity, while showing them to be jointly required for actual cognition.

The logic can again be undertaken with a twofold intention, Kant continues — either as the logic of the *general* or of the *particular* use of the understanding. The first contains the absolutely necessary rules of thinking, without which no use of the understanding takes place, and goes therefore to this use without regard to the difference of objects to which it may be directed. The logic of the *particular* use of the understanding contains the rules of correctly thinking about a certain kind of object. The former one can call *elementary logic*, the latter the *organon* of this or that science. The latter is largely advanced in the schools as a propaedeutic to the sciences, although in accordance with the course of human reason it is the latest at which one arrives, when the science is already long ready and needs only the last hand to its correction and completion. For one must know the objects already to a fairly high degree if one wishes to give the rules according to which a science of them is to be brought about. The general/particular distinction divides logic into (i) general logic — the rules valid for thinking about anything whatever, abstracting from the particular object; (ii) particular logic — the rules valid for thinking about a determinate kind of object (e.g., the logic of mathematics, the logic of jurisprudence). The latter is dependent on prior empirical familiarity with the object-domain.

General logic is now either *pure* or *applied logic*. In the first, we abstract from all empirical conditions under which our understanding is exercised — e.g., the influence of the senses, the play of imagination, the laws of memory, the power of habit, of inclination, etc., hence also the sources of prejudices, indeed in general all causes from which certain cognitions may arise or be foisted upon us — because they concern merely the understanding under certain circumstances of its application, and to know these requires experience. A *general but pure logic* therefore has to do with mere a priori principles, and is a *canon of the understanding and of reason*, but only in regard to the formal of their use, whatever the content may be (empirical or transcendental). A *general logic* however is called *applied* if it is directed to the rules of the use of the understanding under the subjective empirical conditions which psychology teaches us. It has therefore empirical principles, although it is still general insofar as it concerns the use of the understanding without distinction of objects. For that reason it is also neither a canon of the understanding in general nor an organon of particular sciences, but merely a *cathartic* (*Kathartikon*) of common understanding. The pure/applied distinction within general logic separates (i) pure logic — strictly a priori, formal, the canon of valid thinking as such; (ii) applied logic — empirical, psychological, concerning how the understanding actually functions under empirical conditions (attention, doubt, conviction). The latter is a *Kathartikon* — a purgative or cleansing aid — for common understanding, but it is neither a pure science nor an organon of any determinate field. The proper logical science is *general pure logic*.

In general logic, then, the part which is to constitute the *pure doctrine of reason* must be entirely separated from that which constitutes *applied* (although still general) logic. The first alone is properly science, although brief and dry, and as the school-correct presentation of an elementary doctrine of the understanding requires. In this, then, the logicians must always have two rules before their eyes. 1\) As general logic, it abstracts from all content of cognition of the understanding and from the difference of its objects, and has to do with nothing but the mere form of thinking. 2\) As pure logic, it has no empirical principles, hence draws nothing (as one has sometimes persuaded oneself) from psychology, which therefore has no influence on the canon of the understanding. It is a demonstrated doctrine, and everything in it must be completely a priori certain. The two methodological rules are then stated explicitly: (i) general logic abstracts from content; (ii) pure logic abstracts from empirical psychological principles. The independence-from-psychology thesis is significant — Kant rejects any psychologistic reading of logic, anticipating the antipsychologism of Frege and Husserl by more than a century.

What I call *applied logic* (against the common meaning of this word, according to which it should contain certain exercises for which pure logic gives the rules) is a representation of the understanding and the rules of its necessary use *in concreto* — namely, under the contingent conditions of the subject which can hinder or further this use, and which are altogether given only empirically. It treats of attention, its hindrance and consequences, the origin of error, the state of doubt, of scruple, of conviction, etc.; and to it general and pure logic stands as pure morality (which contains merely the necessary moral laws of a free will in general) to the actual doctrine of virtue, which considers these laws under the hindrances of feelings, inclinations, and passions to which men are more or less subject, and which can never yield a true and demonstrated science, because it requires, just as that applied logic, empirical and psychological principles. The methodological parallel to ethics is illuminating: pure logic : applied logic :: pure morality : doctrine of virtue. In both cases, the pure doctrine is a strictly a priori science of the necessary rules; the applied doctrine is an empirical-psychological extension that brings these rules to bear on the actual conditions of finite agents.

#### **II. On Transcendental Logic**

General logic abstracts, as we have shown, from all content of cognition, i.e., from all reference of it to its object, and considers only the logical form in the relation of cognitions to one another, i.e., the form of thinking in general. Since, however, there are now both pure and empirical intuitions (as the transcendental aesthetic shows), a distinction could well also be encountered between *pure* and *empirical thinking* of objects. In this case there would be a logic in which one did not abstract from all content of cognition; for that one which contained merely the rules of the pure thinking of an object would exclude all those cognitions of empirical content. It would also concern the *origin* of our cognitions of objects insofar as this origin cannot be ascribed to the objects; whereas general logic has nothing to do with this origin of cognition, but considers the representations — whether they may be given to us originally a priori in ourselves, or only empirically — merely according to the laws by which the understanding uses them in relation to one another when it thinks, and so deals only with the *form of the understanding* that can be furnished to the representations, however else they may have originated. The transition from general to transcendental logic is now effected. General logic abstracts from content; transcendental logic does *not* abstract from a particular content — namely, the pure a priori content furnished by the Aesthetic (space and time). Transcendental logic is therefore a logic of *pure* (non-empirical) thinking that has its proper content. It also concerns the *origin* of our cognitions in their non-empirical aspects — a question general logic ignores.

And here I make a remark, Kant continues, which extends its influence over all the following considerations, and which one must keep well in view: namely, that not every cognition a priori, but only that through which we cognize *that and how* certain representations (intuitions or concepts) are exclusively applied a priori or are possible, must be called *transcendental* (i.e., the possibility of cognition or the use of it a priori). Therefore neither space nor any geometric determination of it a priori is a *transcendental representation*; rather, only the cognition that these representations are not at all of empirical origin, and the possibility that they can nevertheless relate a priori to objects of experience, can be called transcendental. Likewise the use of space about objects in general would also be transcendental; but if it is restricted merely to objects of the senses, then it is called *empirical*. The distinction between transcendental and empirical therefore concerns only the *critique* of cognitions and does not concern their relation to their object. The crucial definition of *transzendental* is here refined. *Transzendental* names not all a priori cognition but specifically that cognition which concerns *how and whether* certain a priori representations are *possible* and *applicable*. Therefore *space* is not itself a transcendental representation — it is a pure a priori intuition. But the *recognition that space is a pure a priori intuition with valid application to objects of experience* — that is transcendental cognition. The distinction is methodological: it concerns the level of reflection on cognitions, not the cognitions themselves.

In expectation, therefore, that there may perhaps be concepts that relate a priori to objects — not as pure or sensible intuitions but merely as *actions of pure thinking*, hence concepts, but neither of empirical nor of aesthetic origin — we make for ourselves in advance the idea of a *science of pure understanding- and reason-cognition* through which we think objects entirely a priori. Such a science, which would determine the *origin*, the *extent*, and the *objective validity* of such cognitions, would have to be called *transcendental logic*, because it has to do merely with the laws of understanding and reason, but solely insofar as they are related a priori to objects, and not — as general logic — to empirical as well as pure cognitions of reason without distinction. The idea of *Transzendentale Logik* is now defined. It is a science of pure understanding- and reason-cognition, determining (i) the origin, (ii) the extent, (iii) the objective validity of such a priori concepts. The Transcendental Analytic will treat the categories (the a priori concepts of the understanding); the Transcendental Dialectic will treat the Ideas (the a priori concepts of reason). The methodological program of the rest of the Critique is here announced.

#### **III. On the Division of General Logic into Analytic and Dialectic**

The old and famous question by which one thought to drive logicians into a corner, and to bring them to the point that they should either be caught in a miserable *diallele* or confess their ignorance — hence the vanity of their whole art — is this: *What is truth?* The nominal definition of truth — that it is namely the *agreement of cognition with its object* — is here granted and presupposed; but one demands to know what the universal and reliable criterion of the truth of any cognition is. It is already a great and necessary proof of wisdom or insight to know what one ought reasonably to ask. For if the question is in itself absurd and demands unnecessary answers, then it has — besides the shaming of him who poses it — sometimes also the disadvantage of leading the incautious hearer to absurd answers, and presenting the laughable spectacle (as the ancients said) that the one milks the he-goat and the other holds the sieve. The question of truth is opened, and the famous figure of the milked he-goat (he-goats do not give milk) is used to illustrate absurd inquiries. The *Namenserklärung* (nominal definition) — *agreement of cognition with object* — is granted. The question is whether there is a universal criterion of truth.

If truth consists in the agreement of a cognition with its object, then this object must thereby be distinguished from others; for a cognition is false if it does not agree with the object to which it is referred, though it may contain something that could well hold of other objects. Now a *universal criterion of truth* would be that which would be valid of all cognitions without distinction of their objects. But it is clear that, since with such a criterion one abstracts from all *content* of cognition (its relation to its object), and truth concerns precisely this content, it is entirely impossible and absurd to demand a mark of the truth of this content of cognitions, and that a *sufficient* and yet at the same time *universal* characteristic of truth cannot be given. The diagnostic argument is sharp: a universal criterion of truth would have to abstract from content (to be applicable to all cognitions); but truth concerns the content (the agreement with the object); therefore a *universal-and-sufficient* criterion is impossible. The impossibility is logical, not contingent: the very combination of universality and sufficiency is contradictory.

But as regards the cognition *with respect to mere form* (setting aside all content), it is just as clear that a logic, insofar as it presents the universal and necessary rules of the understanding, must precisely in these rules lay down *criteria of truth*. For what contradicts these rules is false, because the understanding thereby contradicts its own universal rules of thinking, hence itself. These criteria, however, concern only the *form* of truth — i.e., of thinking in general — and are to this extent quite right, but not sufficient. For although a cognition might be in complete accord with the logical form — i.e., did not contradict itself — it could still always contradict the object. Therefore the merely logical criterion of truth — namely, the agreement of a cognition with the universal and formal laws of the understanding and of reason — is indeed the *conditio sine qua non*, hence the *negative* condition of all truth: further than this, however, logic cannot go, and the error that concerns not the form but the content, logic can discover through no *touchstone*. The methodological distinction: logical criteria of truth are *negative* (necessary but not sufficient). Self-contradictoriness is a sure mark of falsity, but consistency is not a sure mark of truth — one can be consistent and yet false. Logic is therefore the *conditio sine qua non* of truth, not its sufficient ground.

General logic now resolves the whole formal business of the understanding and of reason into its elements and presents them as principles of all logical judgment of our cognition. This part of logic can therefore be called *Analytic*, and is for that reason at least the *negative touchstone of truth*, in that one must first test and appraise all cognition, with respect to its form, by these rules, before one investigates it itself with respect to its content, in order to determine whether it contains positive truth with respect to the object. Since the mere form of cognition, however much it may agree with logical laws, by no means yet suffices to constitute the *material (objective) truth* of the cognition, no one can dare merely with logic to judge of objects and to assert anything without having previously taken in well-grounded information about them apart from logic, in order then merely to attempt the use and combination of them in a connected whole according to logical laws — yet better, to test them solely thereby. Yet there lies something so misleading in the possession of so plausible an art, of giving all our cognitions the form of the understanding, although with respect to their content one may still be very empty and poor, that this general logic — which is merely a *canon* of judgment — is used as it were as an *organon* for the actual production of objective assertions (at least for the illusion of them), and is thereby in fact misused. The general logic, as supposed organon, is called *Dialectic*. The misuse of logic — pretending that logic alone can generate objective assertions — is now diagnosed. Logic is a *Kanon* (rules of judgment) but is being misused as an *Organon* (instrument of generating cognition). When so misused, general logic is *Dialektik* — the logic of illusion (*Logik des Scheins*).

However different the meaning may be in which the ancients used this name of a science or art, one can yet from its actual use safely infer that with them it was nothing other than the *logic of illusion* (*Logik des Scheins*). A sophistical art of giving to one's ignorance — indeed, also to one's deliberate deceptions — the appearance of truth, by imitating the method of thoroughness which logic in general prescribes, and using its topic to gloss over every empty pretense. The historical genealogy of *Dialektik* — its sophistical origins — is now sketched. The ancient *Dialektik* was a tool of sophistry: imitating logical form to make ignorance or deception look like truth. Now one can note as a sure and useful warning: that general logic, considered as an *Organon*, is always a logic of illusion — i.e., dialectical. For since it teaches us nothing at all about the content of cognition, but only the formal conditions of agreement with the understanding (which moreover are wholly indifferent with respect to objects), the imposition of using it as an instrument (organon) for spreading out and extending one's cognitions, at least according to the pretense, can only run out to *garrulity* — to assert with some semblance what one wants, or also to attack what one wants at will. Such instruction is in no way conformable to the *dignity of philosophy*. Therefore one has counted this designation of *Dialectic* rather, as a *critique of dialectical illusion*, to logic; and as such we shall also wish to understand it here. The reform of the term *Dialektik* is now made: from sophistical pseudo-organon to *Kritik des dialektischen Scheins* — critique of dialectical illusion. The Transcendental Dialectic, much later in the Critique, will be this critique of illusion in its specifically transcendental application.

#### **IV. On the Division of Transcendental Logic into Transcendental Analytic and Dialectic**

In a transcendental logic, Kant continues, we *isolate* the understanding (as above in the transcendental aesthetic we did sensibility) and lift out merely the part of thinking from our cognition which has its origin solely in the understanding. The use of this pure cognition, however, rests on this as its condition: that objects be given to us in intuition, to which it can be applied. For without intuition, all our cognition lacks objects, and remains then completely empty. That part of transcendental logic, then, which presents the elements of the pure cognition of the understanding and the principles without which any object whatsoever can be thought, is the *transcendental analytic*, and at the same time a *logic of truth* (*Logik der Wahrheit*). For no cognition can contradict it without losing all content at the same time — i.e., all reference to any object, hence all truth. The methodological parallel to the Aesthetic is now made: as the Aesthetic isolated sensibility, so the Logic isolates the understanding. The *Transzendentale Analytik* contains the elements of pure understanding-cognition (the categories and their principles); it is called a *Logik der Wahrheit* because any cognition contradicting it loses its very relation to objects (and therefore its truth).

But because it is very alluring and tempting to make use of these pure cognitions and principles of the understanding alone, even beyond the limits of experience — which alone can give us the material (objects) to which those pure concepts of the understanding can be applied — the understanding falls into the danger, through empty sophistries, of making a *material use* of the merely formal principles of the pure understanding, and of judging indiscriminately about objects which are not given to us, indeed which perhaps cannot be given to us in any manner. Since it should properly be only a canon of the appraisal of empirical use, it is misused when one lets it count as the organon of a universal and unrestricted use, and dares with the pure understanding alone to judge synthetically about objects in general, to assert, and to decide. The use of the pure understanding would then be *dialectical*. The second part of transcendental logic must therefore be a *critique of this dialectical illusion*, and is called *transzendentale Dialektik* — not as an art of dogmatically producing such illusion (an art unfortunately very current in the manifold metaphysical jugglery), but as a critique of the understanding and of reason with respect to their hyperphysical use, in order to uncover the false illusion of their groundless pretensions and to reduce their claims to invention and extension — which they suppose to be reached merely through transcendental principles — to the mere appraisal and protection of the pure understanding from sophistical illusion. The architectonic of the Transcendental Logic is now complete: *Analytik* \= positive doctrine of legitimate a priori cognition; *Dialektik* \= critique of illegitimate a priori pretension. The Dialectic is reformed from sophistical art to critical task: not producing illusion, but detecting and dispelling it.

### **First Division: The Transcendental Analytic**

This analytic, Kant begins, is the *resolution* (*Zergliederung*) of our entire a priori cognition into the elements of the pure cognition of the understanding. The following points are crucial here. 1\) That the concepts are pure and not empirical concepts. 2\) That they belong not to intuition and sensibility, but to thinking and the understanding. 3\) That they are *elementary* concepts and are well distinguished from the *derivative* or composite ones. 4\) That their *table* is complete and they entirely fill the whole field of the pure understanding. Now this completeness of a science cannot reliably be assumed by an estimate of an aggregate brought about merely through tentative essays; hence it is possible only by means of an *idea of the whole* of a priori cognition of the understanding and through the determinate division of the concepts that constitute it deriving from this idea — hence only through their connection in a *system*. The pure understanding separates itself completely not only from everything empirical but also from all sensibility. It is therefore a unity self-subsistent for itself, sufficient to itself, and not to be increased by any externally added addendum. Hence the totality (*Inbegriff*) of its cognition will constitute a system to be comprised and determined under one *idea* — whose completeness and articulation can at the same time provide a *touchstone* of the correctness and genuineness of all the cognitive pieces that fit into it. The four desiderata for the Analytic are stated: (1) pure not empirical; (2) belonging to thinking not intuition; (3) elementary not derivative; (4) the table is complete and exhaustive. The methodological warrant for completeness is the *idea of the whole* — the systematic articulation of pure understanding under a single principle. This will turn out to be the principle of judgment: the categories arise from the logical forms of judgment.

This whole part of transcendental logic consists of two *books*, one of which contains the *concepts*, the other the *principles* of the pure understanding. The two-book structure of the Analytic is announced: Book I will treat the categories (the *Begriffe* of pure understanding); Book II will treat the principles (the *Grundsätze* corresponding to them). The present chapter through page 190 covers most of Book I.

#### **First Book: The Analytic of Concepts**

By the analytic of concepts I understand, Kant continues, *not* the analysis of them, or the usual procedure in philosophical investigations of decomposing concepts that present themselves according to their content and bringing them to distinctness, but rather the as yet little attempted *dissection of the faculty of the understanding itself* (*Zergliederung des Verstandesvermögens selbst*), in order to investigate the possibility of a priori concepts by seeking them out in the understanding alone — as their place of birth — and by analyzing its pure use in general; for this is the peculiar business of a transcendental philosophy. The rest is the logical treatment of concepts in philosophy in general. We shall therefore pursue the pure concepts to their *first germs and predispositions* (*Keime und Anlagen*) in the human understanding, in which they lie prepared until at last, on occasion of experience, they are developed and, through this same understanding, freed from their attached empirical conditions and presented in their purity. The methodological clarification is striking. The "Analytic of Concepts" does *not* mean the analysis of pre-given concepts (the ordinary procedure of philosophical analysis, in which one analyzes the concept *body*, *cause*, etc.). It means rather the *dissection of the faculty of understanding* — investigating the *origin* of a priori concepts in the structure of understanding itself. The biological metaphor — *Keime und Anlagen* (germs, predispositions) — preserves the Leibnizian notion of innate dispositions while purifying it of any psychologistic empirical character. The categories lie in the understanding as predispositions, are *occasioned* by experience (which awakens them), and are then *freed from their empirical conditions* by transcendental philosophy.

##### **First Chapter: On the Clue to the Discovery of All Pure Concepts of the Understanding**

When one sets a faculty of cognition into play, Kant begins, then, according to various occasions, different concepts emerge which make this faculty recognizable and which can be collected in a more or less detailed essay, the longer the observation of them has been made or the more acutely. Where this investigation may be completed cannot, on this mechanical procedure as it were, be determined with certainty. Also, the concepts that one only thus by chance comes upon do not reveal themselves in any order or systematic unity, but are at last only paired according to similarities and arranged in series according to the magnitude of their content — from the simple to the more composite — which are nothing less than systematic, although in a certain manner methodical. The methodological survey of the previous (Aristotelian) procedure is offered. Aristotle's list of categories was a *mechanical* procedure (*gleichsam mechanisches Verfahren*) — collecting whatever concepts emerged in the use of understanding, without a guiding principle. The result was unsystematic — concepts paired by similarity, ordered by complexity, but without principled completeness.

Transcendental philosophy, Kant continues, has the advantage but also the obligation to seek out its concepts according to a *principle*; because they arise from the understanding (as absolute unity) pure and unmixed, and must therefore themselves cohere among themselves according to one *concept* or *idea*. Such a connection, however, provides a *rule* by means of which each pure concept of the understanding can be assigned its place a priori, and the completeness of all of them together can be determined — which would otherwise depend on caprice or on chance. The methodological program of the *Leitfaden* (clue) is now stated. From the unity of the understanding follows the systematic unity of its concepts; from their systematic unity follows the possibility of a complete a priori derivation. The "clue" will be the table of logical forms of judgment: from this single table, all the categories can be derived in a principled way, and their completeness can be guaranteed.

###### **First Section: On the Logical Use of the Understanding in General**

The understanding has been only negatively defined above: as a non-sensible faculty of cognition. We can have no intuition independently of sensibility. The understanding is therefore no faculty of intuition. But there is, besides intuition, no other manner of cognition than through *concepts*. Therefore the cognition of every — at least the human — understanding is a *cognition through concepts*: not intuitive but *discursive* (*diskursiv*). All intuitions, as sensible, rest on *affections*; concepts therefore on *functions*. But by *function* I understand the unity of the act of ordering different representations under a common one. Concepts are therefore grounded on the *spontaneity* of thinking, as sensible intuitions are on the *receptivity* of impressions. The understanding cannot make any other use of these concepts than to *judge* by means of them. Since no representation goes immediately to the object except intuition alone, a concept is never related immediately to an object, but rather to some other representation of it (whether intuition or itself already a concept). The understanding is now positively characterized: it is a faculty of *discursive* cognition through *concepts*, which are functions of the unity of representations. Affections (passive impressions from sensibility) are contrasted with *Funktionen* (acts of unifying spontaneity). And concepts are functions — not static contents but acts of unification, ordering manifolds under one representation. The understanding's only use of concepts is judgment: a concept relates to an object only mediately, by way of other representations. This anticipates the famous thesis: *all acts of the understanding are judgments*.

Judgment is therefore the *mediate cognition of an object* — hence the representation of a representation of it. In every judgment there is a concept that holds of many, and among this many comprises also a given representation, which then is referred immediately to the object. Thus in the judgment *all bodies are divisible*, the concept of the divisible refers to various other concepts; among these, however, it is here particularly referred to the concept of body; this however to certain appearances which come before us. These objects are therefore mediately represented through the concept of divisibility. All judgments are accordingly *functions of unity* among our representations, in that instead of an immediate representation a higher one, which comprises this and several others under itself, is used for the cognition of the object, and many possible cognitions are thereby drawn together in one. We can however reduce all acts of the understanding to judgments, so that the *understanding in general* can be represented as a *faculty of judging* (*Vermögen zu urteilen*). For it is, according to the above, a faculty of thinking. Thinking is cognition through concepts. Concepts, however, refer, as predicates of possible judgments, to some representation of an as yet indeterminate object. So the concept of *body* signifies something — e.g., metal — which can be cognized through that concept. It is therefore only thereby a concept, that under it other representations are contained, by means of which it can refer to objects. It is therefore the *predicate* of a possible judgment — e.g., *every metal is a body*. The functions of the understanding can therefore all be found if one can completely present the *functions of unity* in judgments. The crucial thesis: the *understanding is a faculty of judging* (*Vermögen zu urteilen*). Every act of the understanding is a judgment; every concept is a predicate of a possible judgment. The methodological consequence: to find all the functions of the understanding, find all the *logical forms of judgment*. This is the *Leitfaden* — the clue to the categories.

###### **Second Section: On the Logical Function of the Understanding in Judgments**

§ 9\. If we abstract from all content of a judgment in general and pay attention only to the mere *form of the understanding* in it, we find that the *function of thinking* in it can be brought under *four headings*, each of which contains *three moments* under itself. They can suitably be presented in the following table.

| 1\. Quantity of Judgments | 2\. Quality | 3\. Relation | 4\. Modality |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Universal | Affirmative | Categorical | Problematic |
| Particular | Negative | Hypothetical | Assertoric |
| Singular | Infinite | Disjunctive | Apodictic |

Since this division departs in some — though not essential — parts from the customary technique of the logicians, the following protective remarks against the apprehended misunderstanding will not be unnecessary.

The table of judgments is now presented. The fourfold articulation (quantity, quality, relation, modality), each with three moments (universal/particular/singular, affirmative/negative/infinite, categorical/hypothetical/disjunctive, problematic/assertoric/apodictic) is the structural basis from which the categories will be derived. The departures from Aristotelian/scholastic logic — specifically the inclusion of *singular* under quantity, *infinite* under quality, and the modal triad — are signaled in advance and will be defended.

1. The logicians say with right that in the use of judgments in syllogistic inferences, singular judgments can be treated like universal ones. For just because they have no extension at all, the predicate of them cannot be drawn merely on some of what is contained under the concept of the subject and excepted from some others. It therefore holds of that concept without exception, as if it were a common-valid concept which had an extension of whose whole meaning the predicate would hold. If, by contrast, we compare a singular judgment with a common-valid one merely as cognition with respect to magnitude, then it relates to this as *unity* to *infinity*, and is therefore in itself essentially distinguished from it. Therefore, if I appraise a singular judgment (*iudicium singulare*) not merely according to its inner validity, but also as cognition in general according to the magnitude that it has in comparison with other cognitions, it is indeed distinguished from common-valid judgments (*iudicia communia*) and deserves a special place in a complete table of the moments of thinking in general (though admittedly not in the logic restricted merely to the use of judgments among one another). The first defense: the singular judgment, while syllogistically equivalent to the universal, is *transcendentally* distinct from the universal because of its different relation to extension. This will justify its inclusion as a separate moment.

2. Likewise, in a transcendental logic, *infinite judgments* must be distinguished from *affirmative*, though in general logic they are with right counted with the latter and do not constitute a particular member of the division. The latter abstracts from all content of the predicate (although it is negating) and looks only to whether it is attributed to the subject or opposed to it. The former, however, considers the judgment also according to the *value* or *content* of this logical affirmation by means of a merely negating predicate, and what gain it provides with respect to the entire cognition. Had I said of the soul: *it is not mortal*, then I would have averted at least an error through a negative judgment. Now I have through the proposition: *the soul is non-mortal*, indeed affirmed according to the logical form, in that I place the soul in the unrestricted extension of non-mortal beings. Since now of the whole extension of possible beings the mortal contains one part, the non-mortal the other, nothing else is said through my proposition than that the soul is *one* of the infinite number of things that remain over when I take away the mortal in toto. Thereby, however, the infinite sphere of all the possible is only restricted so far that the mortal is separated from it, and in the remaining space of its extension the soul is set. But this space remains, with this exception, still infinite; and more parts of it can be taken away without thereby the concept of the soul growing in the least and being affirmatively determined. These infinite judgments, therefore, with respect to logical extension, are really merely *restricting* with respect to the content of cognition in general, and to that extent they must not be passed over in the transcendental table of all moments of thinking in judgments — because the function of the understanding exercised here can perhaps be important in the field of its pure a priori cognition. The second defense: the infinite judgment (*the soul is non-mortal*) is distinguished from the affirmative because, while logically affirmative in form, it has a *limiting* content — it places the subject in an unrestricted negative class. This will justify the category of *Limitation* under Quality.

3. All relations of thinking in judgments are: a) of the *predicate to the subject*; b) of the *ground to the consequence*; c) of the divided cognition and the assembled members of the division among one another. In the first kind of judgment, only two concepts are considered; in the second, two judgments; in the third, several judgments are considered in relation to one another. The hypothetical proposition: *if there is a perfect justice, then the persistently evil will be punished*, contains properly the relation of two propositions: *There is a perfect justice*, and *the persistently evil will be punished*. Whether both of these propositions are in themselves true remains undecided here. It is only the *consequence* that is thought through this judgment. Finally, the *disjunctive* judgment contains a relation of two or more propositions to one another, not however of consequence but of *logical opposition* — insofar as the sphere of one excludes that of the other, but yet at the same time of *community*, insofar as together they fill the sphere of proper cognition; hence a relation of the *parts of the sphere of a cognition*, in that the sphere of each part is a complementary piece of the sphere of the other to the whole of the *Inbegriff* of the divided cognition — e.g., *the world is there either through blind chance, or through inner necessity, or through an outer cause*. Each of these propositions takes a part of the sphere of possible cognition about the existence of a world in general; all together the whole sphere. To take away the cognition from one of these spheres means to place it in one of the remaining; and conversely to place it in one sphere means to take it away from the remaining ones. There is therefore in a disjunctive judgment a certain *community of cognitions*, which consists in this: that they reciprocally exclude one another, but yet thereby determine the true cognition *in the whole*, in that taken together they constitute the whole content of a single given cognition. The third defense: the three forms under Relation (categorical, hypothetical, disjunctive) correspond to three structurally distinct ways of relating: predicate to subject, ground to consequent, member to member within a divided whole. The hypothetical asserts only the *consequence-relation*, not the truth of either component; the disjunctive asserts a *community* of mutually exclusive members.

4. The *modality* of judgments is a quite peculiar function of them, which has this distinguishing feature: that it contributes nothing to the content of the judgment (for besides magnitude, quality, and relation there is nothing more that could constitute the content of a judgment), but rather concerns only the *value of the copula* in relation to thinking in general. *Problematic* judgments are those in which one takes the affirmation or negation as merely *possible* (optional). *Assertoric* judgments, where it is considered as *actual* (true). *Apodictic*, in which one regards it as *necessary*. (Footnote: *As if in the first case thinking were a function of the understanding, in the second of judgment, in the third of reason. A remark which awaits its clarification only in the sequel.*) The fourth defense: modality (problematic, assertoric, apodictic) is not a content-adding feature but a feature of the *copula* — the way the judgment relates to the act of thinking. The footnote is proleptic: the three moments of modality will correspond to the three higher cognitive faculties (understanding/judgment/reason), a parallelism that becomes important much later.

So the two judgments whose relation constitutes the hypothetical judgment (antecedent and consequent), likewise those in whose reciprocal action the disjunctive consists (members of the division), are altogether merely *problematic*. In the above example the proposition *there is a perfect justice* is not asserted assertorically, but only thought as an optional judgment of which it is possible that someone may assume it; only the *consequence* is assertoric. Therefore such judgments can also obviously be false, and yet, taken problematically, can be conditions of the cognition of the truth. So the judgment *the world is there through blind chance*, in the disjunctive judgment, is of only problematic significance — namely, that someone might assume this proposition perhaps for a moment — and it yet serves (like the marking of the false way among the number of all those one can take) to find the true. The *problematic proposition* is therefore that which expresses only *logical possibility* (which is not objective) — i.e., a free choice to let such a proposition hold, a merely *arbitrary acceptance* of it into the understanding. The *assertoric* says of *logical actuality* or truth — as, for example, in a hypothetical syllogism the antecedent occurs *problematically* in the major premise, *assertorically* in the minor premise, and shows that the proposition is already connected with the understanding according to its laws. The *apodictic* proposition thinks the assertoric as determined through these laws of the understanding themselves, and hence as asserting a priori, and so expresses *logical necessity*. The careful analysis of how problematic/assertoric/apodictic function in inference is given. The role of the antecedent in modus ponens is the paradigm: problematic in the major premise (*if P then Q*), assertoric in the minor premise (*P is the case*), and the conclusion is apodictic *given the law of inference*. Modality is therefore the *grade of integration* of the proposition into the understanding's law-governed thinking.

Since here everything is incorporated into the understanding by degrees — so that one first judges something problematically, then also assumes it assertorically as true, and finally asserts it as inseparably bound up with the understanding, i.e., as necessary and apodictic — one can call these three functions of modality also so many *moments of thinking in general*. The modal triad therefore comprises the three grades of cognitive commitment: hypothesis, assertion, demonstration. This concludes the deduction of the Table of Judgments and its defense against the objections of customary logic.

###### **Third Section: On the Pure Concepts of the Understanding, or Categories**

§ 10\. General logic, as has been said several times, abstracts from all content of cognition and expects that representations be given to it from elsewhere, wherever it may be, in order first to convert these into concepts, which takes place analytically. Transcendental logic, by contrast, has a *manifold of sensibility* lying before it a priori, which the transcendental aesthetic offers it, in order to give material to the pure concepts of the understanding — without which the latter would be without any content, hence wholly empty. Space and time contain a manifold of pure a priori intuition, but at the same time belong to the conditions of the receptivity of our mind under which alone it can receive representations of objects, and hence must always also affect the concept of them. But the *spontaneity* of our thinking requires that this manifold first be *gone through, taken up, and combined* in a certain way in order to make a cognition from it. This act I call *synthesis*. The transition from the Aesthetic to the Logic is now made structurally explicit. The Aesthetic provided the *manifold of pure intuition* (in space and time); the Logic now adds the *spontaneity of thinking* that *synthesizes* this manifold into cognition. The triad *durchgegangen, aufgenommen, verbunden* (gone through, taken up, combined) describes the three moments of synthesis — anticipating the threefold synthesis (apprehension, reproduction, recognition) of the A-edition Deduction. The act of unifying the manifold is *Synthesis* — one of the central concepts of the Critique.

I understand, however, by *synthesis* in the most general sense the act of *adding* different representations to one another and *comprehending* their manifoldness in *one* cognition. Such a synthesis is *pure* if the manifold is given not empirically but a priori (as is the manifold in space and time). Before all analysis of our representations, these must first be given, and no concepts can arise analytically *according to content*. The synthesis of a manifold, however (whether given empirically or a priori) first brings forth a cognition that may indeed initially still be raw and confused and so requires analysis; but synthesis is yet what properly *collects* the elements for cognitions and *unites* them in a certain content. It is therefore the first thing to which we must pay attention if we wish to judge of the original origin of our cognition. The methodological primacy of synthesis is now made explicit. Before any analysis of representations can occur, the representations themselves must first be synthesized (otherwise there would be nothing to analyze). Therefore synthesis is *the first*, the origin of all cognition. The A-edition Deduction will identify synthesis as the work of imagination (*Einbildungskraft*); the present passage anticipates this.

Synthesis in general, as we shall see in the future, is the mere effect of the *imagination* — a blind though indispensable function of the soul, without which we would have no cognition at all, but of which we are seldom even once conscious. To bring this synthesis to *concepts*, however — that is a function which belongs to the understanding, and through which it first procures for us cognition in the proper meaning. The famous characterization of imagination as a *blind but indispensable function* is here introduced. Imagination effects synthesis; understanding brings the synthesis to concepts. The cooperation of imagination and understanding is the work of unifying the sensible manifold into objective cognition.

*Pure synthesis, generally represented, gives the pure concept of the understanding.* I understand, however, by this synthesis that one which rests on a ground of synthetic unity a priori: so our *counting* (this is more noticeable especially in larger numbers) is a synthesis according to concepts, because it occurs according to a *common ground of unity* (e.g., that of the decimal system). Under this concept the unity in the synthesis of the manifold becomes *necessary*. The example of counting illustrates the doctrine: counting in a base (e.g., decimal) is a synthesis under a unifying concept (the base). Without such a concept, the synthesis would be merely random aggregation; with the concept, the synthesis acquires necessary unity.

*Analytically*, different representations are brought under *one* concept (a business of which general logic treats). *But to bring not the representations, but the pure synthesis of the representations under concepts — that is what transcendental logic teaches.* The first thing that must be given to us a priori for the cognition of all objects is the *manifold of pure intuition*; the *synthesis* of this manifold through the *imagination* is the second, but it still gives no cognition. The *concepts* which give *unity* to this pure synthesis, and consist solely in the representation of this necessary synthetic unity, do the third toward the cognition of an object that comes before us, and rest on the understanding. The three moments of cognition are now articulated explicitly:

* First: the *manifold of pure intuition* (Aesthetic).  
* Second: the *synthesis* of this manifold by the *imagination*.  
* Third: the *concepts* that give *unity* to this synthesis — the *categories*, which rest on the understanding.

*The same function* which gives unity to different representations *in a judgment* gives also unity to the mere synthesis of different representations *in an intuition*, which, expressed generally, is called the *pure concept of the understanding*. The same understanding, then — indeed, through the same actions through which it brought about the logical form of a judgment in concepts by means of the analytic unity — also, through the synthetic unity of the manifold in intuition in general, brings into its representations a *transcendental content*, on account of which they are called *pure concepts of the understanding* that relate a priori to objects — which general logic cannot accomplish. The crucial thesis is now stated: the same understanding effects both (i) the logical form of judgment (analytic unity of concepts) and (ii) the synthetic unity in intuition (the categories). The categories are therefore not separate from the logical forms of judgment but are the same act of understanding *applied to a manifold of intuition*. The *Metaphysical Deduction* — the derivation of the categories from the table of judgments — is here accomplished in principle.

In this way there arise just as many *pure concepts of the understanding* which relate a priori to objects of intuition in general as there were *logical functions in all possible judgments* in the previous table: for the understanding is completely *exhausted* through these functions, and its capacity is thereby entirely measured. We shall call these concepts, after Aristotle, *categories* — since our intent is originally one and the same with his, although it greatly departs from his in the execution. The methodological derivation of the categories is now complete: there is one category for each logical function of judgment, and the table of judgments is exhaustive, so the table of categories is exhaustive. The name *Kategorien* is retained from Aristotle, but the procedure is fundamentally different: Aristotle collected, Kant derives.

**Table of the Categories**

| 1\. Of Quantity | 2\. Of Quality | 3\. Of Relation | 4\. Of Modality |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Unity | Reality | Of Inherence and Subsistence (*substantia et accidens*) | Possibility — Impossibility |
| Plurality | Negation | Of Causality and Dependence (Cause and Effect) | Existence — Non-existence |
| Totality | Limitation | Of Community (Reciprocity between agent and patient) | Necessity — Contingency |

This is now the *inventory* (*Verzeichnung*) of all the originally pure concepts of synthesis which the understanding contains a priori in itself, and on account of which alone it is a pure understanding — in that it is only through them that it can understand anything in the manifold of intuition, i.e., think an object of it. This division is systematically generated from a common principle — namely, the *faculty of judging* (which is just as much as the faculty of thinking) — and has not arisen *rhapsodistically* from a haphazard search for pure concepts, of whose completeness one can never be certain since it is concluded only by induction, without thinking that one then never sees why precisely these and no other concepts inhabit the pure understanding. The methodological superiority of Kant's procedure over Aristotle's is now claimed: the categories are derived from a *gemeinschaftliches Prinzip* (common principle), namely the *Vermögen zu urteilen*. This guarantees their systematic completeness — something Aristotle's "rhapsodistic" collection could never guarantee.

It was a project worthy of a sharp-witted man, Kant continues, this aim of *Aristotle* of seeking out these fundamental concepts. But since he had no *principium*, he gathered them up as he stumbled upon them, and at first drove ten of them — which he called *categories* (*predicaments*). Afterwards he believed he had found another five, which he added under the name of *post-predicaments*. But his table still remained defective. Besides, there are also found among them some *modi of pure sensibility* (*quando, ubi, situs*, likewise *prius, simul*), as well as one empirical (*motus*), which do not at all belong in this stem-register of the understanding; or also the derivative concepts are counted in with the original (*actio, passio*), and some of the latter are wholly lacking. The historical critique of Aristotle is now made specific. (i) Aristotle had no principle, so his list was haphazard; (ii) his list mixed temporal-spatial determinations (which belong to sensibility, not understanding) with categories proper; (iii) his list mixed derivative concepts (*actio, passio* — which Kant takes to be derivable) with original concepts; (iv) some original concepts were missing. The Kantian table is principled, pure (free of sensibility), original (free of derived concepts), and complete (exhaustive).

For the sake of the latter it is therefore still to be remarked: that the categories, as the *true stem-concepts* (*Stammbegriffe*) of the pure understanding, also have their just as pure *derivative concepts* (*abgeleitete Begriffe*) which can on no account be passed over in a complete system of transcendental philosophy — but with whose mere mention I can content myself in a merely critical attempt. Let me be permitted to call these pure but derivative concepts of the understanding the *predicabilia* of the pure understanding (in contrast to the predicaments). When one has the original and primitive concepts, the derivative and subaltern ones can easily be added, and the *Stammbaum* (genealogical tree, family tree) of the pure understanding can be completely painted. Since I am here concerned not with the completeness of the system but only with the *principles* of a system, I reserve this supplementation for another occasion. The distinction *Prädikamente* (predicaments — the original categories) vs. *Prädikabilien* (predicables — derived concepts) is now made. The Critique will give the predicaments; the predicables can be derived from them and added later. The botanical metaphor — *Stammbaum* (genealogical tree) — reinforces the systematic ambition: the categories are roots, the predicables grow from them.

One can however reach this aim fairly well if one takes the ontological textbooks at hand and, e.g., under the category of *causality* subordinates the predicables of *force, action, suffering*; under that of *community* those of *presence, resistance*; under the predicaments of modality those of *origination, perishing, alteration*, etc. The categories combined with the modes of pure sensibility, or also with one another, give a great quantity of derived concepts a priori, to remark which, and where possible to bring to completeness in inventorying, would be a useful and not unpleasant — but here dispensable — endeavor. The examples of derivation illustrate the procedure: causality \+ appropriate modifications \= force, action, passion; community \+ modifications \= presence, resistance; modality \+ temporal modifications (which involve sensibility) \= origination, perishing, alteration. The Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science will carry this work further, deriving the specifically Newtonian-physical concepts from the categories.

I deliberately spare myself the definitions of these categories in this treatise, although I should be in possession of them. I shall analyze these concepts in the sequel up to the degree that is sufficient with respect to the methodology I am working on. In a system of pure reason one would justly demand them from me; but here they would only bring the chief point of the investigation out of view by raising doubts and attacks, which one can well refer to another occupation without thereby withdrawing anything from the essential intent. The methodological reservation of definitions to a later point is significant: definitions presuppose the full unfolding of the system, which the Critique provides only in its principles. The Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science (1786) and the Critique of the Power of Judgment (1790) will partly supply some of these definitions, but the full system of derived concepts is never fully completed in Kant's published work.

§ 11\. (B-edition addition.) Over this table of the categories, fine considerations can be made that might perhaps have important consequences with respect to the scientific form of all cognitions of reason. For that this table is uncommonly serviceable — indeed indispensable — in the theoretical part of philosophy in order to draw up *the plan to the whole of a science* (insofar as it rests on a priori concepts) and to *divide* it mathematically *according to determinate principles*, is already evident from the fact that the said table contains all the elementary concepts of the understanding completely — indeed even the form of a *system* of them in the human understanding — and consequently gives an indication of all the *moments* of a proposed speculative science, indeed of its *order*, as I have also given a sample of this elsewhere (footnote: *Metaphysical Foundations of Natural Science*). Here are now some of these remarks. The B-edition adds §§11-12 to develop further structural observations on the table. The reference to the *Metaphysical Foundations* (1786) — which had appeared between the two editions of the Critique — shows that the table of categories has been put to use in actual scientific articulation.

The first is: that this table, which contains four classes of concepts of the understanding, can first be divided into two divisions, the first of which is directed at *objects of intuition* (both pure and empirical), the second however at the *existence of these objects* (either in relation to one another or to the understanding). The first class I would call the *mathematical*, the second the *dynamical* categories. The first class has, as one sees, *no correlates*, which are encountered only in the second class. This difference must yet have a ground in the nature of the understanding. The methodological division: quantity and quality (mathematical categories) relate to objects of intuition; relation and modality (dynamical categories) relate to objects' existence and relations. The mathematical categories have no correlates (unity, plurality, totality are not in correlate-pairs); the dynamical do (substance-accident, cause-effect, agent-patient; possibility-impossibility, existence-nonexistence, necessity-contingency). This structural feature is significant for the application of the categories: the mathematical apply to objects directly, the dynamical involve relations of existence (this is why the Analogies of Experience — which apply the relational categories — will be regulative rather than constitutive principles in the same sense as the Axioms of Intuition and Anticipations of Perception).

Second remark. That throughout an equal number of categories — namely three — is found in each class, which also calls for reflection, since all a priori division through concepts must be dichotomous. To this, however, is added: that *the third category in each class always arises from the connection of the second with the first of its class*. So *totality* (allness) is nothing other than *plurality* considered as *unity*; *limitation* nothing other than *reality* combined with *negation*; *community* is *causality* of a substance in determining the other reciprocally; finally *necessity* nothing other than *existence* which is given through *possibility itself*. One should not however think that for this reason the third category is a merely derived and not a *stem-concept* of the pure understanding. For the *connection* of the first and second to produce the third concept requires a *special act of the understanding* which is not the same as that exercised in the first and second. So the concept of a *number* (which belongs to the category of allness) is not always possible where the concepts of plurality and unity are (e.g., in the representation of the *infinite*); or from my combining both the concept of a cause and that of a substance, the *influence* — i.e., how a substance can become the cause of something in another substance — is not yet to be understood. Hence it is clear that a special act of the understanding is required for this; and so with the rest. The triadic structure of the categories is now observed: each triad has the third member as a unification of the first two. *Totalität* \= *Vielheit* as *Einheit*; *Einschränkung* \= *Realität* \+ *Negation*; *Gemeinschaft* \= reciprocal *Kausalität*; *Notwendigkeit* \= *Existenz* given through *Möglichkeit*. Each third category, however, requires a special act of understanding and is therefore an original stem-concept, not a mere derivation. The triadic structure will resurface in Hegelian dialectic, where it is foundational; but Kant's appeal to it here is structural, not dialectical.

Third remark. Of a single category — namely that of *community*, which is found under the third heading — the agreement with the form of a *disjunctive judgment* corresponding to it in the table of logical functions is not so obvious as in the rest. To assure ourselves of this agreement, we must note: that in all disjunctive judgments the *sphere* (the quantity of everything contained under it) is represented as a *whole* divided into parts (the subordinate concepts), and, since one cannot be contained under the other, they are thought as *coordinated* — not *subordinated* — to one another, so that they determine one another not unilaterally as in a series but reciprocally as in an aggregate (if one member of the division is posited, all the rest are excluded, and conversely). Now a similar connection is thought in a *whole of things*, where one is not subordinated as effect to the other as cause of its existence, but is *simultaneously and reciprocally coordinated* as cause with respect to the determination of the others (e.g., in a body whose parts reciprocally attract and resist each other); which is an entirely different kind of connection than is found in the mere relation of cause to effect (of ground to consequence), in which the consequence does not reciprocally in turn determine the ground and therefore does not constitute with this (as the world-creator with the world) a *whole*. The same procedure of the understanding when it represents to itself the sphere of a divided concept it also observes when it thinks a thing as *divisible*; and as the members of the division in the former exclude one another and yet are connected in one sphere, so it represents to itself the parts of the latter as such whose existence (as substances) belongs to each exclusively from the others, but yet as connected in *one whole*. The structural correspondence between disjunctive judgments and community is now made explicit. Disjunctive judgment: *the world is there either by chance, or by necessity, or by external cause* — the three members are coordinate, exclusive, jointly exhaustive. Community of substances: each substance is independent (substance-status excludes the others from being its substance), yet they form a whole through reciprocal action. The structural form — coordination, mutual exclusion, joint constitution of a whole — is the same in both cases.

§ 12\. There is yet found, in the transcendental philosophy of the ancients, a chapter that contains pure concepts of the understanding which, though they are not counted among the categories, were yet to count as a priori concepts of objects — in which case, however, they would increase the number of the categories, which cannot be. This is represented in the proposition, so renowned among the scholastics: *quodlibet ens est unum, verum, bonum* (every being is one, true, and good). Although the use of this principle with respect to the consequences (which gave merely tautological propositions) turned out very meagre, so that in more recent times one is accustomed almost only for honor's sake to set it up in metaphysics, a thought that has maintained itself for so long, however empty it may seem, yet always deserves an investigation into its origin and authorizes the supposition that it must have its ground in some rule of the understanding which has only, as often happens, been falsely interpreted. The scholastic *unum, verum, bonum* triad is taken up as an apparent supplement to the categories. The diagnostic question: is this triad a genuine set of additional categories? Kant's answer: no, but it has a genuine origin in misinterpreted logical requirements.

These supposedly transcendental predicates of *things* are nothing other than *logical requirements and criteria of all cognition of things in general*, and put the categories of *quantity* — namely *unity, plurality, allness* — at the ground; only that they would have to take these, which would properly be taken *materially* as belonging to the possibility of things themselves, in fact only in formal meaning as belonging to the *logical requirement with respect to every cognition*, and yet incautiously made these criteria of thinking into properties of things in themselves. In every cognition of an object there is namely *unity of the concept*, which one can call *qualitative unity* insofar as one thinks under it only the unity of the comprehension of the manifold of cognitions — as for example the unity of the theme in a play, a speech, a fable. Secondly *truth* in regard to the consequences. The more true consequences from a given concept, the more marks of its objective reality. This one could call the *qualitative plurality* of the marks that belong to a concept as a common ground (not thought in it as magnitude). Finally thirdly *perfection*, which consists in this: that conversely this plurality together leads back to the unity of the concept, and completely agrees with this and no other — which one can call the *qualitative completeness* (totality). The diagnostic reinterpretation: the scholastic *unum, verum, bonum* are not categories of things but *logical criteria* — namely, the criteria for the unity, fruitfulness, and completeness of a *cognition*. They are the *quantitative categories applied formally* (to the structure of cognition) rather than materially (to objects). The misinterpretation of the scholastics was to take them ontologically rather than methodologically.

From which it is evident that these logical criteria of the possibility of cognition in general here transform the three categories of magnitude — in which the unity in the production of the *quantum* must be assumed throughout as homogeneous — only with respect to the *connection of unlike pieces of cognition* in *one consciousness*, through the quality of a cognition as principle. So the criterion of the *possibility of a concept* (not of the object of it) is the *definition*, in which the *unity* of the concept, the *truth* of all that may first be derived from it, finally the *completeness* of what is drawn from it, constitutes the requisite for the production of the whole concept. So also is the *criterion of a hypothesis* the *intelligibility* of the assumed *ground of explanation*, or its *unity* (without auxiliary hypothesis), the *truth* (agreement with itself and with experience) of the consequences to be derived from it, and finally the *completeness* of the ground of explanation for these — which point back to nothing more nor less than was assumed in the hypothesis, and analytically restore a posteriori what was thought a priori synthetically and agree with this. — Therefore, through the concepts of *unity, truth, and perfection*, the transcendental table of categories is not — as if it were defective — supplemented, but only, in that the relation of these concepts to objects is set aside entirely, the procedure with them is brought under universal logical rules of the agreement of cognition with itself. The methodological re-interpretation of the scholastic triad is now complete: *unum, verum, bonum* are not categories of *objects* but methodological criteria for *cognition*. The categories of quantity (unity, plurality, totality), applied to the *qualitative* structure of cognition itself (rather than to the *quantitative* structure of objects), yield the methodological maxims: cognition must be unified, fruitful, and complete (e.g., a definition must have one concept, generate true consequences, and exhaustively determine its object; a hypothesis must have one explanatory ground, agree with itself and with experience, and explain its data fully). The triad is therefore not a supplement to the table of categories but a methodological corollary of it.

##### **Second Chapter: On the Deduction of the Pure Concepts of the Understanding**

###### **First Section: On the Principles of a Transcendental Deduction in General**

§ 13\. *Jurists*, Kant begins, when they speak of *rights and claims* (*Befugnisse und Anmaßungen*), distinguish in a legal proceeding the question of *what is right* (*quid iuris*) from that which concerns the *fact* (*quid facti*); and in demanding proof of both, they call the first — which is to set forth the right or also the legal claim — the *deduction*. We make use of a multitude of empirical concepts without anyone's contradiction and consider ourselves entitled to attribute to them, even without deduction, a sense and imagined signification, because we always have experience at hand to prove their objective reality. There are also, however, *usurped* concepts — such as *luck, fate* — which, though they roam about with almost universal indulgence, are yet sometimes called into question by the question *quid iuris*; in which case one falls into no small embarrassment regarding the deduction of them, since one can adduce no clear legal ground (*Rechtsgrund*) either from experience or from reason whereby the entitlement (*Befugnis*) of their use would become clear. The juridical analogy is now opened. A *Deduktion* in the jurists' sense is the justification of a right or claim — a setting-forth of the legal ground. Applied to concepts: a transcendental deduction is the justification of the *legitimacy* of using certain concepts. Empirical concepts (red, dog) usually need no deduction (experience supplies their justification); but *usurped* concepts (luck, fate — and, by analogy, the categories used dogmatically beyond their proper range) require deduction.

Among the various concepts that constitute the very mixed fabric of human cognition, however, there are some which are also determined for *pure a priori use* (entirely independent of all experience), and the entitlement of these always requires a *deduction*; because for the legitimacy of such a use, *proofs from experience are not sufficient*, but one must yet know how these concepts can refer to objects which they do not borrow from any experience. I therefore call the *explanation of the manner in which concepts a priori can refer to objects* the *transcendental deduction* of them, and distinguish it from the *empirical deduction*, which shows the manner in which a concept is acquired through experience and reflection upon it, and which therefore does not concern the *legitimacy* but the *factum* through which the possession originated. The definition of *transzendentale Deduktion* is now precise: the *explanation of how a priori concepts can refer to objects*. This is to be distinguished from *empirische Deduktion*, which is a genetic-psychological account of concept-formation (the kind of account Locke provided). The transcendental deduction is normative (justifying the right of use), not descriptive (telling the history of acquisition).

We already have two kinds of concepts of an entirely different sort, Kant continues, which yet agree in this — that both refer to objects entirely a priori: namely, the concepts of *space and time*, as forms of sensibility, and the *categories*, as concepts of the understanding. To attempt an *empirical deduction* of them would be entirely vain labor; for precisely in this lies what is distinguishing in their nature: that they refer to their objects without having borrowed anything for their representation from experience. If a deduction of them is therefore necessary, it will always have to be *transcendental*. Both kinds of a priori concepts — the forms of sensibility (deduced in the Aesthetic) and the categories (to be deduced in the Logic) — require transcendental, not empirical, deduction. The Aesthetic has already accomplished one such deduction; the Analytic now undertakes the other.

In the meantime, one can with these concepts — as with all cognition — seek not the *principium* of their possibility but the *occasioning causes* of their generation in experience, where then the impressions of the senses give the first occasion to open up the entire cognitive power with respect to them and to bring about experience — which contains two very heterogeneous elements: namely, a *matter* for cognition from the senses, and a certain *form* for ordering it, from the *inner source* of pure intuition and thinking, which, on the occasion of the former, are first brought into exercise and produce concepts. Such a tracing of the first endeavors of our cognitive power — to ascend from particular perceptions to universal concepts — has without doubt its great utility, and one has the famous *Locke* to thank for first opening up the way to it. But a *deduction* of the pure a priori concepts never comes about this way; for it does not at all lie on this path, because with respect to their future use — which shall be entirely independent of experience — they must show an entirely different birth-certificate than that of descent from experiences. The historical-genealogical role of Locke is acknowledged: Locke's empirical deduction of concepts illuminated the *occasioning causes* (*Gelegenheitsursachen*) of concept-formation, but it cannot accomplish a *transcendental deduction*. Empirical genetic origins do not justify a priori use. The *Geburtsbrief* (birth certificate) of a priori concepts must be something other than empirical descent — it must be the structural necessity of the categories for any cognition of objects whatever, as the Transcendental Deduction will undertake to show.

This attempted *physiological derivation*, Kant continues — which cannot properly be called *deduction* at all, because it concerns a *quaestio facti* — I shall therefore call the *explanation of the possession* of a pure cognition. It is therefore clear that of these alone can there be a transcendental deduction, and by no means an empirical one; and that the latter, in respect of the pure a priori concepts, is nothing but vain attempts, with which only he can occupy himself who has not grasped the wholly peculiar nature of these cognitions. The terminological refinement makes the distinction sharper: an empirical derivation in Locke's manner is no *Deduktion* in the strict (juridical) sense at all — it is at best an *Erklärung des Besitzes* (explanation of possession), the historical account of how we came to have a concept. A *Deduktion* in the strict sense justifies the *right* of use. The diagnostic point against Locke: empirical derivation cannot do this work, because the categories' future use is independent of experience.

But although the sole kind of possible deduction of the pure a priori cognition — namely, that on the transcendental path — is now granted, it does not yet appear from this that such a deduction is so indispensably necessary. We have above pursued the concepts of space and time to their sources by means of a *transcendental deduction* and explained and determined their objective validity a priori. Nevertheless, geometry goes its sure step through nothing but a priori cognitions without having to request a certificate of authenticity from philosophy regarding the pure and lawful descent of its fundamental concept of space. But the use of the concept in this science is also directed only to the outer sense-world, of which space is the pure form of intuition — in which therefore all geometric cognition, because it is grounded on a priori intuition, has *immediate evidence*, and the objects are given a priori (as regards form) in intuition through the cognition itself. The asymmetry between space and the categories is now diagnosed. Geometry does not require explicit transcendental deduction of space for its own practical operation, because the use of space is restricted to the outer sense-world and the synthetic a priori cognitions are given immediate evidence through intuition itself. The geometer can work without philosophy's certification.

By contrast, with the *pure concepts of the understanding* begins the unavoidable need to seek the transcendental deduction not only of them themselves but also of space; because — since they speak of objects not through predicates of intuition and sensibility but of pure thinking a priori — they refer to objects universally without all conditions of sensibility. And since they are not grounded on experience, they also cannot in a priori intuition show any object on which they could ground their synthesis before all experience. They therefore not only raise suspicion regarding the objective validity and limits of their use, but also make ambiguous the *concept of space* by tending to use it beyond the conditions of sensible intuition — wherefore a transcendental deduction of it was needed above as well. The reader must therefore be convinced of the unavoidable necessity of such a transcendental deduction before he has taken a single step in the field of pure reason; for otherwise he proceeds blindly and, after having strayed manifoldly about, must yet return to the ignorance from which he started. He must, however, also clearly grasp in advance the unavoidable difficulty, so that he does not complain of obscurity where the matter itself is deeply enveloped, or grow weary too early of the removal of the obstacles — because it comes to this: either to give up entirely all claims to insights of pure reason as the most beloved field (namely, that beyond the limits of all possible experience), or to bring this critical investigation to perfection. The motivational pre-statement of the difficulty is significant: the deduction will be hard work, both philosophically and rhetorically. The reader is warned not to mistake genuine depth for mere obscurity, and not to lose patience at the removal of obstacles. The stakes are stated as a stark disjunction: either renounce the metaphysical ambitions of pure reason entirely, or complete the critical work.

We have been able above, with the concepts of space and time, with little trouble to make comprehensible how these as a priori cognitions must yet necessarily refer to objects, and made a synthetic cognition of them, independent of all experience, possible. For since only by means of such pure forms of sensibility can an object *appear* to us — i.e., be an object of empirical intuition — space and time are pure intuitions which contain a priori the *condition of the possibility of objects as appearances*, and the synthesis in them has objective validity. The categories of the understanding, by contrast, do not represent to us at all the conditions under which objects are given in intuition; hence objects can indeed appear to us without their necessarily having to refer to functions of the understanding, and the latter therefore containing the conditions of them a priori. Hence there appears here a difficulty that we did not meet with in the field of sensibility — namely, how *subjective conditions of thinking* should have *objective validity*, i.e., supply conditions of the possibility of all cognition of objects: for without functions of the understanding appearances can indeed be given in intuition. I take, e.g., the concept of *cause*, which signifies a particular kind of synthesis in that, after something A, something quite different B is posited according to a rule. It is not clear a priori why appearances should contain anything like this (for experiences cannot be adduced for the proof, since the objective validity of this concept a priori must be capable of being shown), and it is therefore a priori doubtful whether such a concept is not perhaps even *empty* and meets with no object anywhere among the appearances. The central difficulty of the Deduction is now sharply stated. For space and time, the demonstration of necessity was straightforward: space and time *are* the forms under which objects can appear, hence appearances necessarily fall under them. But the categories are not forms of *receptivity*; they are functions of *thinking*. It is not immediately clear why appearances — which are given to us in intuition independently of thinking — should necessarily fall under the categories. Could not appearances be given to us in such disarray that no rule of synthesis (e.g., the rule of causality) applied? If so, the categories would be empty (without objects) and the concept of cause a *Hirngespinst* — a figment.

For that objects of sensible intuition must conform to the formal conditions of sensibility which lie in the mind a priori is clear from the fact that otherwise they would not be objects for us; but that they must over and above conform to the conditions of which the understanding has need for the synthetic unity of thinking — of this the conclusion is not so easy to see. For appearances could in any case be so constituted that the understanding would not find them at all conformable to the conditions of its unity, and everything would lie in such confusion that, for example, nothing would offer itself in the sequence of appearances that would provide a *rule of synthesis*, and hence correspond to the concept of cause and effect; so that this concept would be wholly empty, null, and without significance. Appearances would nevertheless offer objects to our intuition, for intuition does not require the functions of thinking in any way. The hypothetical of chaotic appearance — a flux that yields no rule, where causality finds no purchase — is the threat the Deduction must answer. The crucial point: intuition does not require thinking; objects could appear without falling under any unified rule. Without further argument, this is a real possibility — and the categories would be empty.

If one thought to extricate oneself from the difficulty of these investigations by saying: *experience* incessantly offers examples of such regularity of appearances, sufficient to abstract the concept of *cause* from them and thereby at the same time to attest the objective validity of such a concept — then one does not notice that in this way the concept of cause cannot arise at all; rather, that it must either be wholly a priori grounded in the understanding or be wholly given up as a mere phantom of the brain. For this concept demands absolutely that something A be of such a kind that something else B follows from it *necessarily and according to an absolutely universal rule*. Appearances do indeed provide cases from which a rule is possible — according to which something happens in the customary way — but never that the consequence is *necessary*: hence to the synthesis of cause and effect there attaches also a *dignity* which one cannot at all empirically express, namely, that the effect not merely *be added* to the cause but be *posited* through it and follow *from* it. The strict universality of the rule is also no property of empirical rules, which through induction can acquire no other than *comparative universality* — i.e., extended applicability. Now the use of the pure concepts of the understanding would change entirely if one wished to treat them only as empirical products. The reductio against the Lockean attempt to derive causality from experience is now made: experience can yield only comparative universality (induction), not strict universality; experience can yield only customary sequence, not necessary consequence; therefore the proper concept of cause — with its strict necessity — cannot be derived from experience. Either the concept is a priori or it is a phantasm. The disjunction sets up the Transcendental Deduction's task.

#### **§ 14\. Transition to the Transcendental Deduction of the Categories**

There are only two cases possible, Kant continues, in which synthetic representation and its objects can come together, refer to one another necessarily, and as it were *meet* one another: either when the *object alone makes the representation possible*, or when the *representation alone makes the object possible*. In the first case, this relation is only empirical, and the representation is never possible a priori. And this is the case with appearance in respect of what in it belongs to sensation. If, however, it is the second case — because representation in itself (for of its causality by means of the will there is here no question at all) does not bring forth its object *with respect to existence* — yet the representation is in respect of the object a priori determining, if through it alone it is possible *to cognize something as an object*. The two-case disjunction is the methodological cornerstone of the Deduction. Synthetic a priori cognition of objects requires a necessary connection between representation and object. The connection can run only two ways: object → representation (the empirical case, where the object determines what we represent) or representation → object (the a priori case, where the representation determines what is to count as an object). The exclusion of an alternative is exhaustive: where there is no causal-volitional production (we are not gods who think objects into existence), the only way representations determine objects is by determining *what counts as an object* — i.e., by making the objectivity of objects possible.

There are, however, two conditions under which alone the cognition of an object is possible: first, *intuition*, by which the object is given (but only as appearance); second, *concept*, by which an object is thought that corresponds to this intuition. But it is clear from the above that the first condition — namely, that under which alone objects can be intuited — in fact lies a priori in the mind as ground of the objects according to their form. With this formal condition of sensibility, then, all appearances necessarily agree, because only through it can they appear — i.e., be empirically intuited and given. Now it is asked whether a priori *concepts* also do not precede, as conditions under which alone something — though not intuited — is yet thought *as an object in general*. For then all empirical cognition of objects is necessarily conformable to such concepts, because without their presupposition nothing is possible *as object of experience*. Now all experience contains, besides the *intuition* of the senses by which something is given, also a *concept* of an object that is given in intuition or appears: accordingly, concepts of objects in general will lie at the ground as a priori conditions of all experiential cognition. Consequently, the objective validity of the categories as a priori concepts will rest on this: that through them alone is *experience* possible (with respect to the form of thinking). For then they refer necessarily and a priori to objects of experience, since only by means of them can any object of experience whatsoever be thought. The argument is now the central one of the entire Critique. Cognition of an object requires *both* intuition (givenness) *and* concept (thinking-as-object). Sensibility supplies the form for intuition (proved in the Aesthetic). Now the question is: does the understanding similarly supply forms — concepts — under which objects must be thought? If yes, then these concepts (the categories) are a priori conditions of the possibility of experience itself; hence their objective validity is *necessary* for any possible object of experience. The structure of the Deduction is therefore: the categories are valid of all objects of experience because they are conditions of the very possibility of experience.

The transcendental deduction of all a priori concepts therefore has a *principium* upon which the whole inquiry must be directed: namely, this — that they must be cognized as a priori conditions of the possibility of experience (whether of the intuition that is encountered in it or of the thinking). Concepts that supply the *objective ground* of the possibility of experience are for that very reason necessary. The development of the experience in which they are encountered, however, is not their *deduction* (but *illustration*), because in this they would yet be only contingent. Without this original reference to possible experience, in which all objects of cognition come, the reference of them to any object whatever could not be conceived at all. The methodological principle is stated: an a priori concept is justified iff it is a *condition of the possibility of experience*. This is the criterion that distinguishes a genuine category from a mere fiction. The mention of *Entwicklung der Erfahrung* (development of experience) as *illustration* rather than deduction is significant: showing the category at work in experience exhibits, but does not justify, its use. The Deduction must show that the category is *necessary* for experience to be experience at all.

The famous *Locke*, Kant continues, for lack of this consideration and because he encountered pure concepts of the understanding in experience, also derived them from experience, and yet proceeded so *inconsistently* that with them he ventured upon attempts at cognitions which go far beyond all bounds of experience. *David Hume* recognized that, in order to do the latter, it was necessary that these concepts must have their origin a priori. But since he could not at all explain to himself how it is possible that the understanding must think concepts — which in themselves are not connected in the understanding — yet as necessarily connected in the object, and did not fall upon the idea that perhaps the understanding through these concepts itself might be the author of the experience in which its objects are encountered — so he derived them, driven by necessity, from experience (namely, from a subjective necessity arising through frequent association in experience, which is at last falsely held to be objective — i.e., from *habit*). But afterwards he proceeded very consistently in declaring it impossible to go beyond the bounds of experience with these concepts and the principles which they occasion. The *empirical derivation*, however, into which both fell, cannot be united with the actuality of the scientific a priori cognitions that we have — namely, of *pure mathematics* and *general natural science* — and is therefore refuted by the *factum*. The historical diagnosis is now precise. Locke recognized the empirical aspect but inconsistently used the concepts beyond experience (e.g., for substance, God). Hume saw the inconsistency in Locke and concluded — *consistently* — that the empirical concepts cannot legitimately be used beyond experience; hence he reduced them to *Gewohnheit* (habit). Both nevertheless made the same mistake: empirical derivation. The *factum* of pure mathematics and general natural science (synthetic a priori sciences) refutes this empirical derivation. Hume's near-discovery — that the concepts would need a priori status to be used beyond experience — was missed because he could not envision the understanding as *Urheber der Erfahrung* (author of experience). This is the Copernican insight that the Critique aims to articulate.

The first of these two famous men opened the door wide to *enthusiasm* (*Schwärmerei*), Kant continues, because reason, once it has entitlements on its side, no longer lets itself be held within limits by indeterminate recommendations of moderation; the second gave himself entirely over to *skepticism*, since he believed he had discovered such a universal delusion held to be reason in our cognitive faculty. — We are now in the position to attempt whether one cannot bring human reason successfully through between these two cliffs, assign it determinate limits, and yet keep the whole field of its purposive activity open for it. The diagnostic poses the alternative: *Schwärmerei* (the dogmatic excess of unrestricted speculation) on the Lockean side, *Skeptizismus* (the empiricist collapse) on the Humean side. The Critique navigates between these two cliffs: it sets *determinate limits* to reason (against *Schwärmerei*) while preserving the *zweckmäßige Tätigkeit* (purposive activity) of reason within its proper field (against *Skeptizismus*).

Beforehand I will only send in advance the *explanation of the categories*. They are concepts of an object in general, by which the intuition of it is regarded as *determined* with respect to one of the *logical functions* of judgments. So the function of the *categorical judgment* was that of the relation of subject to predicate — e.g., *all bodies are divisible*. But in respect of the merely logical use of the understanding it remained undetermined which of the two concepts one wished to give the function of subject and which the function of predicate. For one can also say: *some divisible is a body*. By the category of *substance*, however, if I bring the concept of body under it, it is *determined*: that its empirical intuition in experience must always be regarded only as subject, never as mere predicate. And so in all the remaining categories. The definitional summary of *Kategorien* is now stated: *concepts of an object in general by which intuition is determined with respect to one of the logical functions of judgments*. The example is illuminating. Logically, in the judgment *all bodies are divisible*, the subject-position is interchangeable with the predicate-position by conversion (*some divisible things are bodies*). But the *category of substance* fixes the *body* in the subject-position: in any possible experience, body must be the *subject* of predicates, never itself a predicate of something else. The category therefore *determines* (in respect to objects) what logic leaves *undetermined* (in respect to mere subject-predicate relations).

#### **Second Section \[B-edition\]: Transcendental Deduction of the Pure Concepts of the Understanding**

##### **§ 15\. On the Possibility of a Combination in General**

The manifold of representations can be given in an intuition that is merely sensible — i.e., nothing but receptivity — and the form of this intuition can lie a priori in our faculty of representation, without being yet anything other than the manner in which the subject is affected. But the *combination* (*coniunctio*) of a manifold in general can never come to us through the senses, and cannot therefore also be contained at once in the pure form of sensible intuition; for it is an act of the spontaneity of the faculty of representation — and since one must call this — to distinguish it from sensibility — *understanding*: all combination, whether we are conscious of it or not, whether it is a combination of the manifold of intuition or of several concepts, and in the former case whether of sensible or non-sensible intuition, is an *act of the understanding* which we would designate with the general name *synthesis*, in order thereby to make remarkable at the same time that we can represent nothing as combined *in the object* without ourselves having previously combined it; and that among all representations *combination* is the only one which cannot be given through objects but can only be performed by the subject itself, because it is an act of its self-activity. One readily becomes aware here that this act must originally be one and must hold equally for all combination, and that the *dissolution* (analysis), which seems to be its opposite, yet always presupposes it; for where the understanding has not previously combined nothing, there it can also dissolve nothing, because it can only have been given to the faculty of representation as combined *by it*. The B-edition Deduction opens with the most important claim of the entire Analytic. *Combination* (*Verbindung*) — the binding-together of any manifold into a unity — is not a feature given by the senses. The senses are passive, receptive; they can supply manifold content but not its unification. Combination is therefore an *Actus der Spontaneität* — an act of the active faculty, the understanding. This thesis is foundational: anything that appears combined in an object has been combined *by the subject*. The corollary: analysis (decomposition) presupposes synthesis (composition); we cannot decompose what we have not first put together. The methodological consequence is that synthesis is the primary act, prior to analysis.

But the concept of combination brings with it — besides the concept of the manifold and the synthesis of it — yet also the concept of the *unity* of it. *Combination is the representation of the synthetic unity of the manifold*. The representation of this unity, then, cannot arise from the combination; rather, it makes — by being added to the representation of the manifold — the concept of combination first possible. This unity, which precedes a priori all concepts of combination, is not perhaps that *category of unity* (§10); for all categories are grounded on logical functions in judgments, but in the latter combination — hence unity of given concepts — is already thought. The category therefore already presupposes combination. We must therefore seek this unity (as qualitative, §12) yet *higher* — namely, in that which itself contains the *ground of the unity* of different concepts in judgments, hence of the possibility of the understanding even in its logical use. The footnote: whether the representations themselves are identical, and so one could be thought analytically through the other, does not come into consideration here. The *consciousness* of the one, insofar as the manifold is in question, is always to be distinguished from the consciousness of the other; and it is on the synthesis of this (possible) consciousness alone that everything depends here. The argument refines the analysis. *Combination* contains three moments: (i) the manifold; (ii) the synthesis of it; (iii) the *unity* that makes the synthesis a unification. The unity is not itself part of the synthesis but is what makes synthesis-as-unification possible. Importantly, this *Einheit* is not the category of unity (which arises from logical functions of judgment and is itself dependent on combination), but a *higher* unity — the original ground of the possibility of combination itself. This higher unity will be identified in §16 as the original synthetic unity of apperception.

##### **§ 16\. On the Originally Synthetic Unity of Apperception**

*The "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations*; for otherwise something would be represented in me that could not be thought at all — which is as much as to say that the representation would either be impossible, or at least nothing for me. That representation which can be given prior to all thinking is called *intuition*. All the manifold of intuition therefore has a necessary reference to the *I think* in the same subject in which this manifold is encountered. But this representation is an *act of spontaneity* — i.e., it cannot be regarded as belonging to sensibility. I call it *pure apperception*, in order to distinguish it from *empirical* apperception; or also *original apperception*, because it is that self-consciousness which, by bringing forth the representation *I think* (which must be able to accompany all others, and is one and the same in all consciousness), cannot be accompanied by any further. I also call the unity of it the *transcendental unity of self-consciousness*, in order to designate the possibility of a priori cognition from it. For the manifold representations that are given in a certain intuition would not be altogether my representations if they did not all together belong to *one self-consciousness* — i.e., as my representations (although I am not conscious of them as such), they must necessarily conform to the condition under which alone they can stand together in a *general self-consciousness*, because otherwise they would not all belong to me. From this original combination much can be inferred. The most famous proposition of the Critique is now stated: *Das Ich denke muß alle meine Vorstellungen begleiten können* — the *I think* must be able to accompany all my representations. The principle of apperception. The argument: any representation that cannot be brought under the *I think* would be "nothing for me" — i.e., not part of my unified consciousness at all. *Reine Apperzeption* (pure apperception) is distinguished from empirical apperception (the inner sense of my contingent psychological states); it is *original* because it is the source of the unity of consciousness, not derived from anything more basic. The *transzendentale Einheit des Selbstbewußtseins* (transcendental unity of self-consciousness) is identified with this pure apperception. The argument's force: for the manifold given in intuition to be *my* representations, they must all be unifiable in one consciousness — they must satisfy the condition of belonging to a single *I think*.

For this thoroughgoing identity of the apperception of a manifold given in intuition contains a *synthesis* of representations, and is possible only through the consciousness of this synthesis. For the empirical consciousness that accompanies different representations is in itself dispersed and without reference to the identity of the subject. This reference therefore takes place not by my accompanying each representation with consciousness, but by my *adding* one to another and being conscious of the *synthesis* of them. Only therefore through my being able to combine a manifold of given representations *in one consciousness* is it possible that I represent to myself the *identity* of consciousness in these representations themselves — i.e., the *analytic unity of apperception* is only possible under the presupposition of some *synthetic unity*. The footnote: the analytic unity of consciousness attaches to all *common concepts* as such — e.g., if I think *red in general*, I represent thereby a property which (as a mark) can be encountered in something or combined with other representations; hence only by virtue of a previously thought possible synthetic unity can I represent the analytic. A representation that is to be thought as common to different things is regarded as belonging to those that have something different in themselves besides; hence it must be previously thought in synthetic unity with others (even if only possible representations) before I can think in it the analytic unity of consciousness that makes it a *conceptus communis*. And thus the *synthetic unity of apperception* is the highest point on which one must attach all use of the understanding — indeed the whole of logic, and after it transcendental philosophy. Indeed this faculty *is* the understanding itself. The argument is the central one of the entire Critique. The *I think* — the identity of consciousness across representations — requires that the representations be unifiable into one consciousness. This unification is *synthesis*. Therefore the analytic unity of apperception (the *Ich* in *Ich denke*, the same in all representations) presupposes a *synthetic unity* — the active unification of the manifold by the subject. The footnote argues structurally: any common concept (any general representation that applies to many objects) presupposes synthetic unity, because to think a representation as *common to many* is to relate it to multiple individuals — which requires the synthetic act of relating. The conclusion is sweeping: the *synthetische Einheit der Apperzeption* is the *höchste Punkt* (highest point) of all understanding-use; the *Vermögen* (faculty) of synthesis just *is* the understanding itself.

The thought *these representations given in intuition altogether belong to me*, accordingly, says as much as: *I unite them in one self-consciousness*, or at least can unite them in it; and although it is itself not yet the consciousness of the *synthesis* of the representations, it yet presupposes the possibility of the latter — i.e., only by my being able to comprehend the manifold of them in one consciousness do I call them altogether *my* representations. For otherwise I would have as multifariously colored, different a self as I have representations of which I am conscious. *Synthetic unity of the manifold of intuitions*, as given a priori, is therefore the ground of the *identity* of apperception itself, which precedes all my determinate thinking a priori. *Combination*, however, does not lie in the objects, and cannot be borrowed from them through perception and first taken up into the understanding by this means; rather, it is solely a *function of the understanding*, which is itself nothing more than the *faculty of combining a priori* and bringing the manifold of given representations *under the unity of apperception* — which principle is the highest in the whole of human cognition. The argument's culmination. *My* representations are mine because — and only because — I can unite them in one consciousness. This unity is achieved by combination (synthesis), which is an *Action of the understanding*, not a feature of the objects. The understanding is therefore the *Vermögen, a priori zu verbinden* — the faculty of combining a priori. Bringing the manifold under the unity of apperception is the *oberste Grundsatz im ganzen menschlichen Erkenntnis* — the supreme principle in all human cognition.

This principle of the necessary unity of apperception is itself indeed identical, hence an analytic proposition, but yet declares a synthesis of the manifold given in an intuition as necessary — without which that thoroughgoing identity of self-consciousness cannot be thought. For through the *I*, as simple representation, nothing manifold is given; in *intuition*, which is distinct from it, the manifold can only be given, and can be thought through *combination* in *one consciousness*. An understanding in which through self-consciousness the whole manifold would at the same time be given would *intuit*; ours can only think and must seek intuition in the senses. I am therefore conscious of the *identical self* in respect of the manifold of the representations given to me in an intuition, because I call them altogether my representations which make up one. But that is as much as to say that I am conscious a priori of a *necessary synthesis* of them, which is called the *originally synthetic unity of apperception*, under which all representations given to me stand — but under which they must also be brought through a *synthesis*. The careful methodological remark: the principle of apperception is *analytic* in form (the *I* of *I think* is necessarily identical with itself), but its application demands a *synthesis* — for a manifold to be brought under the *I*, it must be combined. This is the crucial step: the analytic principle of apperception, applied to a sensible manifold, demands a synthetic act. The footnote on intuitive understanding is significant: an *intuitiver Verstand* (a divine understanding whose self-consciousness produces its objects) would not need synthesis of a given manifold, because the manifold would be produced in the very act of self-consciousness. Our finite human understanding, however, *only thinks*, *not intuits*; the intuition must be given to it from the senses; and the manifold given in intuition must be brought under apperception through synthesis. This restriction to *finite* understanding marks the place of the human mind in Kant's architectonic.

##### **§ 17\. The Principle of the Synthetic Unity of Apperception Is the Supreme Principle of All Use of the Understanding**

The supreme principle of the possibility of all intuition in reference to *sensibility* was, according to the transcendental aesthetic: *that all the manifold of it stand under the formal conditions of space and time*. The supreme principle of the same in reference to the *understanding* is: *that all the manifold of intuition stand under conditions of the originally synthetic unity of apperception*. The footnote: space and time and all their parts are *intuitions*, hence individual representations with the manifold that they contain in themselves (see the transcendental aesthetic) — not therefore mere concepts, through which the same consciousness is contained in many representations, but rather many representations contained in one and the consciousness of which is encountered as composite, consequently the unity of consciousness as *synthetic* but yet original. This *singularity* of them is important in application (see §25). Under the first principle stand all manifold representations of intuition insofar as they are *given* to us; under the second insofar as they must be able to be *combined* in one consciousness — for without this nothing can be thought or cognized through them, because the given representations do not have the act of apperception, *I think*, in common, and would not be comprised together in one self-consciousness. The two highest principles of the Critique are now stated in parallel:

* *Sensibility-principle*: the manifold of intuition stands under space and time.  
* *Understanding-principle*: the manifold of intuition stands under the synthetic unity of apperception.

These correspond to the two parts of the Transcendental Doctrine of Elements: the Aesthetic established the first; the Analytic now establishes the second.

*Understanding*, generally speaking, is the faculty of *cognitions*. These consist in the *determinate reference of given representations to an object*. *Object*, however, is that in whose concept the manifold of a given intuition is *united*. Now, however, all unification of representations requires unity of consciousness in the synthesis of them. Consequently, the unity of consciousness is alone what makes up the reference of representations to an object, hence their *objective validity* — consequently their becoming *cognitions* — and on it therefore the very possibility of the understanding rests. The pivotal definition of *Gegenstand* (object): an object is that *in whose concept the manifold of a given intuition is united*. Objectivity is therefore not a property of an external thing given independently of our cognition; objectivity is the *unity of synthesis* of a manifold. An "object" is *that which holds the manifold together in one concept*. This is the essence of Kant's Copernican insight: objects are constituted as objects by the synthetic activity of understanding. Consequently, the unity of consciousness (the synthetic unity of apperception) is what *makes* representations refer to objects — hence what makes them genuinely *cognitions* (as opposed to merely subjective associations).

The first pure cognition of the understanding therefore — on which all its other use is grounded, and which is at the same time wholly independent of all conditions of sensible intuition — is the principle of the *originally synthetic unity of apperception*. So the mere form of outer sensible intuition, space, is yet no cognition at all; it gives only the manifold of intuition a priori for a possible cognition. But to cognize anything in space — e.g., a *line* — I must *draw* it, and hence synthetically bring about a determinate combination of the given manifold, so that the unity of this action is at the same time the unity of consciousness (in the concept of a line); and only thereby is an object (a determinate space) first cognized. The synthetic unity of consciousness is therefore an *objective condition of all cognition* — not merely one of which I myself have need in order to cognize an object, but one *under which every intuition must stand in order to become an object for me*, because in any other way and without this synthesis the manifold would not be unified in one consciousness. The line example is significant. To cognize a line is not merely to have a sensory manifold given; it is to *draw* (*ziehen*) the line — to actively synthesize the manifold by a unitary action of the understanding. The act of drawing unifies the manifold in *one* consciousness (the concept of *line*), and thereby constitutes *the line* as an object. The synthetic unity of apperception is therefore the *objektive Bedingung* (objective condition) of all cognition.

This latter proposition is, as said, itself analytic, although it makes synthetic unity into the condition of all thinking; for it says no more than that all my representations in any given intuition must stand under the condition under which alone I can reckon them, as *my* representations, to the identical self — and hence comprise them together, as combined synthetically in *one* apperception, through the general expression *I think*. But this principle is yet not a principle for every possible understanding in general, but only for that one through whose pure apperception in the representation *I am* nothing manifold is yet given. That understanding through whose self-consciousness the manifold of intuition would at the same time be given — an understanding through whose representation the objects of this representation would at the same time *exist* — would not need a special act of the synthesis of the manifold to the unity of consciousness, which the human understanding (which merely thinks and does not intuit) does need. But for the human understanding it is yet unavoidably the first principle, so that it cannot make for itself the slightest concept of another possible understanding — either of one that itself intuits, or of one possessing as ground a sensible intuition of a different kind from that in space and time. The methodological reservation: the supreme principle of apperception is the highest principle of the *human* understanding, not necessarily of every possible intelligence. An intellectual intuition (an *intuitiver Verstand*, a creator-understanding that produces its objects in the act of self-consciousness) would not need the synthesis from a given manifold to the unity. The principle is supreme *for us* — finite, sensibly-receiving thinkers. The remark on alternative sensibilities (not space-and-time-based) is striking: we cannot even *form a concept* of such an intelligence — its understanding would be incommensurable with ours.

##### **§ 18\. What the Objective Unity of Self-Consciousness Is**

The *transcendental unity of apperception* is that through which all the manifold given in an intuition is unified in a *concept of the object*. It is therefore called *objective*, and must be distinguished from the *subjective unity of consciousness* — which is a *determination of inner sense*, through which the said manifold of intuition is empirically given for such a combination. Whether I can be empirically conscious of the manifold *simultaneously* or *successively* depends on circumstances or empirical conditions. The empirical unity of consciousness, through the association of representations, therefore itself concerns an *appearance*, and is wholly contingent. By contrast, the pure form of intuition in time — merely as intuition in general, which contains a given manifold — stands under the original unity of consciousness, solely through the *necessary reference* of the manifold of intuition to the *One: I think*, hence through the pure synthesis of the understanding, which a priori underlies the empirical. That unity alone is objectively valid; the empirical unity of apperception — which we are not considering here, and which moreover is only derived from the former under given conditions *in concreto* — has only *subjective validity*. One person associates the representation of a certain word with one thing, another with another thing; and the unity of consciousness, in what is empirical, is not necessary and universally valid with respect to what is given. The distinction *objective unity / subjective unity* of consciousness is central. Objective unity \= the transcendental unity of apperception, valid for everyone and necessarily, the basis of objective cognition. Subjective unity \= empirical associations, contingent and varying among subjects (one person associates the word *home* with a building, another with a feeling). Only the objective unity gives objective cognition; the subjective unity gives only psychologically contingent associations.

##### **§ 19\. The Logical Form of All Judgments Consists in the Objective Unity of the Apperception of the Concepts Contained in Them**

I have never been able to be satisfied, Kant continues, by the explanation that the logicians give of a *judgment* in general: it is, as they say, the representation of a *relation* between two concepts. Without here quarreling with them about the defectiveness of this explanation — that it at any rate fits only *categorical* and not *hypothetical* and *disjunctive* judgments (which latter contain not a relation of concepts but rather of judgments themselves) — though much vexatious consequence has grown from this oversight of logic — I remark only that *what* this relation consists in is here not determined. The footnote on syllogistic figures: the cumbersome doctrine of the four syllogistic figures concerns only categorical syllogisms, and although it is nothing more than an art of *smuggling in* (through hidden immediate inferences within the premises of a pure syllogism) the appearance of more modes of inference than that of the first figure — it would have made no peculiar success had it not succeeded in bringing the *categorical* judgments, as those to which all others must be referred, into exclusive view: which, however, is false according to §9. The opening polemic against the customary logician's definition of judgment. The defect: it covers only categorical judgments, not hypothetical or disjunctive. Worse: it does not say what kind of *Verhältnis* (relation) is meant. Kant's reform of the definition is now offered.

If, however, I investigate the *reference* of given cognitions in every judgment more precisely, and distinguish them — as belonging to the understanding — from the relation according to laws of the *reproductive imagination* (which has only subjective validity), then I find that a judgment is nothing other than the manner *to bring given cognitions to the objective unity of apperception*. This is what the *relation-word* *is* in them is aiming at: to distinguish the *objective unity* of given representations from the subjective. For this designates the reference of them to the original apperception and the *necessary unity* of them — even if the judgment itself is empirical, hence contingent — e.g., *bodies are heavy*. By this I do not wish to say that these representations *belong necessarily to one another in empirical intuition*, but that they *belong to one another by virtue of the necessary unity of apperception* in the synthesis of intuitions — i.e., according to principles of the objective determination of all representations insofar as cognition can arise from them — which principles are all derived from the principle of the transcendental unity of apperception. Only thereby does there arise from this relation a *judgment* — i.e., a relation that is *objectively valid*, and is sufficiently distinguished from the relation of these same representations in which there would be merely subjective validity — e.g., according to laws of association. The reform: a judgment is the act of bringing given cognitions to the objective unity of apperception. The copula *is* (*ist*) is the linguistic mark of this objective unity — it marks that the representations are united *as belonging to one consciousness in the object*, not merely as associated psychologically. The example: *bodies are heavy*. The *is* signifies that body and heaviness are united by the necessary unity of apperception in their synthesis as objects of possible experience, not merely that I happen to associate them when I perceive a body. According to merely subjective laws of association, I could only say *when I carry a body, I feel a pressure of heaviness* — a report of my psychological state. The judgment *the body is heavy* asserts more: it asserts that body and heaviness are objectively united, valid for any subject. The objectivity is grounded in the necessary unity of apperception.

According to the latter, I could only say: *when I carry a body, I feel a pressure of heaviness*; but not: *it, the body, is heavy* — which is as much as to say that these two representations are united *in the object* (i.e., without distinction of the state of the subject) and not merely together in perception (however often it may be repeated). The crucial structural distinction: the subjective report (*when I carry a body, I feel a pressure*) refers only to my psychological state and the contingent succession of impressions. The objective judgment (*the body is heavy*) asserts a unification in the object itself — valid independently of any subject's particular state. This distinction is the touchstone of objectivity.

##### **§ 20\. All Sensible Intuitions Stand under the Categories, as Conditions under Which Alone the Manifold of Them Can Come Together in One Consciousness**

What is given as manifold in a sensible intuition belongs necessarily under the originally synthetic unity of apperception, because through this alone is the *unity* of intuition possible (§17). That action of the understanding, however, through which the manifold of given representations (whether they are intuitions or concepts) is brought under *one apperception in general*, is the logical *function of judgments* (§19). All manifold, therefore, insofar as it is given in *one* empirical intuition, is *determined* in respect of one of the logical functions of judgment — through which it is brought to one consciousness in general. But the *categories* are nothing other than these *functions of judgment* insofar as the manifold of a given intuition is determined in respect of them (§13 \[should read §14\]). Therefore the manifold in a given intuition also necessarily stands under categories. The first stage of the B-Deduction now concludes. The chain of reasoning:

* (§16) The manifold of intuition, to be *mine*, must be brought under the unity of apperception.  
* (§17) This unification is the act of the understanding (synthesis).  
* (§19) The unification through the understanding is just the logical function of judgment.  
* (§14) The categories are these logical functions of judgment as determining a manifold of intuition.  
* Therefore: the manifold of intuition necessarily stands under categories.

This is the *Beweis* (proof) of the objective validity of the categories — at least at this first stage. The categories are valid for any *given empirical intuition* because any such intuition, in order to be *one* intuition belonging to *me*, must be unified by the same logical functions that constitute the categories.

##### **§ 21\. Remark**

A *manifold* which is contained in an intuition which I call mine is *represented* through the synthesis of the understanding *as belonging to the necessary unity of self-consciousness*, and this occurs *through the category*. The footnote: the proof rests on the represented unity of intuition through which an object is given, which always includes a synthesis of the manifold given for an intuition, and already contains the reference of this latter to the unity of apperception. This therefore indicates that the empirical consciousness of a given manifold of *one* intuition stands just as much under a pure self-consciousness a priori as empirical intuition stands under a pure sensible intuition that likewise takes place a priori. — In the above proposition, therefore, the *beginning* of a deduction of the pure concepts of the understanding has been made, in which — since the categories arise independently of sensibility merely in the understanding — I must still abstract from the manner in which the manifold is given for an empirical intuition, in order to look only to the unity which the category by means of the understanding adds to the intuition. In what follows (§26) it will be shown — from the manner in which empirical intuition is given in sensibility — that the unity of it is no other than that which the category, according to the foregoing §20, prescribes to the manifold of a given intuition in general; and thereby — by the validity of it a priori being explained with respect to all objects of our senses — the aim of the deduction is first fully achieved. The methodological self-clarification: §20 has accomplished only the *first half* of the Deduction. It has shown that the categories apply to any manifold given in *some empirical intuition* taken as one and mine. But it has not yet shown that the categories apply to the *specific way* sensibility gives us the manifold (in space and time). This second half — bringing the categories to bear on the specifically human sensible intuition — is reserved for §26. The Deduction therefore has a *two-stage* structure: §20 establishes the validity of the categories for *any* given intuition unifiable in apperception; §26 will establish that *our* sensible intuitions (in space and time) are precisely such unifiable intuitions, so the categories apply universally to all objects of our experience.

But there is one point in the above proof from which I could not abstract: namely, that the manifold for the intuition must be *given* even before the synthesis of the understanding and independently of it — though how, remains here undetermined. For if I wished to think of an understanding which itself intuited (as perhaps a divine one, which would not represent given objects to itself, but through whose representation the objects themselves would at the same time be given or produced), then the categories would have no significance whatever with respect to such a cognition. They are only *rules for an understanding whose whole capacity consists in thinking* — i.e., in the action of bringing to the unity of apperception the synthesis of the manifold which has otherwise been given to it in intuition — and which therefore for itself cognizes nothing at all, but only combines and orders the *material* for cognition — the intuition — which must be given to it through the object. But of the peculiarity of our understanding — that only by means of the categories and only by precisely this kind and number of them it brings about the unity of apperception a priori — no further ground can be given,

just as little as a further ground can be given why we have precisely these and no other functions of judgment, or why time and space are the only forms of our possible intuition. The methodological self-restraint is significant. The Deduction establishes *that* the categories apply to all objects of our experience, but it does not establish *why* these particular categories rather than others — that is a *brute fact* about the peculiarity (*Eigentümlichkeit*) of our understanding. Similarly, why we have *space and time* as our forms of intuition, rather than some others, is not a question that admits an answer. The two structural givens — the table of judgment-functions and the forms of space and time — are the foundational data of our cognitive constitution; from them everything else can be derived, but they themselves cannot be derived from anything more basic. The remark parallels the qualification in the Aesthetic regarding the unknowability of why we have *these* forms of intuition.

##### **§ 22\. The Category Has No Other Use for the Cognition of Things than Its Application to Objects of Experience**

To *think* an object and to *cognize* an object is therefore not the same, Kant continues. Two pieces belong to cognition: first, the *concept* through which an object is thought in general (the category); and second, the *intuition* through which it is given. For if no corresponding intuition could be given to the concept at all, then it would be a thought according to form, but without any object — and through it no cognition of any thing whatever would be possible, because, so far as I would know, there would be nothing nor could there be anything to which my thought could be applied. The crucial distinction *denken / erkennen* — thinking / cognizing — is now made explicit. To think an object is to bring it under a concept. To cognize an object is to bring it under a concept *and* have it given in intuition. Without intuition, thinking is hollow — a *Gedanke der Form nach* (a thought according to form) without any *Gegenstand* (object). The distinction will be repeatedly important: in the Dialectic, when reason claims to cognize what it can only think (God, soul, world), the diagnostic is precisely that the thought lacks corresponding intuition.

Now, all intuition possible for us is sensible (Aesthetic); hence the thinking of an object in general through a pure concept of the understanding can become *cognition* for us only insofar as this concept is related to objects of the senses. *Sensible intuition* is either pure intuition (space and time) or empirical intuition of that which, in space and time, is *immediately* represented as actual through sensation. Through determination of the former we can acquire a priori cognitions of objects (in mathematics) — but only with respect to their form as appearances; whether there can be *things* that must be intuited in this form remains undetermined. Consequently, all mathematical concepts are not in themselves cognitions, except insofar as one presupposes that there are things that can be exhibited to us only in conformity with the form of that pure sensible intuition. *Things in space and time*, however, are given only insofar as they are *perceptions* (representations accompanied by sensation) — hence through empirical representation. The pure concepts of the understanding, then, even when they are applied to a priori intuitions (as in mathematics), provide cognition only insofar as these — and consequently also the concepts of the understanding by means of them — can be applied to *empirical intuitions*. Consequently, the categories provide us, by means of intuition, no cognition of things either, except only through their *possible application to empirical intuition* — i.e., they serve only for the possibility of *empirical cognition*. This is called *experience*. Consequently, the categories have no other use for the cognition of things than only insofar as these are assumed as objects of *possible experience*. The decisive thesis is now stated in the bluntest form. Categories provide cognition only of *objects of possible experience*. Pure mathematics gives a priori cognition only of the form of objects (in space and time); it does not by itself establish that any things satisfying these forms exist. Empirical cognition supplies the *material* (actual existing things) to which the categories apply. The categories' validity is therefore confined to the field of possible experience. Beyond that field — for things in themselves, supersensible objects — the categories cannot provide cognition, however much they may still permit *thinking*.

##### **§ 23**

The above proposition is of the greatest importance: for it determines just as much *the limits of the use of the pure concepts of the understanding* with respect to objects as the *transcendental aesthetic* determined the limits of the use of the pure form of our sensible intuition. Space and time count, as conditions of the possibility of how objects can be given to us, no further than for objects of the senses — hence only of experience. Beyond these limits they represent nothing at all; for they are only in the senses and have outside them no actuality. The pure concepts of the understanding are *free of this restriction*, and extend to objects of intuition in general, whether it be similar to ours or not — if only it is sensible and not intellectual. But this wider extension of the concepts beyond our sensible intuition helps us to nothing. For they are then empty concepts of objects of which we cannot at all judge — through these alone — whether they are even possible or not. They are mere thought-forms without objective reality, because we have no intuition at hand to which the synthetic unity of apperception, which they alone contain, can be applied — and they thus could determine an object. Only our sensible and empirical intuition can provide them with *sense and significance* (*Sinn und Bedeutung*). The structural symmetry between sensibility's limits and the categories' limits is now stated. Space and time pertain *only* to objects of the senses; they signify nothing beyond. The categories, by contrast, are not so confined in their *thinkability* — they can be thought of objects of any sensible intuition whatever (not just ours). But this wider extension is *empty*: without corresponding intuition, the categories yield no cognition. *Sinn und Bedeutung* — sense and significance — accrue to the categories only through their application to empirical intuition.

If one therefore assumes an *object of a non-sensible intuition* as given, then one can indeed represent it through all the predicates that already lie in the presupposition that nothing belonging to sensible intuition pertains to it: hence that it is not extended, or is not in space; that its duration is not time; that no alteration (sequence of determinations in time) is encountered in it; etc. But that is no proper cognition if I merely indicate *how the intuition of the object is not*, without being able to say *what is contained in it*; for then I have not at all represented the possibility of an object for my pure concept of the understanding, because I could give no intuition that would correspond to it, but could only say that ours does not hold for it. The chief point here, however, is that to such a *Something* not even a single category can be applied: e.g., the concept of a *substance* — i.e., of something that can exist as subject and never as mere predicate — of which I do not know whether there can be any thing that would correspond to this thought-determination, if empirical intuition did not give me the case of application. But more of this in what follows. The diagnostic of negative theology / negative metaphysics. To represent an object of non-sensible intuition as *not extended*, *not in space*, *not in time*, *not subject to alteration* — these are merely *negative determinations*. They tell us how the object is *not*; they tell us nothing of *what it is*. Moreover, even apparently positive concepts (e.g., substance) cannot be applied to such an object: the concept of substance is the concept of *something that is always subject, never predicate*, and without empirical intuition we have no case in which to identify such a *something*. Thus even our positive metaphysical concepts collapse without the support of empirical intuition. The promise *more of this in what follows* will be fulfilled in the chapter on Phenomena and Noumena.

##### **§ 24\. On the Application of the Categories to Objects of the Senses in General**

The pure concepts of the understanding, Kant continues, relate through the mere understanding to objects of intuition in general — undetermined as to whether this is ours or any other, though sensible — but are for that very reason mere thought-forms through which no determinate object is yet cognized. The synthesis or combination of the manifold in them related merely to the unity of apperception, and was thereby the ground of the possibility of cognition a priori insofar as this rests on the understanding; hence it was not only *transcendental* but also *merely intellectual*. But since there lies in us a certain form of *sensible intuition a priori* — which rests on the receptivity of the faculty of representation (sensibility) — the understanding, as spontaneity, can determine inner sense through the manifold of given representations in conformity with the synthetic unity of apperception, and so think the *synthetic unity of the apperception of the manifold of sensible intuition a priori* as the condition under which all objects of our (the human) intuition must necessarily stand. Through this, then, the *categories* — as mere thought-forms — acquire *objective reality* — i.e., application to objects that can be given to us in intuition — but only as appearances; for only of these are we capable of a priori intuition. The crucial transition of the second half of the Deduction is now made. The first half (§§15–20) showed that the categories apply to any sensible manifold *insofar as it is to be unified in apperception*; but the categories there functioned as *bloße Gedankenformen* (mere thought-forms), without yet acquiring application to *our specific* sensibility. Now the second half undertakes to show that the categories acquire *objektive Realität* — objective reality — by being applied to the manifold given in our specifically human form of sensibility (i.e., space and time). The understanding, qua spontaneity, *determines* inner sense in conformity with apperception's unity; this *determining* is what gives the categories their grip on our specific sensible manifold.

This *synthesis of the manifold of sensible intuition*, which is a priori possible and necessary, can be called *figurative* (*synthesis speciosa*) to distinguish it from that which would be thought in mere category with respect to the manifold of an intuition in general — and is called *understanding-combination* (*synthesis intellectualis*). Both are *transcendental*, not only because they themselves proceed a priori but also because they ground the possibility of other a priori cognition. But the figurative synthesis, if it goes only to the originally synthetic unity of apperception — i.e., to this transcendental unity which is thought in the categories — must, to distinguish it from the merely intellectual combination, be called the *transcendental synthesis of the imagination* (*transzendentale Synthesis der Einbildungskraft*). The crucial distinction *synthesis speciosa / synthesis intellectualis* is now made. *Synthesis intellectualis* is synthesis as performed merely by the understanding considered apart from sensibility — the categories as bare thought-forms unifying *any* intuition whatever. *Synthesis speciosa* — figurative synthesis — is synthesis as performed in *our* sensibility, where the unity-of-apperception condition is brought to bear on the manifold given specifically in space and time. The figurative synthesis is the *transzendentale Synthesis der Einbildungskraft* — the transcendental synthesis of the imagination. Imagination is here introduced as the faculty that mediates between understanding and sensibility.

*Imagination* (*Einbildungskraft*) is the faculty of representing an object also without its presence in intuition. Since now all our intuition is sensible, imagination, on account of the subjective condition under which alone it can give a corresponding intuition to the concepts of the understanding, belongs to *sensibility*. But insofar as its synthesis is an exercise of spontaneity — which is *determining*, not, like sense, merely *determinable*, and can therefore a priori determine sense according to its form in accordance with the unity of apperception — imagination is in this respect a faculty of *determining sensibility a priori*; and its synthesis of intuitions, in accordance with the categories, must be the *transcendental synthesis of the imagination*. This is an *effect of the understanding on sensibility* and is the first application of the understanding (and at the same time the ground of all others) to objects of intuition possible for us. As figurative, it is distinguished from the *intellectual synthesis* — which is without any imagination, merely through the understanding. Insofar as imagination is now spontaneity, I also sometimes call it *productive* imagination (*produktive Einbildungskraft*), and distinguish it thereby from the *reproductive* (*reproduktive*) imagination, whose synthesis is subject solely to empirical laws — namely those of association — and which therefore contributes nothing to the explanation of the possibility of a priori cognition, and for that reason belongs not to transcendental philosophy but to psychology. The structural place of *Einbildungskraft* in the architectonic is now precisely defined. Imagination is at once sensible (it must give a *corresponding intuition* to concepts) and spontaneous (its synthesis is an active determining, not merely a passive being-determined). The *transcendental synthesis of the imagination* is the *Wirkung des Verstandes auf die Sinnlichkeit* — the effect of the understanding upon sensibility — and is the *first application* of the understanding to objects of intuition. The distinction *produktive / reproduktive* imagination is crucial. Productive imagination \= the a priori, transcendental, spontaneous synthesis that grounds the possibility of experience. Reproductive imagination \= empirical, psychological, association-governed — irrelevant for the transcendental project. This distinction will be central to the later interpretation of Kant (Heidegger's *Kant and the Problem of Metaphysics* will famously fasten on productive imagination as the "common root" of sensibility and understanding hinted at in §VII of the Introduction).

Here is now the place to make intelligible the *paradox* that must have struck everyone in the exposition of the form of *inner sense* (§6): namely, how inner sense even presents *us ourselves* to consciousness only as we *appear to ourselves*, not as we are in ourselves — because we *intuit* ourselves only as we are inwardly *affected* (*affiziert*), which seems to be contradictory, since we would have to relate to ourselves as *passive* (*als leidend*); for which reason one is also accustomed in the systems of psychology to pass off inner sense and the faculty of *apperception* (which we carefully distinguish) as one and the same. The puzzle of self-affection is now reopened, building on the proleptic remarks of §8 (in the General Remarks to the Aesthetic). The paradox: in inner sense we intuit ourselves; intuition requires being affected; therefore we are passive (*als leidend*) with respect to ourselves. This seems incoherent — how can we be both the affecting agent and the affected patient? The conventional solution (psychology) is to identify inner sense with apperception, eliminating the paradox by collapsing the distinction. Kant rejects this: inner sense and apperception are sharply distinct.

What *determines* inner sense is the understanding and its original faculty of *combining* the manifold of intuition — i.e., of bringing it under an *apperception* (on which its very possibility rests). Now since the understanding in us human beings is not itself a faculty of intuitions, and cannot take this — even if it were given in sensibility — *into itself* in order to combine, as it were, the manifold of *its own* intuition, its synthesis, when it is considered for itself alone, is nothing other than the *unity of the action* of which it is conscious as such, even without sensibility — but through which it is yet able to determine sensibility *inwardly* in respect to the manifold which may be given to it according to the form of its intuition. The understanding therefore exercises — under the designation of a *transcendental synthesis of the imagination* — that action upon the *passive subject*, whose *faculty* it is, of which we rightly say that *inner sense is thereby affected*. Apperception and its synthetic unity is so little one and the same with inner sense that the former, rather, as the source of all combination, goes to the manifold of *intuitions in general* under the name of the *categories* before all sensible intuition to objects in general; whereas inner sense, by contrast, contains the mere form of intuition, but without combination of the manifold in it, hence still contains no determinate intuition at all — which is possible only through consciousness of the determination of it through the transcendental action of the imagination (synthetic influence of the understanding on inner sense), which I have called *figurative synthesis*. The resolution of the paradox is now precise. The understanding is *spontaneity*; it is not itself an intuitor (which would require receptivity). When the understanding acts to combine the manifold of intuition, this action takes place *upon* the passive subject (the receptive sensibility) — which we therefore correctly describe as *affected*. The act of *Selbstaffektion* (self-affection) is the action of the spontaneous understanding upon the receptive sensibility *within the same subject*. Apperception (the original unity) is the source of this combining act; inner sense is the receptive faculty within which the manifold is given and within which the synthesis takes place. They are distinct: apperception is the active source of the unity of consciousness; inner sense is the receptive form of intuition within which my representations are temporally ordered. The paradox is resolved by recognizing that the human subject contains *both* a spontaneous and a receptive aspect, and that the spontaneous aspect can determine the receptive aspect — yielding self-affection. (This resolution will be central to all later phenomenological appropriations of Kant — Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty — for whom self-affection is the basic structure of finite consciousness.)
